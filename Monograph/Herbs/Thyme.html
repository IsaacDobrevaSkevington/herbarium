Thyme

Native To

France, Italy, Spain (Royal Botanic Gardens of Kew [RBGK], n.d.).

Geographic Distribution

Thyme is widely cultivated all over the world in areas with temperate climates. Its current distribution includes countries close to its native Mediterranean range, including Belgium, Czechia, Switzerland, and Algeria, as well as New Zealand and some Carribean islands (Puerto Rico, Leeward Islands, Dominican Republic, Cuba, and Haiti) (RBGK, n.d.). Thyme has also become naturalized in some parts of the northeastern United States (United States Department of Agriculture, n.d.).

Botanical Description

Thyme is a low-growing shrub-like perennial with woody stalks and fibrous roots. It grows anywhere from 2-15 inches tall depending on variety. It grows in dense mats and has a strong and pleasant aroma. Thyme has a creeping stem with tiny, oblong, egg-shaped leaves that are approximately ¼- ½ inch in length and greenish grey in color. Small, pretty flowers arrive during summer in dense, terminal clusters. The flowers can range from pale pink, magenta, mauve, white, and blue-violet with the most common being a lilac color. The calyx is tubular and closed at the mouth with small hairs (Foster & Duke, 2014; Newcomb, 1977).

Key Constituents

Volatile oils (thymol, carvacrol), tannins, flavonoids (apigenin, luteolin, thymonin, naringenin), labiatic acid, caffeic acid (Hoffmann, 2003), saponins, resins (Pursell, 2015).

Sustainability

There are no known sustainability issues.

Harvesting Guidelines

Harvesting can be done by cutting the thyme stems at any time during the summer months (Vincent, 2011), preferably before blooming. To dry, hang the sprigs in a dark, well-ventilated area or place them on a tray to dry (The Old Farmer’s Almanac, n.d.).

Uses

Thyme is the common name for 300-400 different species that are currently cultivated around the world, the most common variety being Thymus vulgaris. A part of the Lamiaceae (mint) family, thyme is also a close relative of Origanum, the oregano genus. The name Thymus can be traced back to the botanical writings of Theophrastus, the Greek philosopher and naturalist of the 3rd century BCE (Bettler, 1997). Thymus comes from the Greek word θυμός (thymós), which historically held a variety of meanings, among them spirit, strength, courage, and anger or other passions (Migliore, 2019).

The theme of courage with respect to thyme is sustained throughout history. Roman soldiers were known to exchange sprigs of thyme as a sign of respect (Dunn, 2013). Centuries later, ladies of the Middle Ages would often embroider a bee hovering about a spray of thyme on the scarves they presented to their knights (Grieve, 1931). Thyme is, in fact, much beloved by bees, and thyme was used in ancient augury for determining the quality of the year’s honey (Pliny, 77/1906). In Scotland, the mere scent of wild thyme was said to imbue one with courage and strength (Mac Coitir, 2016).

Scholars also believe the word thymós could have a common semantic root with the Latin word fumos, meaning smoke (Migliore, 2019). Indeed, thyme does have a history of being burned for various reasons. The ancient Greeks and Romans lit bundles of thyme not only to evoke courage but also as a fumigator to purify their homes and temples (Dunn, 2013). Burning wild thyme in particular was considered useful in warding off creatures of a venomous nature (Pliny, 1906). According to The Old Farmer’s Almanac (n.d.), it is thought that burning thyme gets rid of insects in the home. Modern Pagans burn thyme to purify the space used for a magical ritual, while wild thyme (Thymus serpyllum) was considered sacred to Freyja (Freya), a Norse pagan goddess (Müller-Ebeling et al., 1998).

Throughout history, thyme has upheld its reputation as a protector. During the Roman era, for example, it was widely believed that eating thyme either before or during a meal would protect you from poison, making it popular amongst emperors (Dunn, 2013). If poison was consumed, a warm bath filled with thyme was used to help stop the poisonous effects (Dunn, 2013). It was also considered an antidote to venoms, particularly those of snakes and sea creatures (Pliny, 77/1906).

In Europe during the Middle Ages, thyme was also tucked under pillows to ward against nightmares and promote sleep (White Rabbit Institute of Healing [WRIH], n.d.). Thyme was also a primary ingredient in ritual altar fires and played a key role in rites of passage; in ancient Greece, it was placed inside the coffins of the dead to assure their passage into the afterlife (Bettler, 1997). It was believed that the soul of the deceased took up residence in the flowers of the thyme plant (Bettler, 1997).

The Victorians fancied the more mystical side of thyme and believed that a patch of wild thyme in the woods was a sure sign that fairies had been dancing, and perhaps living, in that very spot. Matted patches of thyme can easily hide small fairy houses to shelter the night workers of the garden who spend their time washing leaves, herding insects, painting flowers, and tidying up the plants (Bettler, 1997). In Shakespeare’s A Midsummer Night’s Dream (1600/1908), Titania the Fairy Queen’s bower is described as “a bank where the wild thyme blows” (2.1.249).

A traditional song dating back to 17th century (maybe further), which later became a well-known hit for the musical duo Foster and Allen in the 1980’s, uses thyme as a reference to a woman’s maidenhood:

Come all you maidens young and fair
All you who are blooming in your prime
Always beware and keep your garden fair
And let no man steal away your thyme

In the United Kingdom, although thyme became a general symbol of love and held other positive associations, the gypsies shunned the herb (Mac Coitir, 2016).

Aside from its folklore and history, thyme is a significant and vibrant herb in herbal practice. Thyme possesses a range of applications in Western and Eastern herbal traditions that have either been sustained to the present day or which are now being investigated and largely supported by science. Thyme was and is most frequently connected to respiratory and digestive systems. This is seen in Chinese medicine, wherein the meridians for thyme are Lungs, Spleen, and Stomach (WRIH, n.d.). Besides its prominent association with these two major body systems, thyme is well known as a protector against infections. It is also worth exploring thyme’s historical herbal roles as a powerful aid to the nervous system, as an ally for menstrual and uterine health, as well as a few more minor traditional uses that are re-emerging from long-ago historical shadows.

Thyme has long been favored by herbalists as a go-to herb for breathing difficulties and for any kind of imbalance that encroaches upon the respiratory system. Thyme acts as a bronchodilator and anti-inflammatory agent. Used to address all types of asthma (Holmes, 2007), the plant was noted in the first century CE by Roman polymath Pliny the Elder (77/1906) as being used for asthma and shortness of breath. These indications are found repeated here and there up through 17th-century herbal writings (Parkinson, 1640) and beyond.

The primary chemical constituent of thyme, thymol, is expectorant, antispasmodic, and antimicrobial; this volatile oil plays a key role with respect to the lungs. Although recent clinical trials looking at thyme and asthma have not been entirely successful, perhaps due to blindspots in their structuring (Bone & Mills, 2013), scientific research is increasingly underway in this direction and is beginning to confirm longstanding traditional practice ( Šmejkal & Rjašková, 2016). Recent studies have shown thyme’s potential use for asthma through its ability to decrease inflammatory responses, particularly with allergic varieties of the condition (Zhou et al., 2014).

In Chinese medicine, thyme is also intimately connected to the Lung meridian, where it is used to warm the lungs in instances of white phlegm, chills, fever, asthma, and tuberculosis, as well as for immune system support in the presence of colds (WRIH, n.d.). In addition, the herb has an opening influence upon the sinuses, where its warming and drying nature helps to clear congestion. It is often used as a tea, gargle, or with honey to expel mucus. Clinical trials have supported the use of thyme in combination for acute bronchitis and as a single herb for promoting a productive cough (Bone & Mills, 2013).

Thyme has had a strong role in the kitchen since antiquity, where it was employed as a culinary addition as well as an appetite stimulant (Pliny, 77/1906). The same goes for its application with respect to digestive issues that include stagnant digestion, flatulence, and heartburn. In the first century CE, Pliny (77/1906) listed thyme as specific to intestinal diseases. The aromatic and hot nature of the herb helps to bring warmth to cold intestines, improving appetite. Thyme also serves to generally combat dysbiosis; by stimulating movement of energy, blood, and oxygen to the digestive tract, it helps dispel gas, bloating, and digestive upset (McBride, 2010). This is connected to its key indications in Chinese medicine for Spleen support in cases of wet diarrhea, colic, nausea, and cramping (WRIH, n.d.).

Despite its ancient reputation as a digestive herb par excellence, thyme has not received much scientific study for use in digestive conditions until recently, and this has been largely in animal studies only. One study showed the powerful essential oil to have potential in strengthening the integrity of the intestines as well as increasing beneficial gut microbes (Placha et al., 2013). Meanwhile, pharmacological studies have suggested its application for ulcers and spasmodic conditions of the gastrointestinal tract (Bone & Mills, 2013). Although thyme’s antispasmodic action appears strongest with respect to the lungs, thyme’s flavonoids in particular have shown its pertinence to the smooth muscle of the ileum (Bone & Mills, 2013).

Thyme has also received many of its historical and contemporary accolades due to its marked ability in warding off various dangers. This applies to infections; its antimicrobial and antifungal properties make it key in protecting the body. This even includes a possible potency against anthrax (Wood, 2008). Sumerians apparently used thyme as an antiseptic as far back as 3000 BCE (WRIH,  n.d.). During the Middle Ages, thyme was worn on clothes to prevent the plague, and in the 18th century, a German apothecary discovered the plant’s essential oil was effective against bacteria and fungi (WRIH, n.d.). No other aspect of thyme has been so highly studied by science as these protective antimicrobial actions have been.

Thyme is also employed as an immunostimulant to help generally reduce infection. In Chinese medicine, this immunity support is found in the presence of colds, skin infections, and parasites (WRIH, n.d.). Thymol, the aforementioned key constituent in the herb’s chemical profile, is also anthelmintic. Thus, thyme is also used in backyard farming as a health-promoting “treat” for chickens to keep them healthy, clear of worms and fungal infections.

Thyme does have a track record of being used externally, although this application brings with it some caution; one of the only major safety issues with respect to thyme is skin safety. German Benedictine abbess and herbalist Hildegard von Bingen (1155/2001) noted that if thyme was used alone—without other herbs or carriers—that the strength of it could perforate ulcers and prove useless in resolving them. Thyme’s external use is also related to its anti-infectious actions. In the 19th century, it was used as an antiseptic on bandages and to disinfect hospitals.

Thymol plays a role in thyme’s contemporary use in disinfecting products such as hand sanitizer, acne medication, and mouthwash (Dunn, 2013). The herb was also historically used for leprosy (von Bingen, 1155/2001) and shingles (Mabey, 1988). The irritating aspect of the volatile oils on skin may account for that fact that thyme oil was used since antiquity as a rubefacient and counter-irritant in rheumatism and neuralgic pain (Pliny, 77/1906; Grieve, 1931; Felter & Lloyd, 1898).

Thyme has also historically been valued as a nervous system restorative, particularly when there is chronic fatigue or melancholy with recurrent infections in the picture. The herb may also aid in bringing the body back into balance in two opposite directions; thyme can be stimulating at the same time that it may be relaxing (Tierra, 1988). We can trace this indication for the nerves at least as far back as ancient Rome, where thyme was taken as an oxymel for loss of reason, delirium, and melancholy (Pliny, 77/1906). A deeper look at its ancient Greek derivations, discussed above, are interesting here; thymós not only denoted courage but also could refer directly to the heart (and also the mind) as the touchstone of emotions, particularly joy, grief, and anger (Migliore, 2019).

Thyme was historically noted for mood imbalances; for example, it is found in 17th-century herbal writings for frenzy or madness (Parkinson, 1640), a reference that goes directly back to ancient Rome (Pliny, 77/1906). In the late 19th century, thyme appears in Eclectic writings as an indication for hysteria (Felter & Lloyd, 1898). In European folk tradition, we also find thyme leaf tea being used for nervous disorders (Foster, 2014). In fact, one of thyme’s herbal actions—thymoleptic—is etymologically connected (literally “lifting the spirits”); it addresses low mood by stimulating a depressive state and the central nervous system to bring the person back into balance. In recent scientific research (animal studies only to date), thymol demonstrated a promising potential antidepressive impact (Deng et al., 2015). It is conjectured that this effect works by increasing dopamine, and thyme has also been considered anxiolytic in mice studies (Bone & Mills, 2013).

Thyme, which stimulates the thymus gland in the body, is furthermore viewed as a relaxant specific to the parasympathetic system, hence its use for disturbed sleep with low-level anxiety or nightmares, and for stimulating the adrenal cortex (Wood, 2008). During the Middle Ages, thyme was tucked under pillows to ward against nightmares and promote sleep (WRIH, n.d.). The specific indication for nightmares is also found in herbals of the early 19th century (Hill, 1812). Herbalist Michael Tierra, known for integrating “Western” herbs into traditional Chinese and ayurvedic systems, suggests taking thyme before going to sleep when nightmares are an issue (Tierra, 1988). In Chinese medicine, thyme is considered a qi tonic that works to bring the nervous system and the adrenals into balance while enhancing immunity (Holmes, 2006). It is of adjacent interest that thyme has been historically remarked upon as useful for other nerve-related issues such as sciatica, epilepsy (Pliny, 77/1906), and vertigo (Parkinson, 1640).

Not many people, perhaps, immediately think of thyme with respect to menstrual and uterine health. When we again trace extant records all the way back to the first century CE, we find thyme advised for bringing on blocked menstruation (Pliny, 77/1906). This is repeated centuries later in John Parkinson’s (1640) Theatrum Botanicum, where it is recorded that thyme promotes menstruation. The Physiomedical Dispensatory also echoes a use for painful and blocked menstruation (Cook, 1869). Thyme is indeed an emmenagogue where there is, for example, amenorrhea or leucorrhea. Although not native to North America, thyme was eventually incorporated into Cherokee herbal traditions and has been used for painful menstruation (Garrett, 2003).

Herbalists also use thyme to stop discharge by virtue of its uterine-stimulating action (Holmes, 2006). It was also long ago traditionally used to expel the placenta and viewed as helpful during labor (Parkinson, 1640). In ancient Rome, thyme was used to dispel a stillborn fetus (Pliny, 77/1906). Thyme also has high iron content, and drinking an iron-rich tea can provide support during menstruation, menopausal changes, and after pregnancy (McBride, 2010).

As an aside, five other more minor aspects of thyme (and others exist in addition to these) may be noted, if only as an interesting illustration of science coming to confirm longstanding herbal applications of thyme:

  • An anticarcinogenic aspect of thyme is increasingly revealing itself in recent years. In 1995, its extracted flavonoid luteolin was found effective against carcinogens created from cooking in vitro (Bone & Mills, 2013). Thyme oil in particular has a high anti-radical impact (Bone & Mills, 2013). In antiquity, thyme was applied externally (in wine) to remove tumors (Pliny, 77/1906) and was remarked upon as dissolving swellings and tumors in the 17th century (Parkinson, 1640).
  • A 2005 study reported that thyme inhibited platelet activity both in vivo and in vitro (Bone & Mills, 2013). Thyme was used in ancient times, ingested with honey, to prevent the blood from clotting (Pliny, 77/1906).
  • A 2003 study on the burn injuries of rats revealed that the application of thyme oil appeared to promote new tissue growth (Bone & Mills, 2013). Related to this is a 2017 study that investigated thyme honey in open skin wounds and affirmed significant acceleration of healing (Takzaree et al., 2017). This echoes a use in ancient Rome, where thyme was mixed with pig lard as a carrier and used topically for burns (Pliny, 77/1906).
  • Two studies on rats in 2017 suggest that thyme extracts and leaves could be hepatoprotective as well as hypolipidemic (El-Newary et al, 2017; Hegazy, 2017). In ancient Rome, thyme was used with respect to the liver (Pliny, 1906). Herbalist Peter Holmes has classified thyme as a liver decongestant, particularly with qi stagnantion (Holmes, 2006).
  • In 2015, a scientific overview of herbs and ocular health in the Persian tradition mentions thyme as benefitting the eyes (Namdar et al., 2015). Another study on ocular pathology suggested that thyme essential oil has a strong antimicrobial affinity to eye health and looked at in vitro cytotoxicity with respect to human conjunctiva cells (Cannas et al., 2015). Indeed, we find this indication of thyme for the eyes as far back as the first century CE (Pliny, 77/1906). An unusually fanciful passage in Hildegard von Bingen’s (1098-1179) writing on herbs takes the healing strength of thyme to a peculiar energetic length: “If blood or water are held beyond measure in a person’s eyes because of old age or some other illness, let them stare at green thyme for a long time until the eyes are damp, as if from crying. This makes the person’s eyes pure and clear because the greenness of that herb carries away the storminess in the eyes” (p. 181).

In terms of preparation, thyme contains powerful volatile oils that are key in its herbal efficacy, so fresh plant preparations are considered best. If drying, the herb needs to be handled and stored carefully in order to retain the oils. Infusions of either fresh or dried herb should be covered to retain the aromatic oils.

Thyme is also a great food additive because, as a strong antibacterial and antioxidant plant, thyme keeps bacteria from growing in food, preserves meat, and keeps oils from going rancid (McBride, 2010). Thus ancient Egyptians used thyme for preservation, both for foods and for embalming (WRIH, n.d.). Monasteries, known as the keepers of herbal knowledge and kitchen gardening wisdom, historically utilized thyme in their breads, soups, and roasts (Müller-Ebeling et al., 1998). During a time before refrigeration and food safety laws, adding thyme to recipes provided some protection against spoiled meat and foodborne disease (Müller-Ebeling et al., 1998). Outside of the kitchen, the dried thyme flower has been used in a fashion similar to lavender (Lavandula spp.) in preserving cloth from the ravages of insects (Bone & Mills, 2013).

An unusually rich history along with an increase in scientific and pharmacological studies—despite a remarkable number of its ancient uses having been carried over to the present day—still leaves much to be explored. What emerges from just a glance at its profile is that thyme is indeed well associated with the concept of strength; this herb possesses undeniable protective and mobilizing powers as well as an unexpected breadth of application.

Adult Dose

Infusion: 8 fl oz (1 tsp dried aerial parts in 1 cup boiling water) 1-3x/day (Pursell, 2015)

Tincture: 2-4 mL (1:5, 45%) 3x/day (Hoffmann, 2003).

Fluid Extract: 2-6 mL (1:2) per day (Mills & Bone, 2013).

Safety

Thyme is generally considered safe to use and has no known side effects (Hoffmann, 2003). There have been some reports of allergic reactions, including cross-sensitivity with rosemary (Salvia rosmarinus) and oregano (Origanum vulgare) (Gardner & McGuffin, 2013). Thyme’s essential oil can cause skin and mucous membrane irritation, as well as possible allergic reactions including headache, vomiting, and diarrhea. Since thyme has been linked with uterine stimulation, it should not be used during pregnancy (Gardner & McGuffin, 2013).

Researchers using a related species, Thymus serpyllum, observed an anti-thyrotropic effect in animals, leading the authors to state that, in theory, thyme may decrease thyroid hormone levels (Basch et al., 2004). However, this effect has not been studied or observed in humans.
