Native To

Calendula’s primary origin is uncertain but has been suggested as North Africa (Barker, 2001), Southern Europe, and/or the Mediterranean (University of Wisconsin, n.d.).

Geographic Distribution

Calendula is widely cultivated and has naturalized in temperate climates throughout the world (University of Wisconsin, n.d.).

Botanical Description

Calendula is a short-lived perennial that is often grown as an annual (University of Wisconsin, n.d.). The plant has a sticky texture due to its resin content (Blakespoor, 2012). Calendula reaches up to 24 inches in height, its stalk supporting many branching stems and oblong leaves that are 3-6 inches long (Foster, 1993). The orange or yellow flower heads are 1-3 inches across and have a central cluster of tubular flowers surrounded by several rows of ray florets (Foster, 1993). The blooms give way to crescent-shaped seeds that are light brown or gray in color (University of Wisconsin, n.d.). Calendula cultivars include both single (one row of petals) and double (two rows of petals) varieties.

Note that C. arvensis is sometimes used similarly to C. officinalis in herbal practice and also in scientific research. This species’ flower, which tends to be both smaller and lighter in color, was noted as being used by practitioners at least as far back as the late 19th century in the United States (Felter & Lloyd, 1898).

Key Constituents

Flavonoids (including rutin and narcissin), triterpenoid saponins (calendulosides A-D), volatile oil (Hoffmann, 2003), carotenoids, phenolic acids (e.g., chlorogenic acid and coumaric acid), resin, sterols, tannin, amino acids, mucilage (Arora et al., 2013), and polysaccharides (Muley et al., 2009).

Sustainability

No known sustainability issues.

Harvesting Guidelines

Calendula may be either annual or perennial, depending on growing conditions and climate factors. Calendula blooms nearly continuously from spring through fall; picking the blossoms every 2-3 days helps to encourage additional blooms (Blakespoor, 2012). Harvest flower heads (in full flower) in the afternoon after the dew has evaporated and dry in a warm, well-ventilated area (Blakespoor, 2012).

While the ray florets (which look like flower petals but are actually tiny flowers characteristic of the Asteraceae family) are sometimes removed and sold separately in commerce, herbalist Juliet Blakespoor (2012) and other herbalists (Barker, 2001; Popham, n.d.) suggest using the whole flower head in herbal preparations for a greater concentration of active constituents.

Uses

Calendula has sunny yellow and orange flowers that open in the morning when the sun rises and close as it sets, inspiring Shakespeare (1623/2017) to famously describe it in The Winter’s Tale:

    The marigold, that goes to bed wi’ the sun

And with him rises weeping (4.4.125-126).

This association was also noted by 17th-century herbalist Nicholas Culpeper (1653/1850), who called calendula “an herb of the sun” (p. 114).

The name calendula is a diminutive Latin form of calends (or kalends), meaning “the first day of each month” in the ancient Roman calendar, presumably because the flower was noted as blooming at that time or throughout each month; today in Italy the plant is commonly known as fiore d’ogni mese (Grieve, 1931/1971) and in France as fleur de tous les mois, meaning “flower of every month.” It should be noted that while many herbal books confidently cite the appearance of calendula and its indications as going back to ancient Greek and Roman sources such as Dioscorides, Theophrastus, or Pliny, there has long been an inconclusive scholarly debate as to whether or not the flower these ancients wrote of—often known by the Latin name caltha—was the same as calendula (Matthiole, 1572; Bostock & Riley, 1855), so we omit these early sources here.

Regardless of caltha’s identity, calendula generally does not appear to have a strong herbal reputation in antiquity; indications of calendula’s uses are not definitively found—whether because the texts did not survive, through confusion with other plants and plant names, or by the fact that it simply wasn’t a notable herb in such early periods of history. The blossoms, however, are recognized in surviving artistic representations such as relief sculpture from ancient Greece and Rome (Caneva et al., 2019).

Calendula is also called marigold or pot marigold, and it has often been said that this prefix of mari– is due to the plant being named after the Virgin Mary in the Middle Ages (McIntyre, 1996). However, herbal historian Maud Grieve (1931/1971) reported that the name is actually a corruption of the Anglo-Saxon term for marsh marigold and that calendula was only later associated with the Virgin Mary or Queen Mary in the 17th century.

It should also be noted that calendula is not the same as marigolds in the genus Tagetes, which are common garden ornamentals hailing from the Americas. Tagetes marigolds (also known as African/French/Mexican Marigolds) are not interchangeable with Calendula officinalis (Blakespoor, 2012). Yet they are still very often confused with calendula, and many herbal fluid preparations towards the end of the 19th century may even have been made from Tagetes (Felter & Lloyd, 1898). While several Tagetes species, such as T. erecta, do have a long history of herbal use (Arvigo & Balick, 1998; Neher, 1968), there are also reports of human toxicity or phototoxicity among some members of this genus (Neher, 1968; North Carolina State University, n.d.); thus, it’s important not to confuse calendula with Tagetes spp.

Opinions vary as to calendula’s energetic profile. Some herbalists view calendula as warming (Wood, 2008) and as having an overall drying effect on the constitution; however, it also has a soothing demulcent effect on mucosal membranes and is used for dryness and irritation in those tissues (Popham, n.d.). Others suggest that the herb is neutral and possibly cooling (Holmes, 2006) or even cold (von Bingen, 1155/2001). While the flower head is the main part of the plant used in herbalism, some records show that the leaves were also employed (Barker, 2001; Felter & Lloyd, 1898).

Calendula does not seem to have been cultivated or at least written about until around the 12th century in Northwestern Europe (Barker, 2001). The herb is mentioned by German Benedictine abbess and herbalist Hildegard von Bingen (1155/2001), who deemed calendula to be cold and moist, and useful against poisons both in humans and livestock.

From the 12th to the 17th centuries, calendula also appears to possess a reputation as a mood enhancer, an indication for which the herb is not as well known today but may eventually be so again. Some editions of De Viribus Herbarum, an herbal poem by Macer Floridus (thought to be a pseudonym of the 12th-century French physician Odo de Meung), suggest calendula for cheerfulness, and propose that just gazing at the flower could draw away evil humors from the head (as cited in Rohde, 1922). In the early 17th century, herbalist and botanist John Parkinson (1629) recorded that calendula was of great use among pot herbs, used in food as well as in syrup or a conserve made from fresh flowers, as a “comforter of the heart and spirits, and to expel any malignant or pestilential quality, gathered near thereunto” (p. 298), but he does not say more than this. Contemporary herbalist Matthew Wood (2008) also records an indication for heavy depression. While the mechanism(s) are not clear, scientific research has very recently begun affirming some antidepressant effects in vivo, but these effects have yet to be confirmed in humans (Rajwinder et al., 2020; Sathisha et al., 2017).

An 18th-century Latin text (Hermann & Boecler, 1726) seems to echo indications found in De Viribus Herbarum, which mentioned clearing the head as well as helping eyesight (as cited in Rohde, 1922). Yet much later, in the late 19th and early 20th centuries, there is far greater interest and more surviving literature on the subject of the herb, but in new and different veins. While there is a relative newness of calendula in records of herbal practice and scientific study, it becomes clear that the herb is far more than the vulnerary or lymphatic for which it is often recognized.

Calendula is most well known, as it has been throughout history, for its use as a vulnerary, and is still largely used externally today. In the early 20th century, however, calendula was sometimes taken internally at the same time as the external preparations were applied for various illnesses, with the aim of reinforcing local action (Felter, 1922). Folk herbalists were already widely using calendula for wounds and skin issues when Samuel Hahnemann (1755-1843), the father of homeopathy, added the herb to his homeopathic preparations for topical infections, wounds, sores, and burns (Wood, 2008). Calendula also appears to have been used for wounds in both the American Civil War (Arora et al., 2013) and in World War I (Parente et al., 2012), particularly when other emergency medical supplies had run low. The few clinical trials that exist thus far on calendula tend to explore its vulnerary action; in a review of the available research, Givol et al. (2019) conclude that there is enough evidence for calendula’s traditional vulnerary use to warrant further clinical studies.

Calendula’s anti-inflammatory and astringent actions combined with its antibacterial, antiviral, and antifungal properties make it a common choice for a wide variety of skin issues (Hoffmann, 2003; Smith, 2008). It has long been used topically to soothe and mend cuts, burns, bites, sprains, bruises, rashes, sunburns, and abrasions, including persistent wounds and gangrene (Glenn, 2020; Hoffmann, 2003; Wood, 2008) and dermatological issues and infestations such as scabies (von Bingen, 1155/2001). Calendula is also used both internally and externally for fungal skin infections (e.g. athlete’s foot and jock itch) and yeast overgrowth (e.g., candida in the gut, thrush, and vaginal yeast infections) (Bennett, 2014). Furthermore, its astringent and blood-decongestant actions may arise from its saponins and flavonoids (rather than tannins) and accordingly, like horse chestnut (Aesculus hippocastanum), may be used for issues such as hemorrhoids, varicose veins, and some types of dysmenorrhea (Holmes, 2006).

Germany’s Commission E (the German equivalent of the Food and Drug Administration) has approved internal and topical use of calendula for inflammation of the oral and pharyngeal mucosa and external use for slow-healing wounds (American Botanical Council, 2000). A review of several clinical studies on the use of calendula for skin toxicity and irritation (e.g. redness, itchiness, burning, ulceration, necrosis) as a side effect of radiation therapy concluded that calendula was useful as a preventative agent to reduce the occurrence of skin toxicity (Stubbe & Valero, 2013). A clinical study on the use of calendula for treatment of diaper rash showed an improvement in severity of rash and attributed these effects to calendula’s anti-inflammatory and antimicrobial properties (Panahi et al., 2012) and it is a common ingredient in current natural diaper rash products.

Calendula’s anti-inflammatory action goes a bit deeper than its indication for infections as well as external skin and wound issues, however. The herb may also address inflammation related to bone and joint diseases: one very recent randomized trial involving 115 rheumatoid arthritis patients demonstrated that a topical preparation that featured lyophilized calendula reduced pain and had an anti-inflammatory local effect (Jurca et al., 2020). The fact that inflammatory responses are part of many cardiac diseases may also account for calendula’s traditional use in relation to the heart (although human studies have yet to be undertaken). In Chinese medicine, calendula is aligned not only with the Liver and Lung meridians, but also with the Heart (Holmes, 2006; Tierra, 1988), where it may tonify Heart qi (deficiency) with issues such as palpitations and wheezing, particularly in old age (Holmes, 2006). In the Western tradition, Culpeper (1653/1850) also remarked that calendula strengthened the heart.

Meanwhile, calendula’s antiviral activity—long acknowledged in herbal literature—has not yet been widely covered in scientific research, although flower extracts have been deemed promising for anti-HIV activity when studied in vitro (Kalvatchev et al., 1997). It appears that the herb’s antiviral activity may arise in part from its triterpenoid saponins (De Tommasi et al., 1991), and a Russian study from the 1970s indicated that a calendula flower tincture suppressed the replication of viruses such as influenza APR-8, influenza A2, and herpes simplex in vitro (Bogdanova et al., 1970, as cited in Muley et al., 2009). Herbalist Julian Barker (2001), meanwhile, remarked that he switched to using a lower concentration of 25% alcohol in tinctures for both enhanced antiviral results (because calendula’s water-soluble constituents contribute to its antiviral activity) as well as to ease any irritation to delicate stomachs.

Calendula is also commonly used as a lymphagogue and is known as a blood cleanser or alterative (Popham, n.d.). It cleanses the lymphatic system by moving the fluid, removing toxins from the lymph, and decongesting swollen lymph nodes (Popham, n.d.), as well as by improving the circulation of the blood (Ashwlayan et al., 2018). Matthew Wood (2008) has pointed out that calendula’s ability to help internal drainage from wounds may also indicate its affinity with the lymphatic system and the veins.

Keeping the lymph system moving is vital for a strong immune system, and calendula is also used as an immune tonic (Popham, n.d.). It is known as a European peasant tonic for the immune system, particularly in wintertime, as it may remove old, toxic lymphatic congestion and stubborn infections (Wood, 2008). This use goes back some time: an English translation of Charles Estienne and Jean Liébault’s (1616) Maison Rustique noted that calendula was also taken for plague or pestilent agues. Some herbalists still include calendula flowers in foods and herbal preparations during the winter to keep colds and the flu at bay (Blakespoor, 2012). Others indicate its use after an acute infection has passed when there are aftereffects such as a low-grade fever, fatigue, or swollen glands (Popham, n.d).

A particular chemical constituent may play a role in calendula’s immune properties: in vitro studies on granulocytes (a type of white blood cell) have determined that a polysaccharide fraction of the plant demonstrated immunostimulant activity (Varljen et al., 1989; Wagner et al., 1985). Beyond this, however, the entwined aspects of calendula’s lymphagogic and immune activity, well known amongst herbalists and folk practitioners, appears still unstudied in any depth in the scientific literature, particularly in clinical studies.

Calendula is also a diaphoretic that has long been used for fevers: it warms the system and pushes fluids to the surface to promote sweating, which helps the body fight off pathogens, subsequently easing fever as well as removing toxins from the body (Popham, n.d.). It has also been used for eruptive fevers, in conditions such as measles, smallpox, or scarlet fever (Culpeper, 1653/1850; Holmes, 2006). Herbalist Anne McIntyre (1996) suggests that in the case of chickenpox or measles, calendula helps the virus erupt on the skin and expel toxins. Wood (2008) has also noted that calendula is a solid herb for deep fevers, particularly when the bones hurt. Calendula’s traditional usage as a diaphoretic—which in Europe has been via leaf usage, not flower (Khalid & Teixeira da Silva, 2012)—has not yet been explored by scientific research.

Calendula’s slightly bitter taste also hints at its use for the digestive system. The herb is considered a cholagogue and hepatic, supporting the function of the gallbladder (Hoffmann, 2003) and liver (Popham, n.d.). By stimulating the secretion of bile and digestive enzymes, calendula supports the digestive process, improves nutrient absorption (McIntyre, 1996), and eases epigastric fullness (Holmes, 2006). Calendula is also taken internally for inflammatory conditions of the digestive tract such as ulcers (Hoffmann, 2003), gastric reflux, inflammatory bowel disease (Blankenspoor, 2012), and leaky gut syndrome (Popham, n.d.). Its mucilage content is wonderfully soothing to irritated tissues and contributes to its demulcent action (Popham, n.d.). It was used by early 20th-century practitioners for ulcers and cancers of the stomach (Wood, 2008). As Wood (2008) notes, not only does calendula soothe digestive mucosa, but it may also warm the stomach.

Calendula may also contribute to the wellbeing of the digestive system due to its spasmolytic action. It has been studied in vitro in relation to its traditional indications for constipation and stomach cramps and found to have both spasmogenic and spasmolytic constituents (Bashir et al., 2006). In line with both the theme of infections and of digestive health is calendula’s use as a vermifuge. Both its flowers and leaves have been deemed anthelmintic in a study that also asserted that this action may arise from its saponins (Purwal et al., 2009).

There is one more major area in which calendula truly blossoms: the herb has been commonly known as a menstrual and reproductive ally. Beyond use for uterine fibroids at menopause or recovering from childbirth, calendula is particularly employed for the regulation of menstruation (Wood, 2008). This may be due to its antispasmodic and emmenagogue actions, which help to resolve delayed menstruation, regulate the cycle, and ease tension, cramping, and pain (Hoffmann, 2003; McIntyre, 1996). Herbalist David Hoffmann (2003) considers it “a normalizer of the menstrual process” (p. 535). Peter Holmes (2006) notes that calendula brings together a hormone-balancing action with that of uterine stimulant and blood decongestant to address dysmenorrhea arising from Liver qi stagnation, and that it may furthermore be used for premenstrual issues on a physiological—rather than emotional—level.

Calendula is now having its traditional indication for tumors and cancers amplified by scientific cancer studies. The herb seems to have been popular for addressing tumors and cancers in the late 19th/early 20th centuries, particularly for cancers of the stomach (Felter & King, 1898; Wood, 2008). Matthew Wood (2008) also cites it as being soothing on the emotional remedy for individuals who are fearful of cancer. Calendula oil is still used as an antitumor agent (Ashwlayan et al., 2018). At least one study has looked at calendula in vitro and found two possible anticarcinogenic effects: lymphocyte activation and cytotoxic tumor cell activity (Jiménez-Medina et al., 2006). An ethyl acetate extract of C. arvensis flowers has recently demonstrated cytotoxic activity against breast cancer cells in vitro (Abutaha et al., 2019).

While much remains to be understood about calendula’s diverse applications, it remains an easy herb to incorporate into the kitchen as well as the garden. As an edible flower, calendula is still used to liven up dishes such as soups, salads, and fish. In older days, its florets were used to color cheese (Grieve, 1931/1971) as well as butter and custards; it was also used as a substitute for saffron, for which use it is still called “poor man’s saffron” (Tucker & DeBaggio, 2009). Calendula flowers at one time furnished a dye for fabric and hair (Tucker & DeBaggio, 2009).

Adult Dose

Tincture: 1-4 mL (1:5, 60%) 3x/day (Hoffmann, 2003).

Tea: 8 fl oz (1-2 teaspoons dried flowers in 1 cup boiling water) 3x/day (Hoffmann, 2003).

Topical: Lotion, salve, or wash as needed (Hoffmann, 2003).

Safety

There are a few safety concerns with calendula. It should not be used during pregnancy, as it has traditionally been used as an emmenagogue and is classed as a uterine stimulant (Blakespoor, 2012; Brinker, 2010).

Calendula is a possible allergen for those sensitive to the Asteraceae plant family (Brinker, 2010; Hoffmann, 2003). While not yet studied in human trials, the herb may have potential for enhancing hypnotic or anxiolytic drugs (Brinker, 2010) and it has been shown—only in animal studies thus far—to increase sedative and hypotensive effects; therefore, it may potentially interact with drugs or agents with these actions as well as those with hypoglycemic or cholesterol-lowering actions (Ashwlayan et al., 2018).

Another concern with calendula relates to its prominent use as a vulnerary; herbalists report that wounds must be fully clean before application because they have observed that the herb rapidly repairs tissue (Holmes, 2006).
