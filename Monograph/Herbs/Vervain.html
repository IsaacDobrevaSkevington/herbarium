Vervain

Native To

Europe, southern Asia, Australia, and Africa (Allen & Hatfield, 2004; Royal Botanic Gardens of Kew [RBGK], n.d.).

Geographic Distribution

Its distribution may be classified as Eurasian Southern-temperate, but vervain is widely naturalized outside of that category (Botanical Society of Britain & Ireland and the Biological Records Centre, 2019), having been introduced not just into Ireland and Great Britain, but also parts of South America and North America (Allen & Hatfield, 2004; Royal Botanic Gardens of Kew [RBGK], n.d.)

Botanical Description

Vervain is a perennial plant that possesses medium to dark green opposite leaves with toothed lobes. It generally grows to between about 1-2 feet in height. Its five-petaled flowers, perched in spike formation, are tiny and pale lavender in color, often with whitish centers. As they bloom, the flowers become more distantly spaced from one another.

Vervain’s stems are very slender and wiry in appearance, branching out in the uppermost portions, and the visual outline of the plant as a whole is diamond-shaped (Barker, 2001). The stems, because they are somewhat squarish, may be confused with mints (Elpel, 2010). The herb does not emit a scent.

In terms of habitat, vervain is broad in scope: it favors fields, roadsides, and open expanses as well as forest borders, walls, and rock outcrops, all while preferring free-draining soils that are often calcareous in nature (Botanical Society of Britain & Ireland & Biological Records Centre, 2019).

Key Constituents

Iridoid glycosides (verbenalin, verbanine, bastatoside, cornin), triterpenes, phenylpropanoids, flavonoids (Tobyn et al., 2016), tannins, mucilage (Holmes, 2007), volatile oils (citral), vitamin K (Argento et al., 2000; Barker, 2001; De Martino et al., 2009; Yu et al., 2016a).

Sustainability

While there appears to be no concrete data available at this time, vervain’s population has declined in eastern England (Botanical Society of Britain & Ireland and the Biological Records Centre, 2019).

Harvesting Guidelines

Vervain should be gathered immediately prior to flowering (Barker, 2001; Grieve, 1931) and dried at once as the quantity of its iridoid glycoside verbenalin is highest at this time (Calvo et al., 2000; Tobyn et al., 2016). Vervain must be gathered earlier in summer before the stems dry out if it is desired for juicing (Tobyn et al., 2016).

Uses

While European vervain (Verbena officinalis) is largely portrayed today in Western herbal practice as a nervine, this gentle herb is historically extremely wide ranging in application. Since antiquity, vervain has also notably been linked to magical practices and considered a pancaea. Despite its immense number of traditional indications—many of which survived into the early 20th century—vervain has thus far received relatively little sustained scientific inquiry of either a broad or deep nature. There have historically been several other species in use that have been employed in some ways similar to common vervain, among them V. jamaicensis, V. lappulaceae, V. urticifolia, and V. sinuata (Grieve, 1931), and, most prominently, the blue vervain of the American continents, V. hastata, and V. odorata which is aromatic, unlike common vervain, and makes a pleasant, fragrant tea.

Vervain’s often suspect affiliation with magic stretches all the way back to the first century CE. The Roman polymath Pliny the Elder (23-79 CE) highlighted a bit of ethnobotany about the herb, namely that the Gallic peoples used two different varieties for soothsaying and predicting the future (Pliny, 77/1906). Pliny also mentioned what he viewed as the idiocy of magicians, who attested that if one rubbed oneself with the plant, they’d obtain the object of their desires, keep away fever, secure help in repairing friendship, and aid every disease (Pliny, 77/1906). Ancient magicians, who gathered the herb via specific rituals, also asserted that banqueting couches, sprinkled with infused vervain water, would promote a good mood amongst company (Pliny, 77/1906), a use that Pedanius Dioscorides (c. 40-90 CE), a Greek physician and botanist who was Pliny’s near contemporary, also recorded (Dioscorides, 70/2000). 

Vervain was not just used in magical contexts by ancient Mediterranean societies; it was widely used by magicians and sorcerers as well as priests and other sacred leaders across cultures and historical periods in various rites. Vervain is most often remembered today as a major plant used by the druids (Wood, 2008), particularly in lustral water (Grieve, 1931). In French, vervain is still known as the herbe aux sorciers (“sorcerer’s plant”). Outside of the suspect practices of some magicians, vervain appears to have been highly esteemed and widely used in ancient Rome in sacred and political contexts; Pliny recorded that there was no other plant that was more regarded than vervain: it was carried in the hands of envoys who interacted with enemies, used to cleanse the table of the god Jupiter, and served for purifying houses and carrying out expiation (Pliny, 77/1906). 

The herb’s ancient connection to magic continued into the medieval period. In the Old English Herbarium it was written that someone who had vervain with them would not be barked at by dogs, and that sorcerers made use of the herb in their practices (Van Ardsall, 2002). Vervain was also found listed in old herbals as among six plants most commonly used as amulets (something also mentioned in an etymological note by Dioscorides, see below), and one herbal gives directions in using it to make guests at the table merry, referring directly back to the ancient practice mentioned by Pliny above (Rohde, 1922).

The magical aspect of vervain filtered into the 18th century. The apothecary (medical writer) John Quincy remarked that “Some pretend it is an Amulet” (Quincy, 1719, p. 132). Its magical reputation also appears to live on today; there are echoes of such usage on the Isle of Man and in Wales (Allen & Hatfield, 2004), where vervain is known in Welsh as ilysiau’r hudol, or “wizard’s herb” (Barker, 2001). Meanwhile, herbalist Julian Barker (2010) asserts that “it has always been a plant of peace and love: good to have around during delicate negotiations!” (p. 360). 

It is interesting to note that across the Atlantic, vervain’s American cousin, blue vervain (V. hastata) is associated with use by some Indigenous tribes for “magical” purposes. The Iroquois people employ a cold infusion of smashed vervain leaves with the specific intention of making an obnoxious person depart (Herrick, 1977).

In terms of etymology, vervain’s botanical name Verbena is directly taken from the Latin, a noun which was very often found in the plural form and was used to describe leafy twigs or boughs of sacred plants generally used for rituals. The plant was also called verbenaca in Latin by Pliny the Elder, who furthermore tells us that, among other names, vervain was known as hierabotane—or “sacred plant” (Pliny, 77/1906), a name derived from its Greek counterpart, ἱερὰ βοτάνη. 

As for some of the herb’s common names, vervain is said to be derived from the Celtic ferfaen, with fer indicating “to drive away” and faen meaning “a stone” (Grieve, 1931), which may be attributed to vervain’s popular and ancient indication for removing bladder stones. Many more of vervain’s common names may be found in the writings of Dioscorides, Parkinson, and Gerard. 

Vervain has been hailed throughout the ages as a general panacea. As far back as ancient Rome, we have seen that it was the magicians who asserted that it was an herb for every single malady, but Pliny also wrote in another passage that vervain was an aid for all the internal organs (Pliny, 77/1906). Similar sentiments survive even today in modern Italy; an old man gathering mullein (Verbascum thapsus) and vervain at the ancient site of Pompeii, just outside Naples, Italy, told one researcher that vervain was “the best of all medicinal plants—it would cure all maladies” (Feemster Jashemski, 1999). Vervain did not enjoy this reputation just among the Italians; in France, vervain is also commonly known as the herbe à tous maux, or “herb for all ailments.”

Even in the East, vervain is broadly and deeply used—it may be connected also to Lung, Kidney, Bladder, and ren meridians (Holmes, 2007) while affecting the organs of liver, lungs, kidneys, stomach, intestines, uterus, skin, nerves, and the immune system (Holmes, 2007). Vervain is generally suited to addressing deficient conditions that need a wide-ranging application to bring the body back into harmony (Holmes, 2007).

Here follows a summary of some of vervain’s main indications, although this account is not exhaustive.

Vervain has been used widely for infections of many different types and phases—most often worms, wounds, plague and pestilence, colds, agues, and fevers. Much of the so-far relatively scant scientific inquiry into vervain has in fact centered on the herb’s antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, and analgesic actions, all of which are pertinent to wounds, different types of infections, and chronic, painful conditions such as rheumatism.

While a great many of vervain’s indications were passed on from antiquity as far back as the Renaissance writers and beyond, the plant seems—at least in extant sources—to have enjoyed a fairly recent reputation as a vermifuge in the 17th century. Both Culpeper (1652) and Parkinson (1640) remarked on vervain’s use for expelling worms, and the herb’s use as a vermifuge is further borne out in folklore records in Gloucestershire (Allen & Hatfield, 2004). Meanwhile, the peoples of southwest China, located in the foothills of the Himalayas, use common vervain as an anthelmintic (Tobyn et al., 2016). The American blue vervain (V. hastata) was also indicated for worms by Cook and Coffin (as cited in Tobyn et al., 2016) and as an anthelmintic by people of the Iroquois tribe in a compound decoction (Herrick, 1977). 

Vervain has been used over many centuries as a vulnerary, in which its astringent, anti-inflammatory, and antimicrobial actions appear to have been key. At least as early as the Roman empire, vervain was utilized as a vulnerary and anti-inflammatory for open wounds, swellings, and possibly tumors. There it was used for gout and chilblains, pounded up with axle grease or the root of another plant, used as a simple, or given as a decoction for foot soaks (Pliny, 77/1906). Vervain was furthermore put to use for all hardened areas created by inflammation, such as masses and swellings, chronic ulcers, and sores (blisters) caused by footwear (Pliny, 77/1906). 

By the medieval period, vervain remained a go-to vulnerary herb, especially when infection was present. Hildegard von Bingen (1155/2001) gave details on an external approach:

Let whoever suffers rotten flesh from ulcers or from worms cook vervain in water. Then place a piece of linen cloth over the putrid sores or over the places made putrid by the worms. Express the water moderately from the vervain, and then place it moderately warm on the top of the linen cloth placed over the putrid flesh. After the vervain has dried out, again place some more cooked in the same way on top of the cloth. Do this until the putridness has been drawn out (pp. 137-138).

The practice described by von Bingen was sustained in European history. In the 17th century, similar use for putrefying ulcers, fistulas, wounds, old swellings, and inflammations were noted by Gerard (1636). Vervain was furthermore used for wounds both internal and external (Culpeper, 1652; Parkinson, 1640), although most often it seems to have been applied externally. Modern ethnobotanical studies demonstrate the continuation of vervain’s vulnerary reputation even today; it is still used in central Italy for addressing wounds (Guarrera et al., 2005). 

Vervain’s many pro-vulnerary actions may also be why it was used for teeth and gum issues as well as eye issues. Vervain was combined with rose oil and vinegar for eye defluxions (Pliny, 77/1906), and Renaissance herbal writers recorded a distilled water of the herb at its peak potency dropped into the eyes for filminess, cloudy vision, and strengthening the optic nerves (Culpeper, 1652; Parkinson, 1640). 

Vervain was furthermore prepared as a gargle for mouth ulcers (Dioscorides, 70/2000), sometimes using the root, boiled in wine and vinegar for this purpose (Pliny, 77/1906) or by simply chewing on the root to strengthen gums and teeth (Pliny, 77/1906). These ancient indications make their way into the Renaissance herbals along with toothache (Culpeper, 1652; Gerard, 1636; Parkinson, 1640). Research has echoed this long-recorded traditional use; one double-blind, randomized, controlled, and multi-center clinical trial has reported good results in using vervain for chronic gingivitis (Grawish et al., 2016).

Vervain was also popularly used in scrofula (swellings in the neck, resulting from lymph node infections or tuberculosis and other mycobacteria). This indication likely appears in both the writings of Pliny and Dioscorides. The Welsh physicians of the Myddfai may have picked up this indication too (Tobyn et al., 2016). By the medieval period, Hildegard von Bingen (1155/2001) was describing a scrofula-like condition and giving instruction:

But if someone’s throat swells up, let them cook some vervain moderately in water, place it moderately warm over the throat, and tie it with a piece of cloth. Do this until the tumor vanishes (p. 138).

Vervain’s traditional use for topical inflammation has seen sustained contemporary scientific review, often largely based on ethnobotany in Spain. One study prepared different formulations for testing, and an extract prepared with V. officinalis in 50% methanol had similar anti-inflammatory effects as piroxicam gel (Calvo, 2006). Another study concluded that ether, chloroform, and methanolic extracts of the herb were all anti-inflammatory (Deepak & Handa, 2000). Animal studies have also confirmed vervain’s topical anti-inflammatory reputation (Calvo et al., 1998). Research has also identified the constituents believed responsible for vervain’s anti-inflammatory action: iridoid and phenylpropanoid glycosides, triterpenoids, and flavonoids (Tobyn et al., 2016).

The antimicrobial aspects of vervain have perhaps received the rest of the bulk of current scientific scrutiny. Whole leaf and root methanol extracts of vervain showed antimicrobial activity against several bacterial strains, with the leaf extract effective against Staphylococcus aureus, Shigella boydii, and Streptococcus pneumoniae and more effective at inhibiting growth than the root extract (Sahile et al., 2015). Monoterpene compounds as found in vervain’s essential oil, which belong to the chemotype of thymol and carvacrol, showed antimicrobial activity against plant and human pathogens (Elshafie et al., 2016). Vervain extract has also been studied for food preservation purposes, specifically to prolong the post-harvest life of oranges, due to its antifungal action (Camele et al., 2010). 

One study, in part fueled by the rising difficulty of addressing some infectious diseases due to antibiotic resistance, set out to evaluate the antimicrobial potential of ethanolic extracts of vervain’s leaves, stems, and roots against 24 different strains of gram-positive and gram-negative bacteria (Ahmed et al., 2017). The stems were most potent against all the different types of bacterial strains, with activity against some strains higher than that of the antibiotic amoxicillin. The leaves and roots were considerable in their action against a few types of bacteria; therefore, it was concluded that vervain has significant potential in terms of new antimicrobial drug development (Ahmed et al., 2017).

Vervain is also used for pain management. Both Culpeper (1652) and Parkinson (1640) remark that vervain was used for all pains and inner torments, including headaches and labor pains (Gerard, 1636). Similarly, the Iroquois people traditionally apply a poultice of cut, wet root of V. hastata to the head to cool off (Rousseau, 1945). V. hastata has also been used as an analgesic by the Cherokee, both as a tonic and for post-childbirth afterpains (Hamel & Chiltoskey, 1975). Also, the Dakota people have a tradition of decocting its leaves to support a painful stomachache (Gilmore, 1919).

Vervain’s use for fever, a hallmark of the onset of infection, appears as far back as first century CE when Pliny the Elder (77/1906) remarked that magicians used vervain to keep away fever. Byzantine sources from the 6th and 7th centuries CE also indicate vervain for common or quotidian fevers (Tobyn et al., 2016).

In the 17th century, Renaissance herbalists indicated vervain for fever (Culpeper, 1652; Parkinson, 1640). The herb’s bitter principle verbenalin is seen as working somewhat like quinine and in folk records has been of use for fevers in people of the Isle of Man (Allen & Hatfield, 2004; Moore, 1898). 

Along these lines, vervain was indicated for agues, which are conditions such as malaria marked by fevers and chills, throughout the Renaissance (Culpeper, 1652; Parkinson, 1640) and pre-Industrial Revolution periods. In the 18th century, Dr. Quincy (1718) remarked with disdain that “Many Country People pretend to do great Feats with it in Agues, by applying it to the Wrist in the form of a Cataplasm” (pp. 132-133).

Vervain’s use for fever was present both in Eastern traditions and the New World. In Chinese medicine, vervain is viewed as a relaxant that is both a cooling diaphoretic and antipyretic that attends to fever and pain (wind-heat). Herbalist Peter Holmes (2007) suggests pairings with linden (Tilia spp.), black horehound (Ballota nigra), and lavender (Lavandula spp.) for these indications. Vervain’s American cousin, blue vervain (V. hastata) was apparently used in the Revolutionary War—quinine being unavailable—for alternating fevers and chills (Wood, 2009) and was used to provoke a sweat.

V. hastata has been used by Indigenous American tribes as a febrifuge as well: the roots are used by the California Mahuna people for stomach fevers (Romero, 1954) and by the Delaware and Oklahoma peoples in a febrifuge compound for chills (Tantaquidgeon, 1942); and the seeds, leaves, and roots are used by the Cherokee to address the early stages of a fever, colds, and as a general sudorific (Hamel & Chiltoskey, 1975).

Vervain may be historically viewed as something of an alterative or blood purifier, frequently employed to either remove poisons from the body or to generally strengthen it. Its ancient use for snake and spider bites was recorded by Pliny (77/1906) in ancient Rome in the first century CE (77/1906), while Dioscorides (70/2000) remarked that both root and leaves were used against serpents. The Old English Herbarium and 17th century literature discusses the internal use of vervain to remove poisons as well as for bites of venomous creatures and even mad dogs (Parkinson, 1640; Van Arsdall, 2001). 

Vervain may be used in both Eastern and Western traditions as a wide-spectrum herb that brings the body into balance; it is excellent for addressing chronic, complex conditions (Holmes, 2007) and is therefore seen as a blood-cleansing tonic. In Gloucestershire, vervain carries a folk reputation as a tonic for general strengthening (Allen & Hatfield, 2004), and some peoples of North American Indigenous tribes, including the Omaha and Cherokee, have historically enjoyed blue vervain as a beverage (Gilmore, 1919), and as a general tonic (Hamel & Chiltoskey, 1975), respectively. It is generally known to work particularly slowly and gently over time to resolve specifically complex or multi-layered chronic conditions (Barker, 2001). 

Vervain also appears to have been used to stimulate and arrest blood flow. The Old English Herbarium indicated vervain for clogged veins that keep blood from reaching the genitals (as cited in Tobyn et al., 2016; Van Arsdall, 2001). In the 17th century, Parkinson (1640) asserts that it “causeth a good colour in the face and body” (p. 676), which perhaps has something to do with stimulating circulation; Parkinson (1640) and Culpeper (1652) also noted its use for stopping bleeding.

Native American Indigenous peoples have a history of using their native blue vervain, V. hastata, as a hemostat—the Chippewa people use dried vervain flowers as a snuff to stop nosebleed (Densmore, 1928). The scientific literature has explored vervain’s connection with the blood, asserting that vervain is one of a handful of herbs that contains quantities of vitamin K that can impact the effects of anticoagulant therapies (Argento et al., 2000), and the iridoid glycoside cornin, found in its fruit, may hold value for myocardial injury, hemodynamic improvement, and reduction of myocardial damage if results on humans prove similar in vivo and in vitro tests (Yu et al., 2016a), with the potential to improve outcomes post-stroke (Yu et al., 2016b).

Vervain has a strong affinity for the liver and kidneys due to its bitter taste, and this appears to be one of its oldest primary indications from the first century onwards. Vervain was reported as an herb for troubles of the liver and jaundice by Pliny (77/1906) and Dioscorides (70/2000) as well as Parkinson (1640) and Culpeper (1652), and by the mid-18th century onwards it was still directly indicated for obstructions of liver and spleen (Hill, 1755/1812). Some Italian women living in the excavation site of Pompeii in the past decades have greatly esteemed vervain for liver issues (Feemster Jashemski, 1999).

In Chinese medicine, vervain is employed for a range of liver issues, such as hepatitis, jaundice, cirrhosis, and congestion (Tierra, 1988). The basic symptomology of vervain has been described as one in which there is nervous weakness and generalized tension that in fact arises from metabolic toxicosis brought on by renal and hepatic insufficiency (Holmes, 2007).

In the West, vervain has also been used for all diseases of the kidneys and bladder, to break up stones, expel gravel, or deal with dropsy. These indications go back to Roman antiquity (Pliny, 77/1906), where it is followed by the Renaissance writers (Culpeper, 1652; Parkinson, 1640), and it has parallel indications in the East, as a diuretic addressing urinary issues in Chinese medicine (Tierra, 1988), including urinary tract infections and nephritic edema (Tobyn et al., 2016). Italian workers at the ancient site of Pompeii, Italy, also used vervain to purify urine, demonstrating yet another example of an ancient indication that lives on today (Feemster Jashemski, 1999).

In North America, meanwhile, the connection to kidney and liver appears in historical records, with an adopted use of the Houma tribe peoples of Louisiana in the form of a decoction of V. officinalis root for an aid for both organs (Speck, 1941), while the Cherokee people use V. hastata as a kidney aid and for dropsy (Hamel & Chiltoskey, 1975). Furthermore, the people of the Menominee tribe of Wisconsin use a root infusion of V. hastata to clear up cloudy urine (Smith, 1923). 

Vervain is also antispasmodic, used as a relaxant since ancient times for the respiratory, gastrointestinal, reproductive, and musculoskeletal systems.

Pliny (77/1906) noted vervain as an aid for all the troubles of lungs and chest; its anti-inflammatory, antispasmodic, and antibacterial actions all likely play a role. Vervain meanwhile appears to have been applied in the medieval period as a poultice for the lungs (Tobyn et al., 2016), and in the 17th century for all disorders of the lungs and kidneys, for coughs, wheezing, and shortness of breath (Culpeper, 1652; Parkinson, 1640). A Spanish study using ethnobotanical interviews proposed vervain for pharmacological validation with respect to respiratory conditions (Cavero & Calvo, 2014), and a survey of the herbal folk traditions from Valvestino (in Brescia, Italy) revealed modern use of the entire herb for both coughs and asthma (Tobyn et al., 2016; Vitalini et al., 2009). 

Its application as a relaxant for the respiratory system is another example of vervain’s parallel uses around the world. In America, V. hastata has been used as a relaxant for coughing, and in pertussis and bronchitis (Tobyn et al., 2016). In Chinese medicine, vervain is prepared in a warm infusion for coughing, which is a symptom of wind-heat or lung qi constraint with such issues as asthmatic breathing and a tightened chest (Holmes, 2007). 

Some of the Renaissance herbals declare vervain to be used for all imbalances of the stomach (Culpeper, 1652; Parkinson, 1640). Vervain is used for what Chinese medicine describes as qi constraint. It is also used for the gastrointestinal system, which includes such issues as irritable bowel syndrome (IBS), nervous indigestion, indigestion in general, and colic-like pains (Holmes, 2007). 

The concept of qi constraint may also be applied to the reproductive system, specifically menstruation, where its antispasmodic action found particular prominence in the Americas with the Eclectics, assisting with dysmenorrhea (Tobyn et al., 2016). It was also used for amenorrhea and to stimulate lactation (Tobyn et al., 2016). Vervain was used for labor pains in the Americas but likely in part serving as a relaxant as well as a vulnerary; vervain’s root in water was considered useful for all troubles during or post-childbirth in antiquity as well (Pliny, 77/1906). 

Its antispasmodic action combined with its analgesic action might also indicate vervain for musculoskeletal disorders and rheumatism. Vervain was furthermore used for epilepsy by the Romans (Pliny, 77/1906), and used for seizures and muscular spasms in general in the Americas (Tobyn et al., 2016). An ethnobotanical review of herbs from Navarra, Spain points to vervain as one of four herbs for musculoskeletal disorders (Cavero & Calvo, 2015).

Today, vervain is primarily viewed as a nervine, specifically a neurotrophorestorative that also acts as a general detoxicant, particularly with diuretic, liver stimulation, and emmenagogic actions (Holmes, 2007). This connection to the nervous system does not seem to appear in antiquity and is not prominent in the earlier historical records of Western Europe. The first references to vervain’s use for restoring the nervous system might be in the 17th century, when Parkinson (1640) remarked that it was useful for individuals who had fallen into a frenzy and Culpeper (1652) wrote that it was used for those who were “frenetick” (p. 233).

Indications for the nervous system seem to become clearer and far more numerous by around the mid-18th century and especially since the Eclectics’ use of V. hastata. Sir John Hill (1755/1818) noted that sustained use of the herb resolved nervous system issues. Contemporary herbalist Julian Barker (2001) has noted that its “specific indications do not fully communicate how reliably vervain ensures the efficacy of a prescription for almost any condition where spasm, exhaustion, anxiety, depression…may be found” (p. 359). An ethnobotanical study from northern Portugal has revealed vervain being traditionally used for depression, insomnia, stress, and nervousness (Neves et al., 2009; Tobyn et al., 2016).

V. hastata, meanwhile, has a greater reputation as a nervine than its European cousin, and was sometimes considered more powerful, more deep-acting, and more attuned to the nervous system, being a more intense bitter than its European cousin (Wood, 2009). Blue vervain was especially championed by Cook and Coffin and by the 19th century Eclectics in general, who viewed V. hastata as more effective (Holmes, 2007). Blue vervain is often seen as synonymous with V. officinalis, although it bears far showier flowers as one major botanical difference and is known for containing saponins (Wood, 2009). V. hastata has long been considered not only more powerfully bitter than European vervain but also a prominent emetic; Coffin ranked it next to lobelia (Lobelia inflata) (Tobyn et al., 2016) and the Cherokee people have a tradition of using its leaves, roots, and seeds to produce vomiting (Hamel & Chiltoskey, 1975). 

Researchers have worked towards confirming European vervain’s anxiolytic, sedative, and anticonvulsant actions (Khan et al., 2016). One study has confirmed the hypothesis that vervain is cytoprotective towards the cells of the central nervous system and suggested that it might be worth looking at as a neuroprotective agent for Alzheimer’s disease (Lai et al., 2006).

In Chinese medicine, vervain addresses tension and weakness from constrained qi—very often chronic—that typically arises from metabolic toxicity and a weak nervous system that comes from both hepatic and renal issues (Holmes, 2007). This may involve chronic pain, fatigue, stress, or depression. In such cases, vervain might be ideally paired up with motherwort (Leonurus cardiaca), skullcap (Scutellaria lateriflora), or St. John’s wort (Hypericum perforatum) (Holmes, 2007).

Vervain’s tiny, inconspicuous flowers were also chosen by Dr. Bach as one of his primary 38 flower essences. In his system, vervain flower essence tends to restraint and self-discipline (Scheffer, 1990) and is not without a connection to the nervous system. Often vervain-type folks in a negative state are so enthusiastic about an idea or cause that they try to carry others along in too forceful a manner, becoming high strung and unable to relax in general, exhibiting raw nerves when things don’t go well, and fanatacism (Scheffer, 1990; Wood, 2009). 

Many herbs are now investigated for their application to cancer. We have seen that vervain was used for “swellings” that may have indicated tumors as early as the first century CE. Herbalist Julian Barker (2001) however, has taken this a step further and noted its reputation for an antitumor action, remarking that “whether its anti-tumor reputation is specifically deserved remains to be seen; more probably it derives from a general immune-enhancement” (p. 360). In 2013, studies on the subject of vervain and cancer included a foray into the antitumor effects of a vervain extract on tumor-bearing mice and immune function, and although this was an animal study, it determined that vervain did exert an in vivo anti-tumor effect, increased the spleen index, and did not damage immune function (Kou et al., 2013). 

At least one human study has looked at the essential oil of vervain and its constituent citral as an agent that induces apoptosis in chronic lymphocytic leukemia, determining after looking at a pool of normal blood donors and those with leukemia that lower concentrations of both extracts yield greater apoptosis, perhaps via direct procaspase 3 activation, and concluding that it might be worth exploring these compounds to develop new beneficial agents (De Martino et al., 2009).

Vervain’s energetics have been described differently over time or have shifted. In medieval times, Hildegard von Bingen (1155/2001) noted that vervain was “more cold than warm” (p. 137). In the 17th century, it was described by at least two sources as being hot as well as dry and bitter (Culpeper, 1652; Parkinson, 1640), while close contemporary Gerard (1636) viewed it as having a drying temperature that bound and cooled. Holmes (2007) views vervain as bitter, cooling, and even neutral.  

Adult Dose

Infusion: 1-3 tsp dried herb in 8 fl oz boiling water, 3x/day (Hoffmann, 2003); 3-9 g/day (Tierra, 1988).

Tincture: 2-5 mL (1:3, 30%) (Holmes, 2007); 2.5-5 mL (1:5, 40%) 3x/day (Hoffmann, 2003).

Safety

Vervain is often confused with lemon verbena (Aloysia triphylla), known as vervaine in France, and which is not interchangeable with V. officinalis, V. hastata, or other species of the verbena family.

Vervain is a uterine stimulant and as such is contraindicated in pregnancy by many herbalists (Holmes, 2007). It is also speculatively suggested that it not be used specifically in early pregnancy due to the herb’s emmenagogic nature (Brinker, 2010).

There has been at least one case report of contact dermatitis arising from an application of vervain and egg white plaster to the chest, but that reaction was complex and may have been due to several factors (Mills & Bone, 2005).

It has been suggested that vervain not be imbibed by vegans and vegetarians with meals; its phenylpropanoids may interfere with non-heme iron absorption (Tobyn et al., 2016).
