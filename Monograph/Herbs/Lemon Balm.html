Lemon Balm

Melissa officinalis

Native To

Southern Europe and the Mediterranean Basin to Central Asia (Royal Botanic Gardens Kew [RBGK], n.d.).

Geographic Distribution

Lemon balm has been introduced to other parts of Europe, Russia, New Zealand, North America, and South America (RBGK, n.d.).

Botanical Description

Lemon balm is a lemon-scented, erect perennial that can grow up to 3 feet (90 centimeters) tall (Bomme et al., 2013). The herb is anchored into the soil with many lateral roots (Moradkhani et al., 2010). The simple, heart-shaped or oval leaves are slightly hairy, with toothed margins and prominent veins on the adaxis, the upper leaf surface (Bomme et al., 2013). The opposite leaves are attached to a square stem characteristic of the mint family. During the vegetative state, the vibrant green leaves can grow up to 3.5 inches (9 centimeters) long; once the herb enters its generative state and flower stalks appear, new leaves become oval and much smaller, clearly different in their appearance (Bomme et al., 2013).

Flowering branches arise from the lower leaf axils in the summer and carry small bilabiate corollas that flower in clusters at the upper ends of the stem (Evans, 2009). Flowers are usually white, sometimes with a slightly pink or yellow hue, and are held in the leaf axils (Wisconsin Horticulture, n.d.).

Key Constituents

Volatile oils: (0.06-0.375%): monoterpenes (>60%) including geraniol, nerolic acid, citronellal, citral (geranial and neral), β-ocimene; sesquiterpenes (>35%) including β-caryophyllene, germacrene D (Barnes et al., 2007; Miraj et al., 2016); flavonoids (0.5%): glycosides (luteolin, quercetin, apigenin, kaempferol) (Barnes et al., 2007); polyphenols: rosmarinic acid, chlorogenic acid, caffeic acids, protocatechuic acid, hydroxycinnamic acid derivatives (Barnes et al., 2007); metrilic acid (Miraj et al., 2016); tannins, triterpenic acids (Hoffmann, 2003); ursene triterpene glycosides: melissiosides A-C (Abdel-Naime et al., 2019), vitamin C, and carotenoids (Moradkhani et al., 2010).

Sustainability

None known.

Harvesting Guidelines

Lemon balm can be harvested with a knife or sickle bar 6-8 inches (approximately 20 centimeters) above the ground, which ensures regrowth of the herb (Carpenter & Carpenter, 2015). In ideal conditions, lemon balm can be cut two to three times in a season. Herbalist Andrew Chevallier (2000) recommends harvesting the aerial parts just before the flowers open, which is when volatile oils are at their strongest concentration. However, agronomists Bomme et al. (2013) advise harvesting before flower development starts to avoid the many flower stalks, smaller leaves, and yellowing of the lower leaves.

Lemon balm’s active compounds are highly volatile and easily lost when not handled carefully, and fresh leaves are prone to discoloration when touched too often, leading to brown and unsightly dried aerial parts (Dachler & Pelzmann, 1999). While many herbalists use the fresh herb for teas and tinctures (Groves, 2019; Wood, 2008), it is possible to have high-quality dried herb when appropriately processed (Bomme et al., 2013). The cut leaves are best processed immediately and spread on drying screens in a single layer. It is important to ensure constant airflow, avoid light exposure, and dry at low temperatures of around 86°F (30°C) to prevent discoloration (Whitten, 2004). According to agronomists Dachler and Pelzmann (1999), the loss of moisture while drying gives a ratio of 6-7 kg fresh leaves required to yield 1 kg dried herb, while Carpenter and Carpenter (2015) give a ratio of 5 to 1 fresh to dried herb. 

Uses

Lemon balm is a cooling nervine with a strong lemony smell and a milder lemony but deliciously pleasant flavor. This popular mint family plant, whose genus name Melissa means “bee” in Greek (Ravindran, 2018), is an easy addition to the garden, producing thick beds of fragrant leaves that impart a delightful scent on your hands if you gently tousle the plants (Groves, 2019). Lemon balm loves dappled sunlight and moist but well-drained soil; it can be started from either seed or root division and may spread quickly in optimal growing conditions (Groves, 2019). It is an ideal herb to grow on slopes and banks to prevent soil erosion (Platt, 2002). Herbalist and historian Maud Grieve (1931/1971) tells us that lemon balm was a common herb found in potpourri and that its name refers to its “honeyed sweetness” (p. 76), and Shakespeare described lemon balm as a strewing herb in The Merry Wives of Windsor (as cited in Russo & Tyler, 2001).

Lemon balm makes a delicious tea from either fresh or dried leaves, but many herbalists prefer using the fresh herb for the best taste and potency (Groves, 2019; Wood, 2008). Its appealing taste makes it a favorite among children and those with sensitive palates, and its addition to formulas can disguise the bitterness or strong taste of other herbs. Its sweetness lends itself well to glycerites and infused honey. It can also be prepared as a cold infusion, which makes a refreshing beverage in the warm summer months.

Lemon balm has been popular in herbal traditions since antiquity and closely associated with bees ever since it was used. Virgil, the ancient Roman poet (70-21 BCE) recorded the attractiveness of lemon balm to bees (as cited in Marzell, 1938/2002), and Pliny (77/1856) noted that beehives rubbed with lemon balm would keep the swarms there. This practice was common among beekeepers well into the 20th century (Marzell, 1938/2002). Interestingly, research has shown that pheromones excreted by the worker honeybees’ scent glands contain the compounds citral, geraniol, and nerolic acid, which are also found in lemon balm, and could explain the attraction (Burge, 1980). Indeed, a study by Winston et al. (1992) showed that baits containing these compounds were more successful in attracting bee swarms than other methods.

Despite the herb’s longstanding use, the European Medicines Agency (EMA, 2013) only lists three well-documented traditional applications of lemon balm, which are to soothe mild symptoms of mental stress, to support healthy sleep, and to ease mild gastrointestinal upset. The ESCOP (European Scientific Cooperative on Phytotherapy) monographs approve the herb’s internal use for minor spasms and other digestive issues as well as to ease irritability and restlessness, and its external application in cold sores (Mills & Hutchins, 2013).

Modern herbalists apply lemon balm for its spasmolytic and anxiolytic actions, as a nervine tonic, and topically for lesions (Fintelmann & Weiss, 2002; Mills & Bone, 2013). As a carminative and antispasmodic, lemon balm is also used to ease digestive issues including spasms and flatulence; due to its mild antidepressant properties, it is especially indicated for digestive upset related to anxiety or depression (Hoffmann, 2003). The antispasmodic nature of lemon balm is thought to stem from its volatile oils citral and citronellal (Chevallier, 2000).

Herbalist Matthew Wood (2008) indicates lemon balm for an overactive sympathetic nervous system and hyperadrenalism or hyperthyroidism, particularly when heart or stomach issues are present. An in vitro study by Santini et al. (2003) suggests that lemon balm can block thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) from binding to its receptor through the herb’s action on both the hormone and the receptor. Two case studies report on the beneficial effects of a combined herbal preparation with lemon balm and bugleweed (Lycopus spp.) on two individuals with Graves’ hyperthyroidism and showed that their thyroid-stimulating immunoglobulin (TSI) normalized after a few months (Kaplan & Dosiou, 2021). However, persons suffering from thyroid conditions should consult a healthcare practitioner before taking lemon balm preparations, which may negatively affect thyroid hormone medication (Winston & Maimes, 2019).

Although less common, lemon balm can also be used as a diaphoretic in the form of a hot tea to promote perspiration. Matthew Wood (2008) indicates the herb for fevers accompanied by nervousness and depression following exhaustive fevers or when digestion has been irritated. The herb’s antiviral and calming anxiolytic properties can also provide support during febrile conditions, and it is gentle enough that it can be applied for fevers in young children (Gosling, 2017).

In De Materia Medica, the ancient Greek physician Dioscorides (70/2000) called lemon balm melissophullon (meaning “bee leaf”) and suggested the leaves decocted in wine and consumed or applied externally as a poultice for scorpion, spider, and dog bites. The herb was also used as a mouth rinse to ease toothache, and drunk as a decoction for gastrointestinal issues such as dysentery, mushroom poisoning, and stomach cramps. Lemon balm syrup was suggested to ease difficult breathing, while the herb mixed with salt was applied topically to cleanse ulcers and, interestingly, reduce goiter (Dioscorides, 70/2000), which is associated with thyroid dysfunction.

Lemon balm’s antimicrobial properties might explain some of these topical uses for skin ulcers and other wounds. The herb’s essential oil has shown antimicrobial activity in vitro against Candida albicans and gram-positive bacteria such as Staphylococcus aureus (Hăncianu et al., 2008). Herbalists also commonly make use of lemon balm’s antiviral action to address issues such as cold sores, shingles, and chicken pox (Chevallier, 2000). Lemon balm’s antiviral action is attributed to its polyphenol content (Gladstar, 2012). Topical applications of lemon balm on herpes simplex sores have been shown to improve healing time (Koytchev et al., 1999) and authors Pizzorno et al. (2016) note that the herb can reduce the recurrence of cold sores.

Roman naturalist Pliny the Elder (77/1856) describes lemon balm in similar ways to Dioscorides and adds that the leaves are an excellent soother for bee and wasp stings. The herb was also applied in afflictions of the joints, to support intestinal health, and to sustain the spleen. Pliny (77/1856) also suggested lemon balm juice with honey to clear the eyes, an application that was still common in Renaissance herbalism but is no longer common in modern herbal literature.

Lemon balm is also known as the “gladdening herb” (Humphrey, 2003) andhas been used to lift the spirits for many centuries, even predating the Middle Ages (Ehrlich, 2011). Truly a balm to the spirits, lemon balm is used to soothe anxiety, nervousness, and depression (Groves, 2019). This herb is sometimes called a nervous system trophorestorative (Hoffmann, 2003), a word indicating that over time, an herb tonifies, nourishes, and restores balance to the body, usually with an affinity to a particular organ or body system (American Botanical Council, n.d.).

Interestingly, the Greek physician and philosopher Galen (129-210 CE) is thought to be the first scholar to indicate lemon balm for the central nervous system and mood disorders (Ghazizadeh et al., 2022). This was developed further by the physicians at the Academy of Jundishapur, the world’s first university, which was established in the year 271 CE in Persia (now Iran) (Daneshfard et al., 2022). As scholars Ghazizadeh et al. (2022) describe, in the 9th century the Persian physician Shapur ibn Sahl suggested using lemon balm to encourage a positive mood, and to ease anxiety-induced palpitations, faintness, melancholia, sadness, and panic. Tenth-century Persian physician and philosopher Rhazes also attributed mood-enhancing properties to lemon balm, particularly in depression, and described the herb’s beneficial effects on neurological issues such as epilepsy and amnesia. Physician and philosopher Ibn Sina (known in the West as Avicenna) (980-1037) praised lemon balm in The Canon of Medicine (Al-Qanun fi’t-Tibb), one of the most widely used medical manuscripts by physicians in both the Islamic world and Europe up to the 18th century, where he described the mood-enhancing properties of lemon balm and its ability to soothe heartache. In addition, Ibn Sina noted the herb’s beneficial effects on cerebral circulation, its anxiolytic and antioxidant properties, and as a general tonic for all organs (as cited in Ghazizadeh et al., 2022).

Several clinical trials seem to support the positive effects of lemon balm on mood regulation. In a clinical trial by Kennedy et al. (2003), mood was assessed in 20 healthy individuals given a single dose of standardized lemon balm extract in capsule form. The researchers found a significant increase in calmness in those given lemon balm extract compared to placebo, with the best mood-modulation effects occurring at a dosage of 300 mg (Kennedy et al., 2003). In 2004, Kennedy et al. found that a dose of 600 mg of lemon balm extract resulted in ameliorated negative moods following stress and was associated with significantly increased feelings of calmness and decreased feelings of alertness among participants. In addition, those who took a lower dose of 300 mg were significantly faster at mathematical processing with no reduction in accuracy (Kennedy et al., 2004). Furthermore, Araj-khodaei et al. (2020) assessed the efficacy of a twice-daily 500 mg dried lemon balm preparation on mild to moderate depression and found that lemon balm had a comparable effect to fluoxetine, a commonly prescribed antidepressant.

As a sedative and anxiolytic herb, lemon balm is used to calm restlessness and nightmares, especially in children (Gladstar, 2012). It is a lovely ally for both children and adults with sleep disturbances, especially in combination with other sedative herbs like valerian (Valeriana officinalis) and passionflower (Passiflora incarnata) (Groves, 2019). A placebo-controlled study by Taavoni et al. (2013) investigated the effects of a formula containing valerian and lemon balm extracts on sleep disruption associated with menopause and found significantly improved quality of sleep among those taking the herbs compared with the placebo group, with no reported side effects.

Researchers Cases et al. (2011) demonstrated both the anxiolytic properties of lemon balm and its effect on sleep quality. The open-label, 15-day pilot study involving participants suffering from mild to moderate anxiety and disrupted sleep showed that a twice-daily 300 mg dose of a standardized hydroalcoholic extract reduced both anxiety and insomnia. The anxiolytic action is thought to involve inhibition of the enzyme GABA transaminase, leading to an increase in GABA availability in the brain (Cases et al., 2011). A small placebo-controlled clinical trial by Haybar et al. (2018) involving participants suffering from chronic stable angina showed that a daily dose of 3 g dried lemon balm over 2 months had a positive impact on anxiety, stress, depression, and sleep.

Lemon balm, also called the “scholar’s herb,” (Humphrey, 2003), is also employed to improve memory and concentration (Easley & Horne, 2016) and is sometimes used in cases of attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) as well as Alzheimer’s disease (Winston & Maimes, 2019). One clinical study found that a combination of lemon balm leaf and valerian (Valeriana officinalis) root extracts significantly improved social behavior and symptoms in children under 12 with hyperactivity, concentration problems, and impulsiveness (Gromball et al., 2014). Other researchers are starting to look to lemon balm as an aid for those with dementia and Alzheimer’s disease; one study found that individuals with mild to moderate Alzheimer’s disease who took a standardized lemon balm extract for 4 months showed significant improvements in cognitive function and less agitation compared with those given a placebo (Akhondzadeh et al., 2003). 

German abbess and herbalist Hildegard von Bingen (1098-1179) called lemon balm Binsuga, meaning “bee eye” and writes that whoever eats the herb will be merry and laugh a lot, as lemon balm’s warming energy works on the spleen, which makes the heart happy (as cited in Marzell, 1938/2002). In medieval humoral medicine, an unbalanced spleen was believed to accumulate “black bile” causing melancholy and sadness (Riva et al., 2019). These applications are also reflected in old German folk names such as Herzkraut (“heart herb”) and Herztrost (“heart heal”) (Marzell, 1938/2002).

Dioscorides, Pliny, and Ibn Sina have been greatly influential in how lemon balm is used to this day, and these ancient scholars are widely cited in the writings of the Renaissance herbalists, including John Gerard (1597/1994), Leonhart Fuchs (1543/2017), and Culpeper (1643/1922). Culpeper (1643/1922) states that lemon balm is ruled by Jupiter and the constellation Cancer and notes that the herb “strengthens nature in all its actions” (p. 21); he suggests using a simple syrup for an upset stomach and general support during illness. Fuchs (1543/2017) writes that lemon balm provokes beautiful dreams. Both physicians describe the herb as warming and drying, quite contrary to modern herbalists who consider lemon balm to be cooling (Gosling, 2017; Wood, 2008).

In his book Flora Magica, folklorist Isidoor Teirlinck (1926) describes lemon balm, along with catnip (Nepeta cataria) and sage (Salvia officinalis), as one of three magical herbs, with rather interesting magical applications. For example, the herb mixed with the juice of a 1-year-old cypress (Cupressus spp.) makes a bowl of soup or porridge appear full of worms, while the juice mixed with the sweat of a red-haired person and put into their belt would promptly cause the belt to burst open. Lemon balm was also believed to have the power to make you rise above your enemies and be loved by everyone. Lemon balm is one of the 16 magical herbs described in The Book of Secrets by Saint Albertus Magnus (1200-1280), who assigned exceptional powers to the plant, although they are not specified (as cited in Teirlinck, 1926).

The occultist Giuseppe Balsamo (1743-1795), better known as Cagliostro, drank lemon balm in his 40-day course for complete rejuvenation and claimed to have lived many centuries using his method; however, records have shown that Cagliostro passed away at 52 (cited in Teirlinck, 1926). Nonetheless, lemon balm has been associated with rejuvenation and immortality. Grieve (1931/1971) notes two centenarians who breakfasted with lemon balm tea.

Several authors state that the Swiss physician and alchemist Paracelsus (1493-1541) created primum ens melissae, which was considered an elixir of life (Cotnoir, 2006; Tucker & DeBaggio, 2009). However, it’s unclear if Paracelsus indeed used lemon balm this way, or if this is a later addition of the physician and occultist Franz Hartmann (1896) who extensively wrote about the life of Paracelsus. The German alchemist Olaf Rippe quotes Paracelsus, stating “of all the plants that the earth brings forth, lemon balm is the best for the heart” (Rippe et al., 2007, p. 227). Furthermore, the herb was also a constituent of the elixir subtilitatis, used to cleanse the body and made with olive oil, honey, brandy, lemon balm, and greater celandine (Chelidonium majus) (Rippe et al., 2007).

Lemon balm has long been used as a culinary herb in a wide variety of dishes and drinks (Fleischhauer et al., 2016). During the Middle Ages, it was a common aromatic in beers and ales to improve flavor (Buhner, 1998), and it is an important ingredient in Bénédictine, a French herbal liqueur (Grossman & Lembeck, 1977).

​​The fragrant herb is also the main component of the well-known Carmelite water, Eau de Melisse de Carme, an alcoholic herbal tonic believed to be reviving, promote longevity, and ease nervous headaches, gout, and neuralgia (Grieve, 1931/1971; James, 1747; Meyers, 2007). The elixir traditionally contained 13 different herbs and was used both internally and externally. The history of Carmelite water is complex and obscure. Herbal sources commonly report that Carmelite water originated in the Abbey Saint Juste in France; however, historians Schwedt and Heckelmann (2019) question this origin, because there is no evidence that Carmelites ever resided in that Abbey. It is known, however, that the elixir was commercialized in 1611 by the Teresian Carmelites in Paris (Schwedt & Heckelmann, 2019) and sold to the Boyer family in 1838, who produces the elixir to this day (Eau des Carmes Boyer, n.d.). In 1826, the German nun Maria Clementine Martin developed a similar lemon balm tonic called Klosterfrau Melissengeist, which is still very popular (Schwedt & Heckelmann, 2019).

Physician Robert James (1747) provides a recipe for Carmelite water, which he claims lists the original ingredients and is made with 4 ounces of fresh lemon balm leaves, 2 ounces fresh lemon (Citrus x limon) peel, 1 ounce nutmeg (Myristica fragrans) seeds, 1 ounce coriander (Coriandrum sativum) seeds, ½ ounce clove (Syzygium aromaticum) buds, ½ ounce cinnamon (Cinnamomum spp.) bark, and ½ ounce angelica (Angelica archangelica) root. Historically, Carmelite water recipes were often distilled, but modern herbalists can simply macerate the herbs in their alcohol of choice, such as brandy or dry white wine.

Lemon balm was historically applied for a variety of conditions affecting uterine health. Dioscorides (70/2000) noted that a lemon balm bath could promote menstruation, and Culpeper (1643/1922) suggested consuming a caudle, a drink made of warm ale or wine mixed with  breadcrumbs, eggs, sugar, lemon balm juice, and rose (Rosa spp.) water, during childbirth to ease pain and promote the release of the placenta after birth. Modern herbalists suggest lemon balm to ease painful menstruation (Wood, 2008). Although lemon balm was historically used to promote menstruation, modern herbalists consider it safe for use in moderate amounts during pregnancy to ease morning sickness (Hoffmann, 2003), and as a general digestive tonic and calming herb (Gladstar, 1993).

Recent research supports some of these uses. A placebo-controlled study looked into the effects of lemon balm extract on primary dysmenorrhea and found that lemon balm may decrease moderate to severe dysmenorrhea due to its antispasmodic properties (Mirabi et al., 2017). In another small double-blind, placebo-controlled trial, participants who took two 600 mg lemon balm capsules daily from the first to the last day of their menstrual cycle experienced a decrease in the intensity of premenstrual syndrome symptoms (Akbarzadeh et al., 2015).

Researchers have also studied lemon balm for its use in postpartum afterpains and found that lemon balm capsules administered every 6 hours for the first 24 hours after childbirth significantly outperformed mefenamic acid, a nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug, and reduced the need for conventional analgesics (Dastjerdi et al., 2019). Although previous research suggests that rosmarinic acid may be responsible for the herb’s analgesic effects, Dastjerdi et al. (2019) state that the antioxidant properties of lemon balm may also be a large contributor; antioxidants reduce prostaglandin formation, which in high concentrations can increase pain sensitivity, by blocking the oxidation of arachidonic acid.  

Research shows that this gentle herb may have protective effects against radiation. A study by Zeraatpishe et al. (2011) suggests strong potential for lemon balm as a radioprotectant that shields DNA from radiation-induced damage. Researchers gave 55 radiology workers 2 cups of lemon balm tea per day for a month, taking measurements of their plasma before and after the tea. They found that after just 1 month, the antioxidant activity increased and DNA damage decreased. These findings led the researchers to conclude that lemon balm tea improved markers of oxidative stress and DNA damage associated with low-dose radiation and may be helpful for radiation protection among radiology staff (Zeraatpishe et al., 2011).

Adult Dose

Tea: 1 cup (1-2 teaspoons dried herb in 8 fl oz water) 2-4x/day (Winston & Kuhn, 2007).

Tincture: 3-5 mL (1:5, 30%) 3-4x/day (Winston & Kuhn, 2007).

Cold infusion: 4-8 fl oz (0.5 oz dried herb in 1 quart water) 1-4x/day (Easley & Horne, 2016).

Safety

Avoid using in large doses with hypothyroid conditions or Hashimoto’s thyroiditis due to lemon balm’s mild thyroxine-inhibiting effect; large doses may also have a negative effect on thyroid hormone medications (Winston & Maimes, 2019). In cases of thyroid conditions, it is best to use lemon balm under the guidance of a healthcare practitioner (Gladstar, 2012). 

Lemon balm receives a safety rating of ‘A’ in The Nursing Mother’s Herbal, and is thought to be a very mild galactogogue (Humphrey, 2003).
