Chaga

Inonotus obliquus

Ways to use

DecoctionTincture
Common name
Chaga, tinder mushroom, tinder conk, cinder conk, clinker polypore, tschaga (Russian)
Family
Hymenochaetaceae
Chinese medicine name
Bai hua rong
Parts used
Chaga “conk” or sclerotia – both outer black and inner orange portions.

Native To

Europe, Asia, and North America

Geographic Distribution

North America, Finland, Poland, Russia, Siberia, northeastern China, and Japan at latitudes of 45º-50º degrees North

Botanical Description

The chaga conk, or sclerotium, appears as a black, deeply cracked mass that looks a bit like a tumor. One might mistake it for burnt wood or a diseased burl (Jex, 2014). It is often pyramidal in shape, showing years upon years of growth, but it is just as often a wedge or a shapeless mass. Size varies from 5 to 40 cm (Song et al., 2013). The outer, black portion of the conk is very hard, while the inside of the conk is rusty orange with a cork-like woody texture and white veins of mycelia running throughout. The chaga conk is actually sterile, and according to Jex (2014), the fruiting body of this fungus is rarely seen and is nothing showy, just a flat, gray, paste-like sheath that produces spores and then disappears.

Key Constituents

Phenolic compounds, polysaccharides, melanin, triterpenoids (betulin and betulinic acid), and lanostane triterpenoids, among other constituents (Zheng et al., 2010; Winston, 2012; Zhao, 2013; Song et al., 2013).

Sustainability

VULNERABLE FUNGUS. CULTIVATED SOURCES DIFFICULT TO FIND; SEEK ALTERNATIVES.

Chaga is currently being reviewed for inclusion in the United Plant Savers “At Risk” list (United Plant Savers, 2022). Since it takes decades for chaga to reach maturity, wild harvest of chaga is discouraged due to the risk of decimating the slow-growing chaga population and because the long-term effects of chaga harvesting on forest ecology are not yet known (Byers, 2013). Due to chaga’s recent popularity, the fungus is facing heavy wildcrafting pressure. Whenever possible, please choose other fungi or herbs.

Harvesting Guidelines

Please seek alternatives first. If you do opt to harvest this fungus, leave part of the chaga conk on the tree so it can continue to grow.

Uses

Chaga is a white rot fungus in the Hymenochaetaceae family. While it has been found on some hardwood trees, it grows primarily on birch trees and is native to the birch’s range in northern latitudes of Europe, Asia, and North America. Its current range is in North America, Finland, Poland, Russia, Siberia, northeastern China, and Japan at latitudes of 45º-50º degrees North (Song et al., 2013). As a parasitic fungus, chaga spores take hold in the heartwood of the host tree, and the fungus then grows in the trunk and bursts through the bark. The chaga takes 7-20 years to mature (Byers, 2013) and eventually kills its host tree.

Laboratory-grown chaga is available commercially, although its efficacy in comparison to wild chaga is still under study. Chaga’s medicinal secondary metabolites are produced in the wild in response to environmental stressors such as extreme cold temperatures, UV radiation, and pathogenic microbes (Song et al., 2013). Song et al. state, “…the types and yields of active anticancer components are influenced by fermentation conditions” and recommend further research to optimize laboratory cultivation techniques to produce chaga rich in bioactive constituents.

Chaga conks are dried, broken into smaller pieces or powdered, and used to make a decocted tea or extracted in a tincture. To decoct chaga, simmer (don’t boil!) small pieces in water for approximately 15-30 minutes; chaga pieces can be broken into smaller pieces and reused until the resulting decoction becomes too weak. In the case of a tincture, chaga should be extracted first in alcohol and then in water to capture both water- and alcohol-soluble constituents. Chaga can be consumed as a tonic on a daily basis. It can also be burned as an incense for an earthy, slightly sweet aroma. Its ability to maintain a slow burn proved useful among nomadic peoples who used chaga to catch a spark and hold it, becoming a transportable coal (Jex, 2014).

Chaga contains phenolic compounds, polysaccharides, melanin, triterpenoids (betulin and betulinic acid), and lanostane triterpenoids, among other constituents (Zheng et al., 2010; Winston, 2012; Zhao, 2013; Song et al., 2013). Energetically, chaga is grounding, calming, and cooling. It is deeply rooted in earth energy and is a wonderful way to help oneself feel grounded. The taste is earthy with a hint of chai-type spice and vanilla.

Chaga has adaptogenic, immunomodulating, anti-cancer, anti-tumor, anti-inflammatory, antiviral, and anti-cholesterol actions. It has been used in folk herbalism for cancer, digestive system complaints, tuberculosis, heart issues, and/or diabetes since the 16th century in Russia, western Siberia, Eastern Europe, and North America (Song et al., 2013), and was listed in the USSR State Pharmacopeia in 1955. Chaga has been researched extensively in Russia and China, although primarily in vitro and in vivo; human clinical studies are rare.

Chaga is an adaptogen and immunomodulant, due in large part to its immune potentiating polysaccharides (Winston, 2012). An adaptogen helps increase the body’s ability to resist and adapt to internal and external physical, mental, and emotional stressors, while an immunomodulant helps modulate or activate our immune system as needed in response to these stressors. Chaga also benefits the immune system via its antiviral and anti-inflammatory actions, attributed to its betulinic acid content. Consuming chaga as a daily tonic is a wonderful support to the immune system.

The aforementioned actions are also used to support wellness in cases of cancer. Chaga has traditionally been used for cancer in Siberia, Canada, Scandinavia, Russia, and the United States (Tillotson, 2001). Betulinic acid, which chaga uptakes from its host birch tree, shows promising anti-cancer and anti-tumor activity. Noda et al. (1997) report that betulinic acid is only active at the lower pH levels that are typical of the interior pH of tumor tissues; while Fulda et al. (2009) reports betulinic acid shows “potent anticancer activity” through activation of apoptosis (cell death) in cancer cells. Chaga is also high in antioxidants from the phenolic compounds in melanin, the black pigment that is found in the outer “crust” of chaga, as well as the yellow pigments inside the conk. These phenolic compounds help control cell oxidation and reduce damage caused by free radicals. Cui et al. (2005) report that a polyphenolic extract of chaga scavenges free radicals and protects cells against oxidative stress. Other phytochemicals in chaga also show anticancer effects in studies. In general, it is understood that chaga’s anticancer activity is due to its ability to arrest cell cycle progression and induce apoptosis (cell death) (Song et al., 2013).

Chaga has also been used for diabetes and to lower cholesterol. A recent study in diabetic mice showed that an ethyl acetate fraction from chaga had anti-hyperglycemic effects due to its terpenoid and sterol compounds (Lu, 2010). Betulinic acid and beta glucans in chaga help break down LDL cholesterol, thus normalizing overall cholesterol levels and supporting cardiovascular health.

Adult Dose

Tincture: 40-60 drops (1:5) 3x/day (Tillotson, 2001).

Decoction: 1 cup (1-2 tsp in 8 fl oz water) 3x/day (Tillotson, 2001).

Safety

No toxicity issues known. Due to its immunomodulating effect, chaga should not be used with immune suppressant medications.
