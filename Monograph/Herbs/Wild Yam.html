Wild Yam

Dioscorea villosa

Actions

Ways to use

DecoctionFluid ExtractTincture
Common name
Wild yam
Family
Dioscoreaceae
Parts used
Dried rhizomes. Fresh rhizomes can cause nausea and vomiting (Foster & Duke, 2014).

Native To

Wild yam is native to the deciduous hardwood forests of eastern North America (USDA NRCS, n.d.).

Geographic Distribution

Eastern Canada and United States from Ontario and Vermont south to Florida. Central United States as far west as Oklahoma and Missouri and as far south as Texas (USDA NRCS, n.d.).

Botanical Description

Wild yam is highly variable in its form. However, the species complex as a whole bears some very unique identifying characteristics. Wild yam is a smoothed-stem twining vine, decorated with prominently veined heart-shaped leaves alternately placed along its length (Foster & Duke, 2014). It is a mighty climber, taking advantage of whatever structural support it can find and often found growing in clumped masses in the wild. Dioscorea villosa is dioecious, having male and female flowers on separate plants, both which bloom from May through August; neither the male nor female flowers are very conspicuous (Foster & Duke, 2014). However, by autumn, the female plant, if reproductively mature, will bear unique three-winged fruits that hang in bundles (Foster & Duke, 2014). It can take up to 4 years or more for female plants to become reproductive (Cech, 2000). The rhizome is the thickness of a pencil or finger and branches profusely, parallel to and just under the surface of the soil; it has a light-colored core and is very hard, making it difficult to process after drying (Cech, 2002).

Key Constituents

D. villosa contains the steroidal saponins diosgenin, dioscin, dioscorin and others including dioscoreavilloside A and B and parvifloside (Avula et al., 2014), as well as alkaloids, tannin, and starch (Chevallier, 2001). Diosgenin is considered by pharmacology to be the primary active constituent in wild yam; it is structurally similar to cholesterol (Araghiniknam et al., 1996; Datta & Datta, 1984).

Sustainability

AT-RISK PLANT. SEEK CULTIVATED SOURCES OR ALTERNATIVE PLANTS.

Wild yam is on the United Plant Savers “At-Risk” list (United Plant Savers, 2022). As of 2001, the United States Fish and Wildlife Service was considering American wild yam for a CITES Appendix II classification, but wild yam currently remains unregulated. Cultivated sources may be hard to find, but wild yam is easy to grow (United Plant Savers, n.d.). Alternative herbs to consider are chamomile (Matricaria chamomilla), licorice (Glycyrrhiza glabra), catnip (Nepeta cataria) (Cech, 2000), or cultivated black haw (Viburnum prunifolium) bark or cramp bark (Viburnum opulus).

Harvesting Guidelines

The rhizomes of cultivated wild yam plants are traditionally harvested in the fall, after the plant has fixed its primary and secondary constituents for winter dormancy (Cech, 2002). These roots tend to remain relatively close to the surface of the soil and can run for extensive lengths. Harvest wild yam from the base of the vine outwards, leaving the end portions of the rhizome in the ground; this is where future growth takes place, and this will allow the plant to continue to survive after harvest (Cech, 2002). The rhizome should be chopped into small slices and dried using a dehydrator, as they are very prone to molding during the drying process. Fresh wild yam roots have, on average, 64% water content (Cech, 2000) and should be dried to 10% moisture content (Müller & Heindl, 2007). Store dried roots or root pieces in sealed glass jars away from light. Properly stored dried rhizomes of wild yam will retain potency for 1 year (Felter & Lloyd, 1898). A tincture can also be made after drying to prolong storage; be sure to macerate tincture for at least 5 weeks, shaking daily, as the roots are tough and slow to give up their herbal virtues (Cech, 2002). Roots collected in autumn are superior in potency to those collected in the spring (Felter & Lloyd, 1898).

Uses

The taxonomy of the genus Dioscorea is notoriously problematic. For example, Dioscorea villosa was once divided by some taxonomists into two species: D. villosa and D. quaternata (Brinker, 2008). D. villosa var. glabra was once considered a separate variety, under the common name “false wild yam” (Felter & Lloyd, 1898), but like D. quaternata is now considered part of the complex of the single species D. villosa (Foster & Duke, 2014). The only other generally recognized species in the United States, D. floridana, is limited to the Southeast (Cech, 2002). The D. villosa species complex is recognized as being highly variable in its form and exhibiting complex patterns of morphological variation across its geographic range (Flora of North America, n.d.). For herbal purposes, this grouping into a single species is also supported by phytochemistry. In an unpublished study from Herb Pharm’s analytical laboratory, a qualitative and quantitative chromatographic analysis showed a high degree of similarity of constituents and concentration of constituents (total saponins including diosgenin) between D. villosa and D. quaternata, which were considered separate species at the time (Cech, 2000).

Chinese yam, D. oppositifolia (formerly known as D. batatas) is also commonly referred to as “cinnamon vine” or “air potatoes.” This highly invasive and noxious species of Dioscorea primarily propagates and spreads by means of starchy, round bulblets that develop in the leaf axils (air potatoes), distinguishing this species from D. villosa, which lacks this feature. Leaves of D. oppositifolia are also morphologically very different from D. villosa, being arrow shaped with a heart-shaped base and perched upon longer stems. The dried, peeled rhizome is used in Chinese medicine for diarrhea, chronic dysentery, diabetes, frequent urination, and as a nutritive tonic (Foster & Duke, 2014). Due to its highly invasive nature and rampant ability to take over viable habitats for native North American species, many states have banned the sale and planting of D. oppositifolia. Other non-native and highly noxious species that are sometimes sold and grown for herbal purposes include the African air yam (D. bulbifera) and Taiwanese water yam (D. alata) (Miller, 2003).

There are many species of Dioscorea in Mexico that are used in herbalism, the principal ones being D. floribunda, D. macrostachya, D. composita, and D. barbasco (Datta & Datta,1984). Of these, D. floribunda has proven to be the richest source of saponins and is grown on a commercial level to supply the pharmaceutical industry with diosgenin, a steroidal saponin that is used as a precursor in the synthesis of synthetic progesterone and dehydroepiandrosterone (DHEA) (Cech, 2002).

American wild yam (D. villosa) is native to the mixed deciduous hardwood forests of eastern North America and is often found growing in association with other eastern forest medicinals, including black cohosh (Actaea racemosa), mayapple (Podophyllum peltatum), trillium (Trillium spp.), sugar maple (Acer saccharum), red maple (Acer rubrum), ash (Fraxinus americana), paw paw (Asimina triloba), spicebush (Lindera benzoin), and American ginseng (Panax quinquefolius) under a canopy of tulip trees (Liriodendron tulipifera). D. villosa can be found growing in both woodland edges and deep forest, as the species is highly adaptable to variable light conditions. Wild yam prefers partial shade but is able to tolerate both full shade and full sun in cooler northern zones (Cech, 2002). It has been hypothesized that differences in ecological conditions may be responsible for the morphological variation observed for the species (Flora of North America, n.d.). This species is also highly adaptable to diverse soil types, being able to prosper equally in loam, clay, and shale, but prefers rich soil with plenty of organic matter like that of the forest floor (Cech, 2000).

Wild yam’s traditional application in soothing spasms and pain in the ovo-uterine system comes from the Mayan and Aztec peoples of Central America (Chevallier, 2001), as well as the Cherokee of North America, who have traditionally used the root as an analgesic and antispasmodic for pain associated with childbirth (Garrett, 2003). According to an ethnobotanical source, the Meskwaki have also used the root for easing childbirth (Moerman,1998). From these traditional uses of wild yam species, two divergent trends arise: 1) the use of Dioscorea for reproductive hormone balance, and 2) the use of Dioscorea for smooth muscle spasm in the reproductive system, as well as the urinary, gastrointestinal, respiratory, and musculoskeletal systems.

The early Thomsonians, Physiomedicalists, and Eclectics referred to wild yam as “colic root” or “rheumatism root” (Beach, 1852; Cook, 1869; Howard, 1854). This suggests that during the American botanical movement of the late 18th century through the early 20th century, the focus of the materia medica of this species was on its use for spasm, pain, and inflammation. Horton Howard (1854), who is given credit for being first to incorporate wild yam into the Thomsonian and Physiomedical materia medica (Cook, 1869), suggests the use of the infusion of the root in bilious colic as well as the use of the tincture as an expectorant, diaphoretic, and emetic. Physiomedicalist William Cook (1869) used Dioscorea to relax the muscular fibers and soothe the nerves of the intestinal tract, ovo-uterine system, and respiratory system (for example, in cases of spasmodic croup). In this regard, he employed the plant’s relaxant and antispasmodic actions for easing painful and flatulent troubles of the bowels (Cook, 1869). This herb was also classified by the Eclectics as an agent for soothing gastric and intestinal pain, and specifically as a gastrointestinal sedative (Ellingwood, 1915). Similar to its ascribed effects in the ovo-uterine system, Dioscorea was thought to impress its actions upon debilitated and irritable mucous membranes that had become painful from what was thought to be spasmodic contraction of their muscular fibers. In this regard, the herb was thought to be specific for bilious colic, general gastrointestinal irritation, painful abdominal neuroses, and spasmodic irritation of the gastric membranes associated with pain, nausea, and vomiting (Felter & Lloyd, 1898).

The Eclectics of North America spoke of wild yam’s efficacy specifically for spasmodic irritation of the mucous membranes (Felter & Lloyd, 1898), whereas the Physiomedicalists believed that this soothing and antispasmodic action was due largely to the herb’s influence over the nervous system (Cook, 1869). Modern herbalists have combined these two perspectives, stating that in general, and with regard to all body systems, Dioscorea seems best suited to irritable and excitable conditions of the mucous membranes, and less efficient in conditions due to lack of tone (McIntyre, 1994). This would suggest that the herb’s overall action is as the Physiomedicalists perceived—that its main influence is that of relaxing smooth muscles, tissues, and nerves as opposed to toning those structures. In modern herbalism, not only is this considered the case with regard to the ovo-uterine system, but also holds great efficacy in the support of similar tissue states of the gastrointestinal system, respiratory, urinary, and musculoskeletal systems.

Wild yam’s anti-inflammatory action is also appreciated with regard to the gastrointestinal system (as employed in cases of irritable bowel syndrome and inflammatory bowel disease), as well as the musculoskeletal system, for which it is considered specific (Menzies-Trull, 2013). It is the combination of the herb’s anti-inflammatory and antispasmodic actions that make it useful in addressing pain and inflammation in both the muscles and joints (Chevallier, 2001). It is employed in cases of generalized myalgia—or inflammation of the muscle fibers, their coverings, or the tendons—as well as for rheumatoid arthritis (Mills, 1991). For all above mentioned body systems, Dioscorea is called upon for excessive heat, inflammation, spasm, and tension of the nerves, smooth muscles, and mucosa.

Modern pharmacological research had originally brought wild yam to the forefront as a hormone regulator as a result of the discovery of the steroidal saponin diosgenin and its ability to be pharmaceutically synthesized into progesterone and DHEA. This finding not only assisted in the creation of the first contraceptive pill (Huxtable, 1996), but it also initiated the belief that Dioscorea would have an effect on the balance of sex and steroidal hormones, specifically progesterone and the hormone precursor DHEA (Mills, 1991). Although diosgenin appears to have progesterogenic, androgenic, and corticosteroidal effects in vivo, there is negligible research validating that this conversion takes place in human beings (Brinker, 2008). This is certainly the case in transdermal absorption, as the commonly cited double-blind, placebo-controlled, cross-over study on the effects of a wild yam cream in 23 healthy individuals with menopausal symptoms found the treatment free of side effects, but with little effect on blood chemistry or menopausal symptoms (Komesaroff et al., 2001).

As with any herb or supplement, caution is always advised, especially during pregnancy. However, there is a long tradition of using wild yam to quell irritability of the stomach during gestation (Kost, 1858), including nausea (McIntyre, 1994) and vomiting (Cook, 1869). It has been used for any kind of cramping during pregnancy, including painful knotting of the uterus and false labor pains (Cook, 1869), particularly when related to stress and tension (McIntyre, 1994). Wild yam has also been indicated in threatened miscarriage (Bartram, 1998; Brinker, 2008).

The following is an overview of modern herbal indications for wild yam root by body system. In the gastrointestinal system, wild yam is thought to be supportive in cases of bilious colic, gastrointestinal irritation, chronic gastritis in alcoholism, indigestion with hepatic involvement, chronic hepatic congestion, spasmodic hiccough, painful vomiting, spasm of the bowel, and yellowing of the skin and conjunctiva with nausea and colicky pains (Felter & Lloyd, 1898). It is also regarded as useful for sudden spasms of griping pain in the stomach and bowels (Ellingwood, 1915), inflammation of the gallbladder, irritable bowel syndrome, diverticulitis (Chevallier, 2001), inflammatory bowel disease (McIntyre, 1994), constipation as a result of visceral tension, and for restoring rhythm in nervous bowel conditions (Mills, 1991).

With regard to general nervous system support, wild yam has been indicated for irritable conditions especially when associated with pain or spasm (Felter & Lloyd, 1898) as well as in adrenal exhaustion (Bartram, 1998; Menzies-Trull, 2013). In terms of reproductive health, wild yam has been indicated in cases of dysmenorrhea if the result of spasmodic irritation of the mucous membranes (Felter & Lloyd, 1898), ovarian neuralgia (Ellingwood, 1915), and general cramping and pain of the ovo-uterine organs, especially when associated with stress and tension (McIntyre, 1994). It has also found use to assist in pelvic decongestion in cases of endometriosis (Rogers, 1998). With regard to the urinary system, wild yam has been used for pain in the bladder (Cook, 1869) as it is also thought to be soothing to spasmodic conditions of the entire urinary tract (McIntyre, 1994). We also see wild yam suggested in cases of arthritis and rheumatism (Chevallier, 2001), myalgia (Mills, 1991), Buerger’s disease (Mills, 1991), high blood pressure, and intermittent claudication caused by poor arterial blood flow to the legs (Menzies-Trull, 2013).

Adult Dose

Tincture: 10 mL (1:5, 25%) daily (Menzies-Trull, 2013).

Fluid Extract: 4 mL (1:1, 45%) daily (Cech, 2000).

Decoction: 2-4 fl oz (½ ounce dried rhizome per 16 fl oz boiling water) 2x/day (Cech, 2000).

Safety

Use caution in pregnancy (Bartram, 1998). Use only the dried rhizome; ingestion of the fresh rhizome may cause gastric irritation, nausea, vomiting, and/or other undesirable effects (Cech, 2002; Foster & Duke, 2014). Overdose can also cause nausea and vomiting. Wild yam may have an additive estrogenic effect when administered with estrogen-containing drugs (“Wild yam,” 2007).
