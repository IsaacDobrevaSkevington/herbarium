Rue

Ruta graveolens

Native To

Ruta graveolens is native to southern Europe (Missouri Botanical Garden [MBG], n.d.), including the Balkans (North Carolina State Extension [NCSE], n.d.).

Geographic Distribution

Rue is cultivated throughout the world and in some cases, such as in the northeastern U.S., it has escaped gardens and become naturalized (MBG, n.d.). It has also naturalized in other regions of North America, as well as parts of Europe, Africa, Ecuador, and Vietnam (Royal Botanic Gardens of Kew, n.d.). Rue is often found in disturbed areas, meadows, and fields (Native Plant Trust, n.d.).

Botanical Description

Ruta graveolens is an evergreen perennial herb which looks like a shrub and grows 2-3 feet tall (NCSE, n.d.) and spreads 2-3 feet wide (MBG, n.d.). Rue is woody at the base and has smooth, hairless stems; the alternate, fern-like leaves are bluish green in color, compound, and pinnately divided, and grow 3-5 inches long (MBG, n.d.; NCSE, n.d.). The leaves are smooth and covered with oil glands (Dugan, 2009) and give off a pungent smell when bruised (MBG, n.d.). The flowers are yellow, small, contain 4-5 petals, and grow in clusters which bloom above the foliage (MBG, n.d.). The fruit is a small brown capsule, which when split contains several dark-colored seeds; rue self seeds in the wild, and can be propagated through division, seeding, or stem cutting (NCSE, n.d.).

Key Constituents

Volatile oils including 2-undecanone (30.73%), 2-nonanone (18.06%), 2-nonyl acetate (11.03%), psoralen (1.28%), bergapten, and xanthotoxin; flavonoids including quercetin (Khare, 2007) and rutin (Hoffmann, 2003); coumarins including daphnoretin, isoimperatorin, naphthoherniarin, psoralen, pangelin, rutamarin, rutarin, scopoletin, and umbelliferone (Khare, 2007); alkaloids including arborinine, fagarine, and graveoline (Hoffmann, 2003; Mabey et al., 1988); and in the root, lignans including savinin and helioxanthin (Hoffmann, 2003).

Sustainability

There are no reported sustainability issues regarding Ruta graveolens.

Harvesting Guidelines

Caution should be taken while harvesting the plant, as some individuals are sensitive to the volatile oil and can develop skin irritation from handling the fresh plant; gloves can be used to protect against this (Hartung, 2011). The aerial parts of Ruta graveolens are harvested from mid- to late summer, by clipping the upper half of the herb (Hartung, 2011). The tops of young shoots are considered the most powerful when it comes to herbal use (Grieve, 1931/1971). It is recommended to harvest the leaves before the flowers form (Balick, 2014). Rue is hardy to USDA growing zones 4-8 and prefers to grow in areas of dry to medium moisture with well-drained soils and full sun; once established, rue is considered drought tolerant (MBG, n.d.).

Uses

The genus name, Ruta, is derived from the Greek word reuo, meaning “to set free,” which it was named in ancient times for its reputed ability to set people free of disease (Hoffmann, 2003). In ancient Greece and during the Middle Ages in Europe, rue was used to ward off evil (Barceloux, 2008). Hippocrates mentions rue as an ingredient in mithridate, which was a Greek preparation for poisons of all types (as cited in Chillemi & Chillemi, 2015). Rue was traditionally used in church ceremonies in England, hence its common name “herb of grace” (van Wyk, 2014). In England in the 17th and 18th centuries, rue sprigs were hung in courtrooms to protect against “jail fever,” a typhus infection that was commonly spread amongst prisoners (Cumo, 2013). Rue was also one of the ingredients in the original recipe for “Vinegar of the Four Thieves,” a famous herbal blend that was thought to protect against plague (Cumo, 2013; Grieve, 1931/1971). Rue is commonly featured in Lithuanian and Ukrainian folklore and folk songs (van Wyk, 2014).

The Sanskrit name for rue is sarpadamstraa; it is cultivated throughout India, and the whole plant and essential oil are used in Ayurveda (Bhutya, 2011). Sarpadamstraa is considered to ease kapha and vata excess (Bhutya, 2011) and to increase pitta (Frawley & Lad, 1988).

Ruta graveolens and Ruta chalepensis are the two most common species of rue used in herbalism; these, along with other species, including R. corsica and R. montana, are used in traditional herbal practices (Barceloux, 2008). When it comes to herbal preparations, the dried and fresh aerial parts are used; decoctions and infusions have traditionally been made using the fresh herb, as the fresh herb is considered more powerful due to its higher volatile oil content, though the dried herb is also used for tea (Grieve, 1931/1971).

Rue has culinary uses in several parts of the world. The leaves are used to flavor wine and olives (de Baïracli Levy, 1996). As author Ben-Erik van Wyk (2014) notes, the Italian drink grappa della ruta is bottled with a sprig of rue inside. Rue is grown in Ethiopia commercially as a spice. In eastern Europe rue is used to flavor meat. In Singapore the leaves are boiled with green beans to add flavor, and the herb is commonly added to soups in Cantonese cuisine. In Ethiopia, the leaves are used to flavor traditional black coffee (van Wyk, 2014).

Today, rue is most commonly used by herbalists as an emmenagogue; it helps to regulate the menstrual cycle, encouraging suppressed menses (Hoffmann, 2003), and is also used to ease congestion of the uterus (de Baïracli Levy, 1996). The constituents that are believed to have a stimulating action on the uterus, and thus are responsible for the plant’s emmenagogue action, include the alkaloids arborinine and fagarine as well as its volatile oil content (Mabey et al., 1988). Rue is specifically indicated when menstrual discomfort and cramps are worse prior to one’s period and improve once menses occurs (Wood, 2008). Rue is also used traditionally in Ayurveda for menstrual disorders (Barceloux, 2008). In Unani medicine, rue is considered a stimulant to the uterus and is used for amenorrhea and dysmenorrhea, and as a diuretic (Khare, 2004). The Eclectics also regarded rue as a urinary stimulant as well as a reproductive stimulant (Scudder, 1870). Felter and Lloyd (1898) mention using rue to improve atonic conditions of the gentio-urinary tract. Rue is indicated for urinary issues that involve straining and feelings of pressure on the bladder after urination, a frequent need to urinate, and involuntary urination during sleep or when moving during the day (Wood, 2008).

Historically, rue has been used as an abortifacient (McGuffin & Gardner, 2013). It is believed that its abortifacient action is due to an anti-implantation action, which in turn causes fetal death; the constituent chalepensin (a furanocoumarin) may be an active component that plays a role in this action (Ravindran, 2017). It’s important to understand that using rue (or any plant) as an abortifacient is extremely dangerous due to the large doses needed for this effect and is strongly discouraged. A review of cases by Ciganda and Laborde (2003) in which herbal infusions of rue (Ruta graveolens or Ruta chalepensis) were used to induce abortion looked at 26 cases between 1986 and 1999 which were reported to the Poison Centre in Montevideo, Uruguay. The researchers found that the use of rue as an abortifacient is linked with several cases of multiple organ system failure and death (Ciganda & Laborde, 2003), indicating that this use is unsafe and should not be attempted.

Another common use for rue among herbalists today is as an antispasmodic (Hoffmann, 2003). It is believed that the following constituents play a role in rue’s antispasmodic actions: bergapten, xanthotoxin, and the volatile oils (Khare, 2007). Rue is regarded as having a relaxing action on smooth muscles, particularly those of the digestive system, where it can be helpful in cases of bowel tension (Hoffmann, 2003).

Rue is also indicated for conditions of irritation of the digestive mucosa and for constipation when the stool presents as dry, hard, and scanty (Wood, 2008). The warming energetics of rue help it facilitate movement in the body, making it useful for breaking down food and phlegm, and for conditions of chronic stagnation (White Rabbit Institute of Healing, n.d.). In Chinese medicine, rue affects the Liver and Spleen meridians; herbs that are stimulating to the Liver meridian help to release bile and ease hypertension, and herbs that are stimulating to the Spleen meridian aid in the process of digestion and assimilation (Tierra, 1988). The bitter flavor of rue is attributed to its high rutin content (van Wyk, 2014). Rue tea has been used to help improve one’s appetite (Cumo, 2013).

Rue is considered a vermifuge (or anthelmintic), having a traditional use as such by the Eclectics (Felter & Lloyd, 1898); with this action, rue is sometimes used internally for parasitic worms or bacteria that are present in the digestive tract (Bellebuono, 2017).

The antispasmodic action of rue also makes it helpful as an antitussive, and it is specifically indicated for spasmodic coughs (Hoffmann, 2003), coughs with copious, thick, yellow mucus, feelings of weakness in the chest, pain on the sternum, and shortness of breath associated with tightness in the chest (Boericke, 1901). For cases of sore throat, an infusion of the leaves can be gargled (Tierra, 1988). For chronic bronchitis, a decoction of the leaves can be used as a compress applied to the chest (Grieve, 1931/1971).

Rue is regarded for increasing peripheral circulation, which has the effect of lowering blood pressure (Hoffmann, 2003). The herb is considered to have a strengthening effect on the capillaries and blood vessels (Tierra, 1988). Rue is specifically indicated for venous congestion that presents as reddening of the blood vessels in the eyes and reddening of the neck when one experiences feelings of embarrassment (Wood, 2008). In Taiwan, rue has a traditional use for heart palpitations and circulatory disorders (Barceloux, 2008). In Ayurveda, rue has been traditionally used for syncope (fainting caused by low blood pressure) (Barceloux, 2008).

When it comes to the musculoskeletal system, rue is indicated for arthritis, particularly when pain is associated with damp, cold weather, when pain is worse when one is not moving, and when pain is intensified with touch or bending at the joint (Wood, 2008). In Eclectic medicine, rue was noted as having a direct influence on the nervous system; it was used for soothing pain and regarded as improving nutrition of the nerve centers (Scudder, 1870). Rue is specifically indicated for general pain throughout the body, in which one feels bruised (Boericke, 1901). In Chinese medicine, rue is used for rheumatic pain, and to increase circulation and reduce swelling (Tierra, 1990). In Ayurveda, rue is also used for rheumatism as well as neuralgia (Barceloux, 2008). Rue also has a traditional use for rheumatism and pain in Saudi Arabia, where a decoction is used for such conditions (Barceoux, 2008). The anti-inflammatory action of rue is attributed to the constituent rutin (Khare, 2007). Rue is regarded as helpful in cases of pulled tendons and muscle sprains and strains, and it has an affinity for the flexor tendons (Wood, 2008). For such instances, rue can be applied topically as a tincture or infused oil (Tierra, 1990). Rue is also indicated for conditions in which there is trauma to the periosteum and nerves, which present with hard swellings and bruising (Wood, 2008). Juliette de Baïracli Levy (1996) mentions that the constituent rutin is considered strengthening to the blood vessels, nerves, and glands, and can help harden the teeth, bones, and nails.

Rue has an affinity for the eyes and is indicated for eye conditions in which the eyes present as watery and sensitive, and can be used for cases in which one’s eyes feel strained from tedious work, working in dim lighting, or excessive computer use (Wood, 2008). Rue has an ancient history of use for the eyes, as 1st-century Roman scholar Pliny mentions that rue strengthens eyesight and that painters and sculptures would ingest the herb to improve their sight (as cited in Chillemi & Chillemi, 2015). The effects on eye conditions are attributed to the constituent rutin, as it is strengthening to the capillaries (Chillemi & Chillemi, 2015). Herbalist Michael Tierra (1988) suggests preserving the fresh juice of rue leaves with honey to be used on the eyes, using 1 drop of the rue honey externally on each eyelid, two to three times daily. It is important to never use undiluted rue in the eyes (Smith, 2011), and due to the contact dermatitis rue can cause it is recommended to always test somewhere less sensitive on the body, such as the inner arm, before applying to the eyes. When using an eye wash with rue, always start with a small amount (Smith, 2011).

Rue can be used topically as a dried powder or lotion for skin parasites and infections, such as lice and ringworm (de Baïracli Levy, 1996). Rue is traditionally prepared as a wash and applied topically for body lice in Mexico (Torres, 2006). Traditionally, a decoction of the roots of rue was used for snake and scorpion bites in Chinese medicine (Barceloux, 2008).

Rue also has a traditional use for toothaches and earaches, with the crushed leaves being applied directly to help ease pain (van Wyk & Wink, 2017). In traditional Mexican herbalism, rue is wrapped in cotton and placed in the ear to soothe earache (Torres, 2006). One can also make an oil for earaches by macerating rue in olive oil to be used as ear drops (Tierra, 1988).

Matthew Wood (2008) describes the personality of someone who would benefit from rue as one who appears calm yet suppresses anger and feelings of helplessness, is often contradictory and suspicious, and experiences depression and anxiety. In Unani medicine, rue is considered a stimulant to the nervous system and is used for hysteria and nervous disorders (Khare, 2004). Herbalist David Hoffmann (2003) suggests chewing the fresh leaves to help ease tension headaches, heart palpitations, and general feelings of anxiety. In South America, rue is used for migraines (Cumo, 2013).

Rue is considered to have a diaphoretic action and as such is used to induce sweat to break fevers (Chillemi & Chillemi, 2015). Decoctions of the aerial parts of the herb have a traditional use for fever in Saudi Arabia, and rue tea is used for fever in Africa (Barceloux, 2008). British herbalist and author of The Family Herbal John Hill (1812) wrote about how the tea is helpful for fevers as well as headaches and nervous disorders which accompany fever.

When it comes to scientific research, there are no human studies regarding use of the whole plant, but there have been case studies regarding rue’s use and some research on the plant’s active constituents. One review article looked at the neuroactive compounds that rue contains, paying particular attention to the constituent rutin, and research related to rue’s effects on neuronal cells; the researchers suggested that rue may have neuroprotective effects and applications for neurodegenerative diseases that warrant further study (Colucci-D’Amato & Cimaglia, 2020). An in vitro study by Al Qaisi et al. (2022) looked at the effects of a Ruta graveolens methanolic extract against protoscolices (larvae of parasitic tapeworms) and isolated bacterial strains from hydatid cysts caused by tapeworm larvae using disc diffusion methods. The results showed that after 40 minutes of exposure, the rue extract (40 mg/mL) was 10 times more effective against the larvae than the conventional medication under the same duration of time. Of the three herbs tested, rue had the lowest minimum inhibitory concentration values against the pathogenic bacteria, indicating that it was an effective antibacterial against these pathogens (Al Qaisi et al., 2022).

It is important not to take rue in excess. In one case study discussed by Seak and Lin (2007), a 78-year-old person made a decoction of rue with 50 grams of the fresh herb in 1000 mL of water, boiled down to 250 mL; they ingested this preparation two times a day for 3 days total and experienced the following adverse side effects: dyspnea, dizziness, nausea, weakness, and decreased urination. The patient was hospitalized for a total of 5 days and presented with a pulse of 41, blood pressure of 78/50, atrial fibrillation, an abnormal electrocardiogram, hyperkalemia, and acute liver failure. It was noted that the patient had a 5-year history of cardiomyopathy, with a normal pulse of 78. When the case was reviewed in detail, the possibility of an herb-drug interaction was taken into consideration, yet it was strongly believed that the high dosage of rue was likely the cause (Seak & Lin, 2007).

Rue is a good plant for attracting pollinators, serving as a host plant to several types of butterflies, including the black swallowtail, anise swallowtail, and giant swallowtail (NCSE, n.d.). The aromatic nature of rue makes it helpful for deterring other insects, such as fleas (Calder, n.d.). As such, rue is often used as a companion plant, being planted between other plants to protect them from pests (Cumo, 2013); it is recommended to plant where aphids, whiteflies, and thrips are an issue (Hartung, 2011).

Adult Dose

Infusion: 8 fl oz (1-2 tsp dried herb in 1 cup water) 3x/day (Hoffmann, 2003).

Tincture: 1-4 mL (1:5, 40%) 3x/day (Hoffmann, 2003).

Safety

Rue contains volatile oils which are strong abortifacients, and as such should not be used when pregnant (Hoffmann, 2003). There is no data regarding use of rue while nursing (McGuffin & Gardner, 2013). Rue can cause phototoxicity; this is attributed to the herb’s furocoumarin content (Khare, 2007). For this reason, it is recommended to avoid excessive sun exposure after ingestion or topical use of rue (McGuffin & Gardner, 2013). It is also recommended to wear protective clothing when handling the fresh plant to avoid direct contact with the skin (van Wyk, 2014). There are no known drug or supplement interactions, but larger doses of rue have been associated with the following adverse side effects: depression, drowsiness, sleep disorders, dizziness, cramps, and gastrointestinal irritation (McGuffin & Gardner, 2013).

There have been cases in which people using rue as an abortifacient (doses are not mentioned) have experienced the following adverse effects: vomiting, abdominal pain, genital hemorrhage, anemia, jaundice, liver enlargement, elevated liver enzyme levels, depression of the central nervous system, and respiratory issues (Ciganda & Lorde, 2003). There were also several cases of multiple organ system failure and death among those attempting to use rue as an abortifacient (Ciganda & Lorde, 2003). It’s important to note that the use of rue (or any plant) as an abortifacient is extremely dangerous and as such is strongly discouraged.
