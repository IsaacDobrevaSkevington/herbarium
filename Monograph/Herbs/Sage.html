Sage

Salvia officinalis

Native To

Southeastern Europe, including areas of Spain, Italy, France, Albania, Bosnia and Herzegovina, Croatia, Serbia, Slovenia, Macedonia, and coastal areas of northwestern Africa, including Morocco and Algeria (Allen, 2014; Mars, 2007).

Geographic Distribution

From its native regions, sage was introduced to most of Europe, being cultivated in Austria, Germany, Switzerland, Belgium, the United Kingdom, Denmark, Norway, Poland, Sweden, Romania, Slovakia, Turkey, and the Ukraine (Allen, 2014). From these areas it has migrated with the human population, eventually becoming cultivated throughout North America and in some areas of the Middle East (Allen, 2014).

Botanical Description

Garden sage is a shrubby perennial, with the square stem typical of the Lamiaceae family. Its grayish-green (or purplish) leaves are between 1.5-2 inches long and deeply veined, slightly hairy, wrinkled, and growing in pairs. Its bluish-purple flowers grow in whorls set on racemes; each flower produces four smooth, round seeds. S. officinalis grows to a height of 1-3 feet. It is not an especially finicky plant; although it is often found cultivated in gardens and loves rich, well-drained soil and full sun, the herb can be found growing in dry meadows and rocky steppes as well. It also thrives in a range of climates—including colder climates, unlike most other sage varieties (Allen, 2014; Culpeper, 1653/2006; Grieve, 1931/1971; Mars, 2003).

Key Constituents

Beta-carotene, vitamins B1, B2, B3, and C, calcium, iron, magnesium, volatile oils (thujone, borneol, cineol, camphor, pinene, salvene), bitter principle (picrosalvine), flavonoids, tannin, phenolic acids (rosmarinic, caffeic, labiatic), phytoestrogens, resin (Mars, 2007).

 

Sustainability

There are no known sustainability issues or indications that S. officinalis faces environmental risks, and populations in its natural range are considered to be stable (Allen, 2014).

 

Harvesting Guidelines

S. officinalis can be started from seeds or cuttings. Seeds should be planted at a depth of ½ inch and will germinate in about three weeks (Beale & Boswell, 1991; Reppert, 1976). Nineteenth-century Shakers recommended that sage be sown in rich, well-drained soil in rows spaced 2 feet apart and thinned as needed, with new seed sown every 3-4 years (Beale & Boswell, 1991). In colder climates, garden sage may be kept over the winter by covering with straw or mulch.

The longest stems may be harvested from June onward (in temperate climates) and the leaves may be harvested year round. Once harvested, hang stems to dry or dry the leaves and preserve whole until needed (Reppert, 1976). Store in dark containers and protect from heat and light.

Uses

Because the earth knows
The scent of history,
It gave the people sage.
~Faty Joudah, from The Tea and Sage Poem

Although there are approximately 500 varieties of sage, Salvia officinalis is the variety that has been historically used in Western herbalism as the primary sage of commerce. Its genus name is a derivative of the Latin salvus, meaning “healthy”; this is appropriate when considering the varied uses of the herb to promote wellness in the nervous, digestive, integumentary, reproductive, and immune systems (Mars, 2007). Used for ages as a common culinary herb, it serves more purpose than to flavor and preserve foods such as meats and cheeses. Its bitterness also serves to stimulate digestive secretions, and as a choleretic, it increases the flow of bile to improve the digestion of fats (Mars, 2007). Sage is also used as a carminative to ease gas (Mars, 2007).

Sage is, as herbalist Matthew Wood (2004) notes, “an herb of remarkable range and depth” (p. 220). The specific indications for its use are myriad, as are the issues it helps to balance. These include “anxiety, blood clots, colds, cystitis, depression, diarrhea, dyspepsia, fever, flatulence, flu, hot flashes, indigestion, irregular menses, memory problems, menopause symptoms, menorrhagia, migraines, night sweats, perspiration (excessive), respiratory congestion, rheumatic pain, and staphylococcus infection” (Mars, 2007, p. 265).

In folk herbal traditions, sage has historically been used as an herb that promotes long life and increased wisdom (think of the “wise sage”), and also to attract protection and prosperity (Mars, 2007). In the 16th century, John Gerard—in line with Greco-Roman tradition—noted sage’s affinity for the head and the brain: “Sage is singularly good for the head and brain, it quickeneth the senses and memory, strengtheneth the sinews, restoreth health to those that have the palsy, and taketh away shakey trembling of the members” (as cited in Grieve, 1931/1971, p. 701).

Modern research on sage’s effectiveness as a nootropic, cognition-enhancing herb—particularly for memory and in imbalances and degenerative diseases such as Alzheimer’s—bears out this traditional wisdom. Compounds in sage act on the muscarinic and nicotinic cholinergic systems that are involved in cognition and memory processes (Miroddi et al., 2014). Herbalist James Snow (2010) explains that because sage exhibits anti-cholinesterase activity (inhibiting the enzyme that breaks down acetylcholine), it is an important supportive herb for dementia and Alzheimer’s disease. For example, in one study on patients with mild to moderate Alzheimer’s disease, researchers found that 60 drops per day of sage extract (1:1, 45%) taken for only 4 months produced significant improvements in cognition, and possibly reduced agitation (Akhondzadeh et al., 2003).

In addition to its anti-cholinesterase activity, other factors may contribute to sage’s ability to support cognitive health. As a powerful herb that protects brain tissue, sage has been shown in vitro to inhibit lipid peroxidation—a biomarker of oxidative stress and damage (Oboh & Henle, 2009). It also acts as an antioxidant that promotes the scavenging of free radicals, indicating its potential usefulness in managing and preventing Alzheimer’s disease and other degenerative diseases associated with oxidative damage (Oboh & Henle, 2009). This same research shows similar protective action in the liver (Oboh & Henle, 2009).

Wood (2004) also notes that sage is well-suited to support atrophic conditions because, like other herbs containing fixed oils, it increases digestion and helps to metabolize fats and oils by encouraging the release of bile. This may mean that in addition to directly impacting the digestive process, sage could offset the atrophy that is a hallmark of Alzheimer’s by maintaining the integrity of fatty brain tissue. Sage’s affinity for the kidneys is evident in its ability to slow excessive urination (thereby reducing fluid loss) and assist in the reabsorption of blood from the kidneys back into the bloodstream (Wood, 2004). Urine of low specific gravity, which appears clear, is one of sage’s specific indications. Interestingly, enhancing fluid retention supports cognition as well: “By preserving internal fluids, sage prevents nervous irritability and debility (from lack of fluids to sedate and calm the nervous system)” (Wood, 2004, p. 221). Some herbalists use sage as a nervine; as clinical herbalist Katja Swift (n.d.) says, “Sage is the perfect nervine for over-loaded, overwhelmed Type-A personalities” (para. 3).

Sage is helpful in mild imbalances of cognition and mood, such as simple mental fatigue and lethargy. It is helpful for lifting a “dull and heavy spirit,” (Wood, 2004, p. 226) as evidenced by the old French practice of using sage in times of grief and loss (Grieve, 1931/1971). Modern studies have found that a single dose of sage extract or dried leaf is capable of increasing memory capacity and improving mood (Miroddi et al., 2014), but it needn’t always be taken internally. As herbalist Brigitte Mars (2007) explains, “Even just the aroma of sage helps promote mental alertness” (p. 265).

A feature noted by traditional and modern herbalists is sage’s ability to regulate the flow of fluids within the body, which manifests in many ways. As Wood (2004) explains, sage’s oily, astringent, warming qualities allow it to both build fluids and restore lack of fluids. Furthermore, this versatile plant helps ease the excess loss of fluids, as can occur with excessive urination, diarrhea, menstruation, and drenching sweats associated with menopause and tuberculosis (Wood, 2004). Sage is also a circulatory stimulant used to move blood to cold peripheral limbs, improve cardiovascular health, and reduce stagnation of blood; it may even dissolve blood clots, reducing risk of stroke and ischemic injury (Mars, 2007; Wood, 2004).

Sage has also been used traditionally to subdue cold symptoms. It has antimicrobial and anti-inflammatory actions, making it helpful for soothing sore and inflamed throats and tonsillitis. Sage’s tannin content tightens tissues, making those tissues more impervious to pathogens and infection. As an expectorant, sage also helps to thin and expel phlegm from the lungs and dry up postnasal drip and runny sinuses. In febrile conditions, sage regulates the flow of fluids, operating as either a diaphoretic that promotes sweating or an antisudorific that it inhibits excess sweating and loss of fluid (Culpeper, 1653/2006; Grieve, 1931/1971; Mars, 2007; Wood, 2004).

Sage exercises similar fluid-regulating actions in the ovo-uterine system. While it has historically been used as an emmenagogue (Grieve, 1931/1971; Romm, 2010), it is also used as an astringent, drying up breast milk, easing leucorrhea, and slowing heavy menstrual bleeding. Its complex actions on the endocrine system may be mitigated by its relationship to fats, as lipids are the primary transport for the fat-soluble hormones affecting the gonads, thyroid, and adrenal glands. This may explain why sage has such differing effects on younger versus older individuals, and how it can offer such specific and multi-targeted support to those experiencing hormone-related changes associated with perimenopause and menopause, including changes in libido, vaginal dryness, hot flashes and night sweats, as well as mental and emotional imbalances, such as cognitive ‘fogginess’ and depression (Romm, 2010; Wood, 2004).

This may also explain sage’s reputation as a tonic appropriate for the elder years, when the drying and atrophy of skin, muscles and tendons, and loss of fat is commonplace. Sage helps to build tissue, moisten and rebuild dry skin and connective tissue, ease arthritis and joint pain (specifically that which worsens in cold, damp weather), and is also a well-known ally for hair loss and graying (Wood, 2004). Sage serves as a tonic for relaxed, atrophied, and depressed tissues (Wood, 2004). Herbalist James Snow (2010) suggests using sage to tone flaccid tissue throughout the body; tighten skin, varicosities, and relaxed throat tissue (found in snoring and sleep apnea); and support atonic digestive mucosa where there is a lack of digestive secretions contributing to indigestion and difficulty digesting rich foods.

Sage also has longstanding traditional use in supporting oral health. Herbalist Maud Grieve (1931/1971) writes that sage is most valued as a “wash for the cure of affections of the mouth and as a gargle in inflamed sore throat” (p. 703), and its many indications include sore, bleeding, inflamed, or receding gums (Wood, 2004). Modern research confirms traditional wisdom in this case as well; one recent trial found that a mouthwash preparation containing 5% sage extract (in which 50 grams of leaves were extracted into 1500 mL of a 50% ETOH solvent) significantly reduced bacterial colony count (Beheshti-Rouy et al., 2015).

Topically, sage also supports the skin when prepared as a compress, wash, or bath. A cream-based preparation including sage extract has been shown to be effective in calming the symptoms of genital herpes outbreaks (Romm, 2010). It soothes eczema, psoriasis and other chronic skin eruptions, as well as the acute discomfort of insect bites and poison ivy or poison oak exposure. Sage can be employed as a facial steam to balance oily complexions, as a douche to address leucorrhea, or as a mouthwash (Mars, 2007).

 

Adult Dose

Infusion: 8 fl oz (1-2 teaspoons dried herb in 1 cup boiling water) 3x/day (Hoffmann, 2003). For menopausal night sweats and hot flashes: 1-2 cups (8-16 fl oz) during the day, with another cup taken 1-2 hours before bedtime (Romm, 2010).

Tincture: 2-4 mL (1:5, 40%) 3x/day (Hoffmann, 2003).

 

Safety

Sage is generally considered to be safe, and side effects are rare. However, it does contain thujone, which in very high doses may cause convulsions (Duke, 1997) and can also adversely affect those with epilepsy, high blood pressure, and kidney disease (Mars, 2007). Because it is an antigalactagogue, sage should be avoided during breastfeeding. Large doses of sage should also be avoided in pregnancy; there is at least one known case in which a strong infusion has been connected to miscarriage (Romm, 2010).
