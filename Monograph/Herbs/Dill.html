Dill

Anethum graveolens

Native To

North Africa and the Middle East (Royal Botanic Gardens of Kew [RBGK], n.d.).

Geographic Distribution

Introduced into Southern, Eastern, and Southeast Europe, Benelux countries, the Baltic states, Russia, India, Mongolia, Southeast Asia, East Africa, Cameroon, Nigeria, Niger, Angola, Southeast Brazil, Northeast Argentina, Peru, Ecuador, Kentucky, New York, Illinois, and Louisiana (RBGK, n.d.). Cultivated widely throughout the world (World Health Organization [WHO], 2007).

Botanical Description

Dill is an annual aromatic herb that can grow up to 4 feet (1.2 meters) tall and is anchored in the soil with a taproot that can grow deep into the ground in ideal circumstances (Wright, 2010). The herb can be confused with wild fennel (Foeniculum vulgare), but dill has a hollow, erect stem, while fennel has multiple stems filled with pith. The alternate, bluish green leaves are feathery, delicate, and divided (Gupta, 2012). The small yellow hermaphroditic flowers appear in terminal umbels and bloom in the summer. Interestingly, even though the flowers have a strong aroma, they contain little nectar, which is not typical for this family (Gupta, 2012). The flowers develop into small, 0.1-0.2 inch (3-5 mm) long fruits, which are often referred to as seeds but botanically they are schizocarps, a dry fruit that splits into single parts. Dill seeds are oval, flattened, and have three longitudinal ridges on the back with five dark lines of oil cells (Jana & Shekhawat, 2010). The seeds turn brown when they mature (Whitten, 2004).

Key Constituents

Seeds: volatile oil (2-5%): d-carvone (40-60%), limonene (20-50%) (Dachler & Pelzman, 2017), dihydrocarvone, 1,8-cineole, p-cymene, α-phellandrene, α-pinene, α-terpinene (WHO, 2007), dillapiole (Gupta, 2012). Flavonoids: kaempferol-glucuronide (WHO, 2007), quercetin. Furanocoumarin, oxypeucedanin, falcarindiol (Stavri & Gibbons, 2005).

Aerial parts: volatile oil (0.3-0.4%): α-phellandrene (up to 50%), anethofuran (35%), limonene, d-carvone (Dachler & Pelzman, 2017), dillapiole (Gupta, 2012). Carbohydrates (36%), proteins, fatty oils, mineral elements, vitamins A and B3 (Kaur & Arora, 2010).

Sustainability

No known sustainability issues.

 

Harvesting Guidelines

The stem and leaves can be harvested in the early summer before or while the herb starts to flower (Whitten, 2004). Dried leaves lose most of their aroma and the herb is often used fresh. Dill seeds ripen unevenly, which is typical for many umbellifers, and are easily shed (Dachler & Pelzman, 2017). Whitten (2004) recommends harvesting when the first seeds turn brown to prevent major losses. The seeds can be harvested by placing a brown bag over the ripe seed heads and closing the opening with a piece of string (Wright, 2010). After cutting off the stem at the base, it can be hung in a warm and well-ventilated room, after 2 weeks the seeds are dry and can easily be separated manually. The optimal drying temperature to preserve the aromatic compounds is 95 degrees F (35 degrees C) (Whitten, 2004).

According to Janeen Wright (2010) of The Herb Society of America, the best time to harvest dill is in the early morning when the plants are turgid. This will ensure a better flavor of the herb and minimize the shattering of seeds (Wright, 2010).

Uses

Dill has a long history as a culinary and aromatic herb, and both the herb and the seeds are widely used for making pickles, confections, and various international signature dishes. The Papyrus Ebers, one of the oldest medical papyri of ancient Egypt and dated to c. 1550 BCE, noted that dill, or amnest, could be used for headaches (Marzell, 1938/2002), and the seeds were part of recipes to ease pain (Gupta, 2012). Modern herbalists value dill as a typical stomachic and aromatic, and suggest its use to stimulate digestion and metabolism in general (Mills & Bone, 2000).

Currently, the genus Anethum comprises only two recognized species, A. graveolens and A. theurkauffii; the latter is native to Mauritania. Dill has at least 18 synonyms including Anethum arvense, Angelica graveolens, Peucedanum graveolens, and Anethum sowa  (RBGK, n.d.). However, while East Indian dill (A. sowa) is often classified as a synonym, some authors do consider it to be a distinct species. Research has shown that A. sowa contains a high proportion of dillapiole (around 20%) and a lower carvone content (Gupta, 2012). This is likely because of the different climatic conditions; it is known that plant constituents respond and vary according to their environmental circumstances (Said-Al Ahl et al., 2015).

The common name, dill, is likely derived from the Norse word dilla meaning “to lull” and possibly refers to its uses to ease insomnia (Kiple & Ornelas, 2000) or soothe crying babies (Marzell, 1938/2002). The botanical genus name comes from the Greek word aneton, the name for dill. Unfortunately, the meaning is not clear. Some authors claim it refers to the ancient Greek custom of burning the strong smelling seeds (Barton & Castle, 1845), while others state that aneton comes from ano, “upward,” and theo, “I run,” referring to dill’s vigorous growth habit (Tucker & DeBaggio, 2009). The species name graveolens means “strong smelling” (Gledhill, 2002).

Note that the Ancient Greek word for anise (Pimpinella anisum) is aneson, and there have been mistranslations in historical literature where the names for dill and anise have been used interchangeably, and it is sometimes unclear which herb is meant.

The name “meeting house seeds” can be traced back to early settlers who brought dill to North America. The story goes that dill seeds were given to children to chew during long church sermons to suppress hunger and keep them still (McVicar, 2009). There are a few variations of this story, but it is not clear how common this use really was.

Evidence has shown that dill has been used at least since 3000 BCE to flavor dishes and was cultivated for this purpose (Kiple & Ornelas, 2000). Dill was widely represented in Roman food culture and particularly associated with the military; seeds were commonly found on ancient military sites (van der Veen et al., 2008). The ancient Romans believed that the herb had strong invigorating qualities, and it was commonly given to gladiators before a combat game (Kiple & Ornelas, 2000).

In magical practices, dill has been associated with protective rituals. In rural areas of Germany, Belgium, the Netherlands, and most likely in other countries too, dill was used to guard against ill-intended spirits and witches who were believed to curse livestock and interfere with marriages (Marzell, 1938/2002). There are many accounts of rituals involving dill, which were still practiced until at least the early 20th century. For example, salt and dill were sprinkled onto newborn calves to protect them against curses, and if hens did not lay eggs, dill was scattered in the hen pen.

Dill was not only used as a protection charm, but amusingly, some brides would carry dill and mustard seeds to their wedding and mutter throughout the ceremony, “I have you, mustard and dill. Husband, when I speak, you stay still” (Marzell, 1938/2002, p. 169). Another interesting use was to place a combination of dill and oat straw in the shoes, which was supposed to ensure that the wearer would receive a fair trial and judgment in court. There are recordings of these uses dating back to the German Hildesheim witch trials in 1513 (Marzell, 1938/2002).

In the ayurvedic tradition, dill seeds are used to support digestion and ease abdominal discomfort, colic, and uterine pains (Jana & Shekhawat, 2010), similar to the applications in traditional Western herbalism. The Compendium of Charaka (c. 100 BCE – 200 CE), one of two foundational texts on Ayurveda, describes a paste made with linseed (Linum usitatissimum), castor (Ricinus communis) seed, and dill seed pounded together with milk and externally applied to reduce rheumatic pains and swellings of the joints. Saint Hildegard von Bingen (1098-1179 CE) suggested dill for rheumatic pains, even though she warned against using the fresh herb because she considered it to be unhealthy (as cited in Hertza & Strehlow, 2003).

The Kashyapa Samhita (600 BCE), one of the earliest treatises on Indian medicine, and often consulted in the fields of ayurvedic pediatrics, gynecology, and obstetrics, attributed tonic, rejuvenating, and intellect-promoting properties to dill (Jana & Shekhawat, 2010). In The Complete Herbal, originally published in 1643, Culpeper (1643/1995) places dill under the dominion of Mercury and says it therefore “strengthens the brain” (p. 89). In other herbal traditions, however, dill is used as a mild calmative (Zevin, 1997), to promote healthy sleep (Fuchs, 1543/2017; Uyldert, 1996), and as a central nervous system depressant (Ivanov et al., 1973).

Dill has commonly been employed as a carminative to help ease flatulence, especially in combination with abdominal cramps (Mills & Bone, 2000; van Hellemont, 2000). The carminative properties of dill seeds are mentioned in most well-known historical Western herbals. Dill is often an ingredient of gripe water used to ease colic and flatulence in infants. Pharmacological studies have shown the antispasmodic effects of dill seeds (Gharib Naseri & Heidari, 2007), but clinical trials regarding the efficacy of the above uses are lacking.

However, there is some evidence that supports the positive effect of dill on the gastrointestinal tract. In vitro studies of aqueous extracts of dill seeds have shown moderate activity against Helicobacter pylori, a bacterium that is an important contributor to gastric and duodenal ulcers (Rifat-uz-Zaman et al., 2006). In addition, hot water extracts (40 degrees C) of dill seeds have been found to inhibit different bacteria including Enterococcus faecalis, Staphylococcus aureus, Escherichia coli, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Shigella flexneri, which are important pathogens associated with various gastrointestinal disorders leading to indigestion and diarrhea (Kaur & Arora, 2009).

A small, randomized clinical trial involving 28 participants under the age of 1 year investigated the effects of dill leaves on the intestinal parasite Giardia lamblia compared with metronidazole, a commonly prescribed antibiotic (Sahib et al., 2014). The infants received 1 mL of an aqueous dill extract, three times per day over a period of 5 days. Stool samples were taken before the start of the treatment, after 5 days, and again after 14 days. The researchers found that the Giardia lamblia had significantly decreased after 5 days, with only two samples containing the parasite, as opposed to one in the antibiotic sample group. Without treatment, Giardia infections can last up to 6 weeks; however, in both study groups the infection had cleared completely after 14 days. In addition, the study showed that the infants tolerated the dill extract very well.

Dill seeds are also often employed for respiratory health, especially as an antitussive and to ease catarrh and bronchial congestion (Mills & Bone, 2000; van Hellemont, 2000). This may be due to the antimicrobial properties of dill (Kaur & Arora, 2009), but the exact mechanism is unknown and there are no clinical studies available to support these uses. Dill seed simple syrup can be used to enhance the taste of unpleasant herbal cough preparations, especially for children (van Hellemont, 2000).

Dill seeds are traditionally used as a galactagogue to improve and support milk supply in nursing mothers (Romm, 2017). This use goes back a long time and was recorded in De Materia Medica, the precursor to modern pharmacopeias written by 1st-century Greek physician Dioscorides (70/2000). Today, dill seeds are still used to support nursing mothers in many traditional practices around the world. However, The World Health Organization (2007) does not endorse this use because there is no safety data available.

Interestingly, dill was historically applied for a variety of conditions affecting menstrual and vaginal health. Dioscorides noted that an oil infusion of dill flowers can “sooth distress around the vulva and open it” (Dioscorides, 70/2000, p. 442), and Culpeper (1643/1995) suggested a white wine decoction of dill seeds to soothe pain and promote menstruation.

A small clinical pilot study seems to partly support Culpeper’s indication. Heidarifar et al. (2014) conducted double-blind, randomized research involving 75 women with primary dysmenorrhea, painful menstruation without pelvic disease. In the first group, the participants were orally given capsules containing 1000 mg of powdered dill seed every 12 hours, while the second group received 250 mg mefenamic acid, a prescription drug used for menstrual pain, and the third group received a placebo. The capsules were taken 2 days before the onset of menstruation and were taken for 5 days per cycle, while the researchers tracked the participants for 2 months. Interestingly, the research concluded that powdered dill seed could be as effective as mefenamic acid in decreasing the pain severity of primary dysmenorrhea. Of course, larger-scale research is necessary to draw more meaningful conclusions.

Leonhart Fuchs’ The New Herbal of 1543 (1543/2017), which is mainly based on Dioscorides’ work, expands on dill’s uses for vaginal and uterine health. The German physician writes that burned dill seeds can be used to resolve “ulcers in secret places,” (p. ix) most likely referring to sores in the genital areas (Fuchs, 1543/2001). Fuchs also indicates that dill seeds can help during the process of giving birth, while taking a bath after delivery using a dill seed infusion could help the uterus shrink back to its normal size.

Interestingly, in modern Iranian folk medicine, dill seed infusion is commonly applied to stimulate uterine contractions and speed up vaginal delivery. Two clinical trials support these uses.

Zagami et al. (2012) conducted a clinical pilot study to evaluate the effect of dill seed on contractions during the active phase of labor. Forty women received a dill seed infusion made with 1 tablespoon of seeds per dose, while 60 women in the control group received nothing. The researchers noted that the contractions were significantly more frequent in the women who received the dill extract, and concluded that it shortens the first stage of labor.

Another randomized clinical trial looked into the effect of a dill seed decoction on pain intensity and duration of labor stages in 153 women who had regular uterine contractions (Hekmatzadah et al., 2014). The supplement consisted of a decoction made of 10 g dill seeds in 100 cc water and was given to the intervention group after the cervix reached 3-4 cm dilation. Even though the supplement was given only once, the stages of labor were significantly shorter than in the control group, but no effect on pain intensity was observed.

Recently, dill has drawn interest for its potential positive effect on reducing cholesterol and other lipids in the blood. Mirhosseini et al. (2014) conducted a small randomized single-blind clinical study involving 91 patients with hyperlipidemia. One group received a dill tablet six times per day for the duration of 2 months. Unfortunately, the study does not report the exact dose or preparation method of the dill tablets. The blood lipids under investigation included total cholesterol, triglyceride, and high-density lipoprotein (HDL), which were measured at the beginning and the end of the trial. The researchers concluded that dill significantly reduced total cholesterol, even more than the conventional medication in the control group. However, dill tablets reduced triglycerides less than in the control group, while dill had no significant effect on HDL levels at all. The mode of action is not clear but the researchers suggest that the antioxidative properties of the volatile oil components could be responsible.

Apart from the multiple uses as a bioactive herb and for culinary purposes, dill can also be a useful ally in organic gardening and farming. The essential oil has well-documented insecticidal properties against the red flour beetle (Tribolium castaneum), and its use is non-toxic to mammals (Chaubey, 2007). A methanolic extract of dill seed could also be used as an eco-friendly fungicide to control some fungal pathogens such as Rhynchosporium secalis and Pyrenophora tritici-repentis (Zeyneb et al., 2019).

Adult Dose

Infusion: 1 cup (4-8 g seeds per liter water) 2-3x/day after meals (van Hellemont, 2000); 1 cup (1-2 tsp gently crushed seeds per 8 fl oz boiling water) before meals to promote digestion (Hoffmann, 2003).

Tincture: 2-4 mL (1:5, 25%) 3x/day (Hoffmann, 2003).

Safety

Dill is generally considered safe when used within the recommended dose (Gardner & McGuffin, 2013). However, allergic reactions such as contact dermatitis (Monteseirin et al., 2003) and photosensitivity (Ortan et al., 2013) have been reported. In addition, cytotoxic effects of dill seed and leaf essential oils were observed in human lymphocytes (Lazutka et al., 2001). Despite many traditional uses that indicate otherwise, The World Health Organization (2007) does not recommend the use of dill seeds during pregnancy and nursing because of dill’s use as a contraceptive and to induce labor. However, there is no safety data on the use of dill during pregnancy and lactation. Herbalist Aviva Romm (2017) states that nursing mothers who have an overactive milk ejection reflex should avoid any dill preparations. There is no information available regarding drug interactions.
