Kava

Piper methysticum

Botanical Description

A swamp-loving plant, kava best flourishes in the cooler highlands and wet forests up to 1000 feet above sea level, reaching a height of between six and twelve feet. It is coarsely branching and succulent, with alternating large, heart-shaped leaves, a spotted, dichotomous stem, and male (and occasionally female) inflorescences growing in spikes from the axil of the leaf. The rootstock is whitish to dark yellowish, depending on the amount of kavalactones present. It is composed of a dense, central pith surrounded by radiating vascular sections. (Bastyr University, n.d.; mcdonald, n.d.; tierra, n.d.; Grieve, 1971; Lebot et al., 1992).

Key Constituents

Kavalactones—primarily methysticin, dihydromethysticin, kavain, dihydrokavain, demethoxyyangonin and yangonin (LaPorte et al., 2002; Singh and Singh, 2002), alkaloids found in the leaves and stems including pipermethystine (Nerurkar et al., 2004), amino acids, minerals (aluminum, iron, magnesium, potassium, calcium and sodium), and flavokavains A, B and C (Singh and Singh, 2002; Xian-guo et al., 1997).

Sustainability

AT-RISK PLANT. SEEK CULTIVATED SOURCES OR ALTERNATIVE PLANTS.

Kava is on the United Plant Savers “At-Risk” list (United Plant Savers, 2022), with wild populations of the plant declining in Hawaii due largely to habitat destruction and commercial wildcrafting (Datta, 2000). Additionally, the plants do not reproduce sexually and depend upon humans for reproduction via root division or stem cuttings (United Plant Savers, n.d.). Please seek cultivated sources of kava or use other herbs in its place. Other herbs that can be used for their calming, sedative, and/or pain-easing properties include valerian (Valeriana officinalis) root, chamomile (Matricaria chamomilla) flower, hop (Humulus lupulus) strobile, and California poppy (Eschscholzia californica) whole plant (Bothwell, 2002).

Harvesting Guidelines

When cultivating kava, do not harvest plants before they 3 years old as they may suffer injury and die. Kava plants can better withstand harvesting at 3-5 years of age, which also gives the plants time to accumulate kavalactones. Carefully pull up roots, ensuring they do not break off, harvest a few lateral roots, then return the vertical roots back into the soil (“Kava Plants,” n.d.).

Uses

Piper methysticum is a slow-growing, perennial shrub native to the islands of the South Pacific, including Polynesia, the Sandwich Islands, New Guinea, Fiji, Hawai’i, and Vanuatu. The common name is derived from the proto-Polynesian awa or kawa, meaning “bitter,” but kava is known by hundreds of common names—maybe more—throughout the South Pacific Islands, including ‘awa in Hawai’i and yaqona in Fiji. Its botanical name, Piper methysticum, translates to “intoxicating pepper.”

Kava has an incredibly rich tradition throughout the island cultures of the Pacific, having as much of a major impact in those cultures as coffee or alcohol does in the west. It is surmised to have been first domesticated from a wild precursor about three thousand years ago in what is now northern Vanuatu (Lebot et al., 1993). Traditionally, it has been used as a medicine that supports connection and enhanced communication between individuals, between cultures, and even between humans and the divine. Kava is a social lubricant in the political arena—to this day, visiting dignitaries and heads of state are offered the traditional brew. It has been said of kava that “hate cannot exist” in its presence (mcdonald, n.d.), whether that manifests as angst between spouses or opposing sides of a community conflict. This same relaxing energy translates to the physiological, as used in traditional Western herbalism.

Historically speaking, kava is not only a beverage or medicine, but in and of itself a ritual, with its own symbolic meanings, protocols, and procedures for cultivation, harvesting, and consumption. In many cultures, its use marked virtually every major transitional event—from births, marriages, and deaths, to the beginning and end of political conflicts—as well as informal, social events. As a spiritual medicine, kava was used as an offering to the gods and a divination tool. Maly (2006) offers cultural historian Mary Pukui’s 1943 recording of an ancient chant which speaks to the reconciliatory power of kava:

‘Awa for the men,
And women of the heavens,
‘Awa for the men,
And for the women of the pit [volcano].
Thus it was, thus it is,
Silence the mouth,
Wash the hands.
Sacred is the taboo, sacred is the freeing,
The taboo is lifted and one can go,
The law is lifted by the gods.

Kava’s medicine is found in the outer layers of the rootstock (also called the “stump”), with the bark removed. While herbalists commonly refer to the “rhizome” as the medicinal part of the plant, this is botanically incorrect, as a rhizome is a horizonally-creeping, underground stem that gives rise to the leaves and roots (Lebot et al., 1992). Kava’s roots grow both vertically and horizontally—the laterally-growing roots have higher levels of kavalactones.

Nearly all of the pharmacological activity of kava is attributed to the kavalactones—primarily methysticin, dihydromethysticin, kavain, dihydrokavain, demethoxyyangonin and yangonin (LaPorte et al., 2002; Singh and Singh, 2002)–which are concentrated in the rootstock. The different ratios of kavalactones (also called kavapyrones) differ according to the specific strain of the plant, of which there are many. The distribution of kavalactones decreases in the aerial parts, where alkaloids are found in greater amounts.  Some alkaloids found in the leaves and stems are toxic, including pipermethystine (Nerurkar et al., 2004), giving rise to concern—and controversy—over its potential hepatotoxicity. Other constituents include amino acids, minerals (aluminium, iron, magnesium, potassium, calcium and sodium), and flavokavains A, B and C (Singh and Singh, 2002; Xian-guo et al., 1997).

Kavalactones are lipid-soluble, and only somewhat alcohol soluble; they are not soluble in water. However, the presence of saponins in the root allows for solubility of kavalactones in water-based preparations; their presence in water results in a solvent into which the constituents are readily extracted (Santich, n.d.). Perhaps the best solvent for kava—although not the most appetizing preparation in the West—is saliva (mcdonald, n.d.).

Kava is strong and penetrating in taste; as a member of the pepper family, it tastes appropriately pungent, but also bitter and aromatic (mdconald, n.d.; Grieve, 1971).  It also has a distinct tingly, then numbing effect due to the kavalactones kavain and dihydrokavain, which act as a local anesthetic.

In traditional Pacific cultures, kava was used as medicine for the body as well as the mind and spirit. Among the many physical imbalances it has been used to address are anxiety, insomnia, fatigue, genitourinary infection, asthma, obesity, headache, parasitic and fungal infections, and leprosy (Tierra, n.d.). Kava was introduced to the Western materia medica following Captain James Cook’s famous expeditions to the South Pacific in the 1770s, during which crew member George Forster documented kava’s cultivation and use among both indigenous people (and the effect on his own crewmates) (Cook, 1842). While the Physiomedicalists generally eschewed its use (mcdonald, n.d.), kava was a part of the Eclectic pharmacopoeia. Reflecting kava’s usefulness as a genitourinary remedy, the Eclectics employed the plant as an a pain reliever and antispasmodic for the urinary tract, and an astringent and tonic to be used for “weakness and laxity” of the tissues, particularly for nocturnal incontinence in the young and old (Felter and Lloyd, 1898). Conversely, some modern herbalists find it useful as a urethral relaxant.

King’s American Dispensatory (1898), while mildly implying disapproval of its use, nevertheless lists several specific indications for kava, primarily related to nerve pain and genitourinary concerns. These include neuralgia, toothache, earache, eye pain, dizziness and despondency, gonorrhea, painful urination, and dizziness and despondency. While this list mirrors neither traditional Pacific cultures’ uses nor modern, evidence-based herbal use, it does point to kava’s specific affinity for the nervous and genitourinary systems. Grieve (1971) offers as specific indications heart issues, gout, and other conditions that benefit from kava’s diuretic action. Kava is also an ally of the musculoskeletal system, particularly where it is affected by acute stress. Mcdonald (n.d.) writes: “It is most effective when your mind is overwhelmed and your body is tightly strung from a crazy hectic day and that’s what’s making you unable to relax.” Pain with restlessness and anxiety is another specific indication. Because kava lets down social barriers, social anxiety and even stage fright could be seen as specific indications.

Michael Tierra (n.d.) identifies kava’s most powerful therapeutic properties as being antispasmodic, anxiolytic, diuretic, carminative, and anodyne.  Kava also acts as a dose-dependent hypnotic and sedative, although mcdonald (n.d.) attributes its strong and immediate stress-reducing effect to relaxation, not sedation.

In modern herbalism, kava is best-known and well-studied as an herb that addresses acute stress responses, nervous and social anxiety, insomnia, and some causes of nausea. Sarris and Kavanagh (2009) report: “A Cochrane review has been undertaken of 11 RCTs of rigorous methodology using kava monopreparations (60 mg–280mg of kavalactones) in generalized anxiety. Results revealed statistically significant anxiolytic activity of kava compared with placebo in all but one trial.”  Many other studies have shown it to be notably relaxing without causing negative cognitive effects or physiological dependency, as can develop with chronic alcohol or pharmaceutical anxiolytic use (Gendle et al., 2011; Singh, 1992), making it an effective treatment for a variety of anxiety-related disorders. In fact, kava is a useful adjunctive therapy for people who are benzodiazepine-dependent. However, caution may still be advised for those who tend toward addiction, as kava can become a “crutch” that allows avoidance of the underlying causes of anxiety (mcdonald, n.d.).

Several mechanisms of action may mediate kava’s pharmacological effects on the brain, where it impacts a wide variety of neurotransmitters. Among them are its modulation of GABA, glutamine and seratonin activity (Sarris and Kavanagh, 2009); reversible MAO and dopamine reuptake (Uebelhack et al., 1998); the blockage of voltage-dependent calcium and sodium ion channels (Jamieson and Duffield, 1990; Schirrmacher et al., 1999); and inhibition of cyclooxygenase (Gleitz et al., 1997).  Kava is also associated with a reduced neuronal reuptake of noradrenaline (Münte et al., 1993; Kobayashi and Yashishima, 2001), which enhances cognitive function, including visual processing speeds, working memory scanning processes and retrieval efficacy (Uhlenhuth et al., 1988).

Interestingly, both isolated kavain and whole extract seem to influence the amygdalar complex and reticular formation, parts of the brain’s limbic system, which is linked to learned fear responses and patterns, social interactions, and sleep (Bastyr University, n.d.). Herbalist James Snow notes kava’s utility in working with those with a history of sexual abuse, particularly when its impact is yet unresolved, and is associated with anxiety and social withdrawal.

As a carminative, kava “exerts tonic activity on the gastrointestinal tract. It is most indicated in persons who demonstrate sluggish digestion and are lethargic in their overall disposition. Kava extract will promote glandular secretions of the digestive tract and enhance assimilation of food” (Bastyr University, n.d.). The combination of diuretic and anodyne activity, along with its tonifying and soothing effect on the mucosa of the urinary system, make kava an excellent remedy for urinary tract infections.

Taken internally or used topically, kava has a relaxing effect on both skeletal and smooth muscle, making it an excellent option for addressing muscle soreness from exercise or injury as well as the cramping and/or pain that accompanies dysmennorhea, endometriosis, fibromyalgia, and interstitial cystitis (Bastyr University, n.d.). Kava also addresses nerve pain, and is particularly calming to shooting neurological pain in the head, ears, eyes, and teeth because it affects the fifth and seventh cranial nerves, linked to the face and head. Kava’s strength “falls between that of aspirin and morphine,” showing an additive synergism when taken with aspirin (Bastyr University, n.d.).

Historically and in modern times, kava has been used as an aphrodisiac—Susun Weed (2002) calls it “an erotic friend.” A positive effect on sexual expressiveness, as an extension of its tendency to break down barriers and create intimacy, is certainly reasonable. At the same time, mcdonald (n.d.) suggests that an aphrodisiac effect might depend on the person; for those whose interest in sex “is inhibited by anxiety and muscle tension, kava could certainly be helpful. Its spicy flavor also indicates that it stimulates peripheral circulation, which can also be helpful. So, for cool, tense, anxious people, sounds good. People already sufficiently mellow have claimed it ‘puts them to sleep’”.

Kava is slightly warming, and both relaxing and stimulating, depending on the dose.  jim mcdonald paints a vivid picture of kava’s energetics in this description: “The best way I can describe the effects of kava kava is to compare it to lying on a sunny beach with nothing pressing to do or think about and being so laid back it feels as if you’ve sunk halfway into the sand. Kava puts you there” (mcdonald, n.d.).

Note that kava can have widely varying effects, depending on the strain, preparation and dose. For insomnia, a fully daily dose can be helpful taken 30-60 minutes before retiring (Bastyr University, n.d.); for menopausal discomfort including insomnia, nervousness, and hot flashes, the equivalent of 100mg of kavalactones may be appropriate (Warnecke, 1991).

Traditionally, a small amount fresh kava root was first chewed, allowing for saliva to predigest starches, then mixed with water or coconut water or milk. jim mcdonald (n.d.) offers the following recipe for a more traditional kava drink: “[T]ake a half ounce of ground kava, 2 tablespoons of cream or coconut milk and whisk it up in ¼ cup of water for a minute or two. Strain the mixture through a loose weave strainer (the “pulp” should get into your drink) and squeeze out the wet herb. Add another ¼ cup of water to the kava pulp, whisk up, and strain again, squeezing as much liquid from the kava pulp as possible.”

Kava’s pain-relieving qualities are also evident when prepared as an infused oil for muscle massage, or as a tincture diluted with water or glycerin and applied to the vulva and anus in the case of UTI, pruritis, or yeast infection (mcdonald, n.d.).

Adult Dose

Whole kava: 1-4.5 mL of 1:2 tincture (70% ETOH) in divided doses 1.5 grams/day of dried roostock in divided doses 2-3.5 grams/day of fresh rhizome in divided doses Standardized capsules: 100-200 mg kavalactones/day in divided doses (Bastyr University, n.d.).

Safety

There has been much debate and controversy over kava’s hepatotoxicity, particularly when taken over the long term. LaPorte et al. (2011) offers several potential causes of hepatoxicity, including inhibition of CYP450 enzymes, reduction of liver glutathione, and genetic polymorphisms; in addition, inappropriate storage or preparation of kava—particularly using synthetic substances as additives, and chemical solvents such as hexane and acetone to extract constituents—could be potentially toxic (Denham et al., 2002; Zhou et al., 2010; mcdonald, n.d.). However, human studies using kava root at appropriate and realistic doses have shown no evidence to prove this to be the case; as importantly, the millennia of traditional use (even among children) belie this argument. Unfortunately, some manufacturers have incorporated kava leaves, stems, or root peelings (Teschke et al., 2008; Teschke, 2011) rather than the outer layers of the rootstock in commercial preparations, which can contribute to liver toxicity. The alkaloid pipermethystine has been shown to “significantly decrease cellular ATP levels, mitochondrial membrane potential, and induced apoptosis as measured by the release of caspase-3 after 24 hours of treatment. These observations suggest that PM, rather than kavalactones, is capable of causing cell death, probably in part by disrupting mitochondrial function.” (Nerurkar et al., 2003). In support of kava’s safety record, mcdonald (n.d.) notes the vast difference between traditionally-prepared kava—whether following indigenous tradition or the ethanolic extracts prepared by early European practitioners—and some modern kava (or kava-derived) supplements. To protect quality and prevent hepatoxicity, Teschke et al. (2011) outline a six-point prophylactic plan that includes the following safety measures: “(1) use of a noble kava cultivar such as Borogu, at least 5 years old at time of harvest, (2) use of peeled and dried rhizomes and roots, (3) aqueous extraction, (4) dosage recommendation of ≤250mg kavalactones per day (for medicinal use), (5) systematic rigorous future research, and (6) a Pan Pacific quality control system enforced by strict policing.” That said, kava can have both side effects and contraindications. Overuse of large doses of kava can lead to a delayed-type hypersensitivity and induce a particular dermatitis, in which the skin becomes dry, scaly, and hyperpigmented, eventually peeling off (Cook, 1842; Grieve, 1971; mcdonald, n.d.). A dose equivalent to 400mg or more kavalactones daily over a prolonged period has been shown to have this effect, which is reversible upon discontinuing kava (Bastyr University, n.d.). In addition, kava may be contraindicated in pregnancy because of the possibility of alkaloid content as well as its potential as a stimulant. In addition, kava may interact with or potentiate dopamine agonists or antagonists, so should not be taken by those also taking L-dopa or other medications used to treat Parkinson’s disease ( Mills and Bone, 2005). While small doses of kava have a positive effect on cognition and motor skills, larger doses (or frequently taken smaller ones) can cause sedation; for this reason, reasonable caution is advised when driving operating heavy machinery (Mills and Bone, 2005).
