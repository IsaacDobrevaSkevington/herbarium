Geographic Distribution

Found throughout many different regions of the planet, including circumpolar, boreal, temperate, subtropical, and tropical areas (Stamets, 2002).

Botanical Description

Turkey tails are polypore mushrooms; the fruiting bodies of members of this class of fungi have a much sturdier texture than those of fleshy mushrooms like shiitakes, and can persist for months if not years. The spores of polypore mushrooms are released through tube-like pores, rather than from the gills stereotypical of commonly known fleshy mushrooms. Turkey tails are an annual mushroom whose fruiting bodies last for at least a few months, and their pores are located in a thin, white layer on the underside of the very thin, leathery, fan-shaped, shelf-like fruiting body. The upper side of the wavy fruiting body is distinguished by beautiful, radial bands of various contrasting colors, commonly brown, red, blue, white, gray, or black, and sometimes green when the mushroom is colonized by algae (Arora, 1991). The fruiting body (about 2-7 cm across) usually lacks an obvious stem (Arora, 1986).

Turkey tails cause white-rot via delignification of dead or dying trees, primarily hardwood, and are often found in overlapping clusters and sometimes large masses and rosettes; new fruiting bodies appear in the rainy winter and spring seasons in California (Arora, 1986).

Key Constituents

Polysaccharides (beta-glucans and others); polysaccharopeptides (PSK, PSP), sterols (ergosta-7,22,dien-3beta-ol, ergost-7-en-3beta-ol, ergosterol, beta-sitosterol, 5α,8α-epidioxyergosta-6,22-dien-3β-ol, 8,9-epoxyergosta-5,22-dien-3β,15-diol ), phenols, triterpenoids (glutinol, ursolic acid), sesquiterpenoids (coriolin), hydroxymethylquinoline, deoxyxoriolic acid (Chu et al., 2002; Hobbs, 1986; Jin et al., 2018; Stamets, 2002)

Sustainability

No known sustainability issues.

Harvesting Guidelines

Turkey tail mushrooms are annual polypore mushrooms that degrade over time, so harvest young mushrooms at their peak vitality, and freeze or dry soon after harvesting. Freezing for 24-48 hours before drying can reduce the possibility of maggot damage (Sinadinos, 2008).

Uses

Currently classified as Trametes versicolor, the genus name comes from the Latin trama (“weft, woof” terms used in the practice of weaving, and which also refer to the interwoven hyphal fibers that make up a mushroom). The specific epithet is from Latin verso (“turn”) and color, meaning “various changing colors” and referring to the bands of color on the fruiting body. The common name “turkey tail” also refers to the fruiting body and its likeness to its namesake bird. Relevant Latin synonyms that may be used in older literature include Coriolus versicolor, Polyporus versicolor, and Polystictus versicolor.

While turkey tail occurs globally, most traditional and scientific literature describing its use derives from eastern Asia. Called yun zhi (“cloud-like mushroom”) in China and kawaratake (“mushroom by the river bank”) in Japan (Hobbs, 1986), traditional use in Chinese medicine emphasizes its effects on the Liver and Spleen, and Chinese medicine and Western herbal practitioners value its ability to reduce dampness and phlegm (Chu et al., 2002; Sinadinos, 2008).

There is extensive scientific literature on the herbal applications of turkey tail and its extracts. At least as of 2008, of all health-promoting mushrooms investigated, the constituents of turkey tail had been the focus of the most scientific studies (Standish et al., 2008). Many studies have focused on investigating the clinical effects of water-soluble protein-bound polysaccharides, also known as proteoglycans, found in the mushroom. Among those deemed particularly active include PSP and PSK.

PSK, a patented compound called polysaccharide Krestin, or simply Krestin, was discovered in Japan in 1965. PSK is isolated by using ammonium sulfate to salt out the compound from water extracts of fermented deep-layer cultivated mycelia of T. versicolor strain CM101 (Wan, 2013). It consists of a strand of beta-glucans and other saccharides bound to polypeptide chains rich in aspartic and glutamic acids (among others) (Hobbs, 2004; Saleh et al., 2017; Standish et al., 2008). PSP, also known as polysaccharopeptide, was first isolated in China in 1983 from T. versicolor strain COV-1 and is commonly produced by boiling the deep-culture mycelia of COV-1 in water, then precipitating the extract in ethanol (Saleh et al., 2017; Standish et al., 2008). The two compounds vary in saccharide and peptide composition as well as types of linkages and branching in the glucan backbones, but have many similarities including being soluble in water and mostly insoluble in ethanol (Hobbs, 2004).

PSP is a mix of various protein-bound polysaccharides with similar characteristics (Chu, et al., 2002). These highly refined extracts are produced in liquid culture using fermentation technologies, and because of this, results from studies that utilize these extracts are not directly transferable to use of crude turkey tail mushroom extracts (Hobbs, 2014a). The higher molecular weight polysaccharides are known to have stronger immunomodulating effects than their derivative constituents, and the actual decomposition process of those larger polysaccharides within the body may itself actually stimulate the human immune system (Levy, 1998).

Of these two proteoglycans, Krestin has been more extensively investigated, and in Japan and China, it has been widely adopted as an adjunct cancer therapy (Standish et al., 2008). Even as early as 1993, Krestin was so widely utilized in Japan that it comprised 25% of the total anticancer drug market in that country (Wasser, 2002). PSK is considered a biological response modifier, defined as a substance that causes no harm, places no additional stress on the body, helps the body adapt to environmental or biological stresses, and has nonspecific action on the body that supports major systems and regulatory functions (Wasser, 2002). This definition is quite parallel to what herbalists call adaptogens.

Beta-glucans, non-digestible glucose polymers which occur in many forms, have often captured the attention of researchers, as they occur ubiquitously throughout all fungi as a component of their hyphal cell walls (Hobbs, 2014a). An extract of turkey tail fruiting body could contain 40-50% beta glucans (Hobbs, 2014a). Beta-glucans activate the immune response and inhibit pathogens, via binding to gut-associated lymphatic tissue and also macrophages associated with the gut barrier. Beta-glucans also activate macrophages, neutrophils, monocytes, natural killer cells, and dendritic cells, thus modulating both innate and adaptive immune responses (Hobbs, 2014a).

Rich in heavy weight polysaccharides that serve as prebiotics, turkey tail water extract has been found to have a beneficial effect on the composition of intestinal microbiota (Pallav, 2014; Yu et al., 2013). In an open-label, randomized clinical trial of 24 healthy adults, consumption of PSP had a regulatory, prebiotic activity on the intestinal microbiome. Furthermore, an in vitro study (Yu et al., 2013) of human fecal microbiota cultured anaerobically with PSP showed that PSP was able to promote many of the same genera of the human microbiota that fructooligosaccharides promote, including Bifidobacterium spp.

This data provides fodder for explaining traditional use of turkey tail in Chinese medicine as a general vitality enhancer that benefits people with chronic diseases (Hobbs, 1986), as its prebiotic action alone would benefit those with disorders of the digestive and immune systems, hypertension, metabolic disorder and its components including obesity, hyperglycemia, and dyslipidemia, respiratory disorders such as allergies or asthma, psychiatric conditions such as anxiety and depression, and immune conditions such as cancer (Friedman, 2016). More health conditions are found to be modulated by gut microflora each year.

Intriguingly, the ramifications of the impact on the intestinal microbiota give us clues as to how an herb like turkey tail, which is largely composed of these non-digestible polysaccharides with low bioavailability, could have such a high bioactivity (Chen et al., 2015). In the in vitro study by Yu et. al. (2013) mentioned above, PSP fermentation in fecal microbiota also increased the concentration of lactate and short-chain fatty acids, decreased the pH, and increased β-galactosidase and β-glucosidase activities, of which the latter are known to improve the bioactivity of dietary isoflavones (Otieno & Shah, 2007; Yu et al., 2013). This action combined with the other indirect benefits of optimizing our commensal relationships with intestinal microbiota could shed light on the mechanisms behind the benefits of turkey tail and other mushrooms.

One author speculated that while the PSP could be directly altering the microbiota as a prebiotic, due to its immunomodulatory activity, it is also possible that PSP “shaped microbiome composition not by acting directly on microbes, but instead by eliciting host responses that, in turn, reshaped the microbiome” (Pallav, 2014, p. 464). More research is called for to determine the ability of PSP or turkey tail to modulate “microbial composition in the elderly, during and after antibiotic therapy, in immunosuppression or during periods of impaired oral nutrition” (Pallav, 2014, p. 464). This future data could better illuminate how effective turkey tail extract could be to mend damage caused by antibiotic use.

Turkey tail and its extracts may improve levels of blood lipids. In a 2007 study of 240 people with hyperlipidemia, consumption of PSP alone was able to reduce total blood cholesterol, low-density lipoprotein (LDL), and triglycerides compared to control (Chang et al., 2017).

Turkey tail extracts are known to effectively support patients with chronic liver disease including drug-induced and viral hepatitis (including chronic forms) (Chang et al., 2017). PSP on its own or combined with either interferon treatment or other traditional Chinese herbs has improved recovery rate of key liver enzymes, improved development of antibodies against hepatitis B virus, reduced hepatitis B viral load, and increased HBeAG seroconversion rate (meaning the rate of converting to a disease state where the disease is less infectious to others) (Chang et al., 2017).

Turkey tail is an excellent immune stimulant and modulator, and its extracts are widely utilized as adjunct support, with demonstrated ability to prevent and inhibit cancer and tumor growth (Chang et al., 2017). Among the known immunological effects from preclinical and clinical trials utilizing aqueous turkey tail extracts are support of cell-mediated immunity, reticuloendothelial system activation, cytokine modulation, and improvement of dendritic cell viability, T-cell maturation, and natural killer cell activity (Hobbs, 2004). Turkey tail also supports humoral immunity and exhibits protective immunological effects, and may reduce the carcinogenicity of exposure to some substances such as tobacco smoke or asbestos (Hobbs, 2004).

Demonstrating anticancer, antitumor, anticarcinogenic, and antiproliferative effects in vitro and in vivo, clinical trials have also shown that consumption of turkey tail extracts, especially PSP and PSK, as an adjunct to standard cancer treatment can improve outcomes, such as survival rates, in people experiencing a number of different cancers (Hobbs, 2004). These include but are not limited to colorectal cancer (Sakamoto et al., 2006), breast cancer (Standish et al., 2008), gastric cancer (Nakazato et al., 1994; Toge & Yamaguchi, 2000), esophageal cancer (Standish et al., 2008), and non-small cell lung cancer (Tsang et al., 2003). A phase I study found that women consuming as much as 9 grams a day of a commercially available turkey tail mycelial product had improved immune status compared with those receiving standard care (surgery, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy) alone (Torkelson, 2012).

Turkey tail is also employed by herbalists and physicians to reduce chemotherapy and radiation-induced side effects such as anorexia, fatigue, and pain, and to speed recuperation overall (Hobbs, 2014b; Sinadinos, 2008). PSP has been shown to improve quality of life in patients undergoing chemotherapy, and it can enable patients to tolerate further chemotherapy and potentially prolong life (Chang et al., 2017). In one clinical study, the addition of PSK reduced adverse effects of bone-marrow suppression and peripheral neuropathy induced by standard chemotherapy treatment (Shibata et al., 2011). It may have protective benefits for immunocompromised individuals who undergo standard cancer treatments (Hobbs, 2004).

Turkey tail’s immunomodulatory action has also been traditionally used for those with autoimmune conditions, such as rheumatoid arthritis (Jin et al., 2018). A preliminary trial in patients with systemic lupus erythematosus showed administration of PSK resulted in an improvement of symptoms, though more studies are called for to elucidate these findings (Yarnell & Abascal, 2008). PSP has also been shown to have ciclosporin-like homeostatic effects on human lymphocytes in vitro, further suggesting potential as an immunomodulant in the treatment of autoimmune disease (Lee et al., 2010).

Based on its traditional indication for reducing dampness and phlegm, turkey tail is relied upon for thwarting and soothing colds, flus, and other respiratory infections (Sinadinos, 2008). Herbalists also employ turkey tail to speed recuperation from pneumonia and bronchitis, and to support those with asthma (Jin et al., 2018; Sinadinos, 2008). Two human clinical trials from 1979 back up this traditional use; the studies showed that consumption of PSP from turkey tail reduced cough, phlegm, and breathing difficulties and enhanced innate immunity in patients with chronic bronchitis (as cited in Chang et al., 2017). In vitro, turkey tail extracts showed a high efficacy against influenza H1N1, with low cytotoxicity (Krupodorova et al., 2014).

In vitro and clinical trials, as well as traditional use, show that turkey tail has potential in working with a variety of chronic viral infections. A preliminary randomized clinical trial found administration of a combination of turkey tail and Ganoderma lucidum for 2 months in patients positive for oral human papillomavirus-16 or -18 produced a clearance rate of 88%, compared to just 5% in those consuming Laetiporus sulphureus (Donatini, 2014). Turkey tail may have application for herpes infection as well. In vitro results showed a potent effect against herpes simplex virus type 2 in infected human cells (Wang & Ng, 2001).

Even more notably, a recent in vitro study in human immunodeficiency virus (HIV)-positive human cells showed that PSP could downregulate viral replication and upregulate key antiviral chemokines (Rodríguez-Valentín et al., 2018). Another study noted the ability of PSP to inhibit HIV-1 reverse transcriptase in vitro (Wang & Ng, 2001).

In herbal practice, the method of preparation is key. According to herbalist, researcher, and mushroom expert Christopher Hobbs (2014a), water extracts instead of tinctures are called for in cases of immune disorders, cancer, or viral syndromes, because alcohol not only does not extract the polysaccharides and proteins, but it also precipitates the high-molecular weight compounds and interferes with the molecular structure, and thus the immunomodulatory activity of those compounds. However, alcohol does extract triterpenes associated with sedative, antiallergenic, and hepatoprotective effects (Hobbs, 2014a). It is also worth repeating that the vast majority of the cited studies used either standardized extracts PSP or PSK, which may not translate directly to use of crude mushroom or mycelium extract. That said, the water extract of Trametes versicolor has been found to contain variously sized PSP-like molecules, with the largest ones considered the most effective (Hobbs, 2004).

Adult Dose

Tincture: 30-90 drops (1:5, 60%) 2-3x/day (Sinadinos, 2008).

Decoction: Up to 25 g shredded or powdered mushroom, divided into 2-3 doses/day (Hobbs, 2014a).

Capsules: 1-3 #00 capsules filled with powder, 1-2x/day Sinadinos, 2008).

Standardized Extract: 1-2 g PSP or PSK extract, 2-3x/day (Hobbs, 2014a).

Safety

No relevant safety issues known from use of fruiting bodies. Mycelia may have blood thinning effects that could have an impact on bleeding before or after surgery or when consuming blood thinning medications; consult with a qualified practitioner (Sinadinos, 2008). As of 2002, there were no known drug interactions reported for Trametes versicolor, but caution should be taken as there is a theoretical concern that it could counteract the effect of pharmaceutical immunosuppressants (Chu et al., 2002).
Experts differ on their opinions regarding the consumption of raw mushrooms. Some, including Paul Stamets and Andrew Weil, suggest never consuming any raw mushroom because of potential inherent toxins that are destroyed when cooked (Stamets, 2019), while Christopher Hobbs personally chews raw turkey tail mushrooms as gum (likely swallowing whatever raw compounds that may be extracted during mastication) (Grusauskas, 2015).
