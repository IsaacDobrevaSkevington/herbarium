Yellow Dock

Rumex crispus

Native To

Europe, western Asia, and northern Africa.

Geographic Distribution

Yellow dock is widespread and naturalized in many parts of the world. It is found throughout Asia, Europe, North and South Africa, Australia, New Zealand, South America, and North America including all fifty of the United States. It is considered one of the five most widely distributed plants in the world and is listed as an invasive species in some regions (Xu et al., 2013).

Botanical Description

Rumex crispus is a perennial that reaches a height of 4-5 feet. First-year plants form a basal rosette of leaves which gives way in the second year to a stalk bearing small, greenish, apetalous flowers in panicles. Lance-shaped, alternate leaves have distinctly curly margins. Grooved, angular stems are usually branched from the base up, with larger leaves at the bottom of the plant and smaller, thin, willowy leaves toward the top. The small seeds are enclosed by a winged, papery, heart-shaped husk. Both stalks and seeds turn a distinctive, rusty brown hue in the autumn and persist throughout the winter. Carrot-like taproots are typically between 8-12 inches long and have a relatively thick bark. The root bark is an orange-yellow color, while the root’s interior has a lighter, brighter yellow hue. Other Rumex species are often mistaken for R. crispus. Look for narrow leaves with very wavy margins and a pale green stem; another indicator is the yellow color of the roots.

Key Constituents

Anthraquinones (such as aglycone, aloe emodin, chrysophanol, emodin, physcion, and rhein), calcium oxalate, flavonoids (including quercitrin), naphthalene derivatives (including neopodin and lapodin), oxalic acid, minerals (including aluminum, ash, iron, magnesium, manganese, niacin, phosphorous, potassium, selenium, silicon, and tin), parietin, tannins, and vitamins A, B1 (thiamine), B2 (riboflavin), B3 (niacin), and C (ascorbic acid) (Choi et al., 2004; Katz, 2016; Pederson, 1998; “Yellow Dock,” 2007).

Sustainability

No known sustainability issues.

Harvesting Guidelines

Dig roots in the autumn after the entire plant has turned its characteristic reddish-brown color (Masé, 2013; Ross, 2014). Collect young leaves that have recently unfurled in early spring. Avoid harvesting leaves during very dry conditions, as this can increase the levels of oxalic acid (Raser-Rowland & Grubb, n.d.).

Uses

Yellow dock’s botanical name, Rumex crispus, describes the plant’s physical form. The genus name Rumex, which is shared with other buckwheat or Polygonaceae family plants such as sorrel, is derived from a Latin word meaning “lance-like,” and denotes the shape of the leaves. The species name crispus means “curly” and refers to the distinctly undulating leaf margins (Pederson, 1998). The common name, yellow dock, also describes a physical attribute of the plant—its yellow roots.

There are about 25 species of dock in North America, and several of these have been used interchangeably in herbalism (de la Forêt, 2012). Many of the old texts, such as King’s American Dispensatory (Lloyd & Felter, 1898) and Grieve’s A Modern Herbal (1931/1961) mention several species of Rumex as having similar actions and uses. However, R. crispus is usually preferred by modern practitioners (Wood, 2008).

Rumex has a strong affinity for the digestive system, and has been used by various cultures to balance digestion. Dioscorides and Culpeper both used yellow dock seed as an astringent for dysentery and other gastrointestinal complaints (Hoffelt, 2016). Once the plant spread into North America after the arrival of colonists, many Native American tribes, such as the Cherokee, Delaware, and Iroquois, also employed yellow dock for gastrointestinal complaints, such as diarrhea and dysentery (Moerman, 2009). American pioneers drank a decoction of yellow dock root as a tonic, digestive aid, and slight laxative (Angier, 2008). The Eclectics also used Rumex for digestive issues such as stomach ulcers and diarrhea (Ellingwood, 1919). King’s American Dispensatory specifically recommends small doses of Rumex in cases of nervous dyspepsia with gas and darting pain in the left chest (Lloyd & Felter, 1898).

Contemporary herbalists still employ yellow dock as an important digestive ally. The plant’s bitter properties activate the liver and gallbladder, aid in the digestion of fats and the absorption of nutrients, and speed up bowel transit time. Meanwhile, its cooling properties ease inflammation of the digestive tract; herbalists use it in cases of mouth and stomach ulcers, heartburn, and esophageal reflux (Katz, 2016).

Yellow dock is often given as a mild laxative; according to Guido Masé (2013), “there is no safer or gentler bitter when it comes to ensuring regular bowel function” (p. 178). He uses a tincture of R. crispus in combination with a diet high in fiber and bitter greens for clients who have become dependent upon over-the-counter laxatives for bowel movements. He also suggests yellow dock for easing constipation that can occur during travel and pregnancy (Masé, 2013). Herbalist Amanda Klenner (2016) uses R. crispus in cases of constipation caused by side effects of prescription drugs. According to Matthew Wood (2008), constipation causing lower back pain is a specific indication for yellow dock, along with slow digestion and hemorrhoids.

Interestingly, yellow dock is also employed in cases of diarrhea. While the plant’s anthraquinone content gives it a mild laxative effect, its astringent qualities also work the opposite way to restrain the bowels (Wood, 2008). According to Ellingwood (1919), “It has cured exceedingly persistent cases of exhaustive morning diarrhea, the discharges being very frequent between six and twelve o’clock” (“Rumex crispus” section, para. 1). Wood (2008) advises lowering the dosage in cases of loose stool. He specifically suggests yellow dock “in cases where the bowels tend to be loose but at the same time are difficult to expel, due to weakness of the rectal muscles or the intestinal muscles in general” (Wood, 2008, p. 439). Wood (2008) also describes using yellow dock in a severe case of ulcerative colitis; the client, who was having 15-20 stools a day, regained health to such an extent that she did not need a colostomy.

Following yellow dock’s long history of use for the gastrointestinal tract, recent studies investigated the plant’s possible utility in cases of colorectal cancer. One study found that a methanol extract of R. crispus root had antidiabetic, anticancer, and free radical scavenging properties in vitro (Shiwani et al., 2012). A 2015 study found water soluble compounds from R. crispus to have anticancer activity on human colon cancer cells (Bhandari & Clark, 2015). Another study found chrysophanic acid, an anthraquinone found in yellow dock, to block the proliferation of human colon cancer cells (Lee et al., 2011). While these studies are promising, more in-depth and in vivo studies are needed to determine the yellow dock’s efficacy for colon cancer.

Of course, the digestive system is strongly linked to our emotions and “gut feelings.” Energetically, yellow dock brings balance here as well. According to Masé, (2013), “…old herbalists who know of such things say that this plant can help us let go of the influences that are blocking our progress, impeding our understanding, or simply weighing us down” (p. 178). Meanwhile, Wood (2008) describes the condition calling for yellow dock as “excess badger energy,” which means “too much fire in the stomach and digestive tract, too much appetite, too much emotional energy” (p. 438).

In Ayurveda, yellow dock is used to reduce excess pitta, especially when it manifests as an overabundance of bile (Frawley & Lad, 1986). In this tradition, high pitta is connected with the emotional state of anger. Similarly, bile (or choler in Greek), has long been connected in Western traditions with anger, irritability, and tension (Masé, 2013). As Masé (2013) puts it, “By helping us release old patterns we hold on to (and a little bile as well), bitters such as yellowdock can counter the choleric tendency in our modern culture” (p. 181).

Yellow dock also has a long history of use as an alterative, which in folk medicine is often called a “blood purifier” or “blood cleanser.” Several Native American tribes, such as the Cherokee, Delaware, Iroquois, Mohegan, Paiute, and Shoshoni, have a history of using yellow dock to purify the blood and ease a variety of ailments (Moerman, 2009). Physiomedical and Eclectic physicians classified the plant as an alterative, employing it in cases of “bad blood” associated with skin conditions and glandular congestion (Cook, 1869; Ellingwood, 1919; Lloyd & Felter, 1898).

As herbal jim mcdonald (n.d.) points out, alteratives are a bit tricky to define because they do not work on the blood itself, but rather, improve the metabolic functioning of the body. Herbalist Ed Smith (2013) defines an alterative as acting through the lymphatic, glandular, and mucous membrane systems to favorably alter the breakdown and elimination of metabolic waste. Yellow dock has an affinity for the glandular system, and was used in the Eclectic tradition for glandular conditions such as scrofula, bubonic swellings, and chronic enlargement of the lymph nodes (Ellingwood, 1919; Lloyd & Felter, 1898). By 1890, American drug maker Parke, Davis and Company was producing Rumex extracts for scrofula and skin diseases associated with syphilis (Hoffelt, 2016).

Alterative plants also nourish the body and support the assimilation of nutrients. As mcdonald (n.d.) puts it, “It is important to understand that alteratives act by feeding and nourishing the body—by supporting it—rather than by forcing it to behave in one manner or another. Therefore, these herbs are more akin to nourishing foods than medicines” (“Alterative” section, para. 2). Indeed, yellow dock is chock full of nutrients and has been enjoyed in a wide variety of foods by foragers and herbalists.

Young yellow dock leaves are often eaten as a cooked green used in place of spinach (Peterson, 1977). In fact, both spinach and yellow dock leaf are high in oxalic acid, so some moderation is required when adding dock leaf to the diet, especially for those with kidney or liver issues. The leaves of yellow dock are tangy and make a delicious addition to omelets, frittatas, quiches, soups, curries, and lasagna. Since the stems can be tough, many people recommend de-stemming yellow dock leaves (Gibbons, 2016). However, the flower stalks can be harvested in the mid-spring, peeled, and eaten cooked or raw (Katz, 2016).

Some folks harvest yellow dock’s abundant seeds and grind them into flour. Rosalee de la Forêt (2012) makes dock seed crackers with the flour and says it is a favorite in her household. Others enjoy adding yellow dock root to various dishes; Susun Weed (1989) includes it in her “spring tonic soup” recipe, which also contains burdock root, dandelion greens and root, and seaweed.

Yellow dock is high in a variety of vitamins and minerals such as calcium, magnesium, manganese, phosphorus, and vitamin A. Historically used for scurvy, the plant also contains ample vitamin C (Pedersen, 1998; Wood, 2008). Yellow dock has also been used to ease anemia, due to its iron content as well as its ability to promote the absorption of iron. Thus, it is helpful to combine yellow dock with other iron-rich foods, such as nettle and seaweed, for the most benefit (Katz, 2016). One traditional remedy is yellow dock syrup, made by combining a root decoction with molasses, honey, or maple syrup (Angier, 2008; Hoffelt, 2016).

Yellow dock can tolerate infertile soil and often grows in waste areas and places disturbed by humans. Yet, we must take care to harvest this plant from unpolluted areas, as it bioaccumulates heavy metals such as cadmium and lead from the soil (Zhang et al., 2014). Some have even cultivated the plant in iron-rich areas in order to boost iron levels in the plant (Ellingwood, 1919). In fact, yellow dock has an interesting relationship with iron; it increases iron levels in soil around its roots and may help release iron stored in the liver (Justis, 2016; Kisson et al., 2010). Even the distinctive rust-color this plant adopts in the fall can be considered a signature for iron (Masé, 2013).

Herbalists also employ Rumex crispus to stimulate a congested, sluggish liver (Grieve, 1931/1971; Wood, 2008). This can help address deep, chronic issues that manifest on the skin, such as acne, boils, eczema, jaundice, and psoriasis (Katz, 2016). In fact, yellow dock root is used both internally and externally for skin conditions. Externally, it has been employed for burns, ulcerations, sores, scabs, rashes, diabetic ulcers, and hives (Angier, 2008; Hoffelt, 2016; Wood, 2008). Appalachian folk herbalists soak dock root in vinegar and apply as a wash on ringworm, rashes, and on animals in cases of mange or saddle sores (Angier, 2008).

Dock has also been used as a folk remedy for nettle rash, an old tradition with roots in the United Kingdom (Hoffelt, 2016). There is a saying that goes along with this practice: “In dock, out nettle” (Grieve, 1931/1971). In the springtime, new dock leaves emerging from the soil are accompanied with sticky mucilage at the base; this is the best part of the plant to use on nettle rash (Hoffelt, 2016; Masé, 2013).

R. crispus also has a history of use for the respiratory system. The Cheyenne have a history of using yellow dock for pulmonary hemorrhages, while the Yavapai have used the root decoction as a cough medicine (Moerman, 2009). Yellow dock was employed by Eclectic herbalists for respiratory issues such as catarrh, chronic sore throat, and tuberculosis (Lloyd & Felter, 1898). Modern herbalists still use it for respiratory issues such as a dry and irritated cough, laryngitis, and bronchitis (Katz, 2016; Wood, 2008).

Used to ease a variety of reproductive complaints, yellow dock is employed for hormonal imbalances related to liver congestion and poor fat digestion, such as fibrocystic breasts, uterine fibroids, and menstrual issues. For example, it is used to ease premenstrual syndrome, dysmenorrhea, amenorrhea, menorrhagia, as well as menstrual headaches and acne (Justis, 2016). Rosemary Gladstar (1993) suggests yellow dock for young individuals who are anemic or low in iron, chronically tired and stressed, and struggle with stubborn skin problems. For the latter issue, herbalist Angela Justis (2016) combines yellow dock with burdock, sarsaparilla, and echinacea.

Yellow dock also has a history of use in cases of chronic pain, such as rheumatism, gout, and fibromyalgia (Katz, 2016; Klenner, 2016). A tradition in the Blackfoot, Shoshoni, and Paiute tribes is to use mashed root pulp to ease rheumatic swellings, while the Iroquois have made a poultice from cooked stalks for this purpose (Moerman, 2009). Grieve (1931/1971) mentions yellow dock’s tonic and laxative properties as being useful for rheumatism. Amanda Klenner (2016) employs yellow dock to support the liver and help the body deal with excess stress hormones for those dealing with chronic pain from conditions such as fibromyalgia.

Wood (2008) mentions the use of yellow dock for kidney issues such as edema, stones, and the retention of urine. However, many herbalists caution against using yellow dock when kidney disease is present (Masé, 2013). Some warn that the oxalic acid in yellow dock can aggravate rheumatism and gout, despite the plant’s traditional use for these conditions. Thus, moderation is advised when it comes to the dosage and duration of using yellow dock.

Adult Dose

Tincture: 30-75 drops fresh root (1:2, 50%) or dried root (1:5, 50%) 3x/day (Moore, 1995).

Decoction: 6-12 g dried root/day divided into 1-3 doses (Mills & Bone, 2005).

Capsule: 1-2 #00 capsules 1-2x/day (Moore, 1995).

Safety

Large doses, especially of the fresh root, may cause gastrointestinal upset, nausea, and diarrhea (Foster & Duke, 2000; “Yellow Dock,” 2007). Moore (1995) suggests that yellow dock is most beneficial in sub-laxative doses. Many herbalists recommend using yellow dock moderately and for short-term periods, such as 2-3 weeks at most (Chevalier, 2007; Masé, 2013).
Additionally, the leaves of R. crispus contain oxalic acid, which can bind to minerals and create oxalate crystals that may contribute to kidney stones, gout, vulvodynia, and rheumatoid arthritis. Thus, caution is advised for people prone to any of these conditions (Raser-Rowland & Grubb, n.d.). However, it is interesting to note that yellow dock has traditionally been used for gout, kidney stones, edema, and rheumatism (Grieve, 1931/1971; Wood, 2008). To put this into perspective, consider that some foods such as spinach also contain oxalic acid. Most people can tolerate eating some raw leaves of spinach or yellow dock occasionally, but it is important not to over consume (Gibbons, 2016). One way of reducing oxalic acid is by blanching the greens for a few minutes and disposing of the water before eating (Raser-Rowland & Grubb, n.d.).
