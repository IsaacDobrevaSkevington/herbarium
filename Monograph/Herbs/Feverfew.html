Feverfew

Tanacetum parthenium

Native To

Geographic Distribution

Feverfew was introduced to North America in the mid-19th century. In the States it may now be found from eastern Canada as far south as Maryland and as far west as Missouri (Pareek et al., 2011). The herb is also present in North Africa, Japan, China, Australia, and Europe, where it can be seen in hedges and fields, but feverfew is more often cultivated in gardens for ornamental purposes.

Botanical Description

Feverfew is a perennial and grows to a height of about 2-3 feet. In summer feverfew bears clusters of numerous white flowers with yellow centers that resemble daisy flowers.

Feverfew is related to chamomile and is often confused with it. Unlike chamomile, however, feverfew’s properties are primarily in the leaves rather than the flowers; it is an upright and tall plant, rather than prone to be recumbent; and the central yellow portion of the flower is flat in shape, rather than conical. Feverfew is also often confused with tansy (Tanacetum vulgare).

Feverfew’s vaguely feather-shaped leaves are pinnately lobed, yellow-green in color, and vibrant even in early or mild winters. While the stem is hairy, the leaves feature short hairs and are smooth. Feverfew is marked by a strong, bitter odor and bees noticeably avoid the plant.

Key Constituents

Bitter resin, pyrethrin, inulin (in root), gums, tannic acid (Holmes, 2006); sesquiterpene lactones (incl. parthenolide), flavonoids (Bones & Mills, 2013; Pareek et al., 2011); volatile oils (incl. camphor, camphene, bornyl acetate, p-cymene) (Pareek et al., 2011). 

Sustainability

No known sustainability issues.

Harvesting Guidelines

One may wish to wear gloves when handling feverfew in case of contact dermatitis or sensitivity to the Asteraceae / Compositae family (alternatively: test a patch of skin at least 24 hours in advance of handling to ascertain any allergic reactions).

Feverfew is harvested in the herb’s second year of growth in mid-summer or whenever the flowers are in full bloom. Cutting down the herb by about 3-4 inches will also allow feverfew to regrow for a secondary harvest.

When harvesting, strip the leaves and flowers from each stem. It is ideal to use feverfew fresh, so if not tincturing or otherwise using the fresh leaves, dry the leaves and flowers carefully before proper storage.

Uses

Feverfew can be a challenging herb to pin down in terms of identification; not only does it frequently get confused with chamomile or tansy (see above under “botanical identification”) but the plant has also been given several names throughout its history: the binomials alone include Chrysanthemum parthenium, Pyrethrum parthenium, and Matricaria pyrethrum; it has been categorized in at least five genera (Pareek et al., 2011).

At the same time, due in part to a pronounced appearance in the public sphere since the late 1970s, feverfew has been the subject of numerous scientific studies. Its chemical component parthenolide has been a feature of many of these inquiries and championed as responsible for much of the herb’s efficacy.

Throughout history, feverfew has been primarily supportive of the female reproductive system and an aid to conditions specific to congestion in the head. It has also been a notable balancer of the respiratory and nervous systems, a pain aid, and useful in warding off insects. Recent scientific studies are cropping up for some of these traditional uses as well as for other indications found in historical records such as arthritis and asthma. At the same time, cancer has become a strong focal point of feverfew research.

Feverfew’s ancient reputation as a female reproductive herb was etymologically linked since ancient Greece, where it was known as τό παρθένιον (parthenion) and in Latin as parthenium. Both names have been directly transplanted as the modern Latin botanical name, parthenium.

The ancient Greek parthenion referred to an herb and by poetic extension meant a “girl;” the word was derived from the ancient Greek adjective παρθένιος, α, ον, which means “maidenly” or “virginal.” Parkinson—a royal botanist and apothecary who often sifted through and compiled ancient sources on herbs—and other herbal writers of the Renaissance age confirmed that the name parthenium was related to the fact that this herb is especially appropriate for uterine issues (Parkinson, 1640).

Herbalist Culpeper (1770) remarked in his contemporaneous herbal:

“Venus has commended this herb to succour her Sisters, to be a general strengthener of their Wombs, and to remedy such Infirmities as a careless Midwife has there caused…and doth a Woman all the Good she can desire of an Herb” (p. 131)

Several different herbs, however, received the appellation of parthenion or parthenium in antiquity, making it difficult to clearly identify feverfew in old texts. The above-mentioned Parkinson added in his entry on feverfew that “Parthenium was a word applyed to many hearbes” (Parkinson, 1640, p. 85).

Parthenon is another ancient Greek noun closely related to parthenion and is likewise the name of the temple on the Athenian acropolis. The Roman writer Pliny, in describing an herb that may or may not be feverfew, recounts an etymological story concerning a slave of Pericles who was undertaking roofing work on the temple buildings of the citadel when he fell. The effects of his fall were addressed by a plant called parthenium, the virtues of which the virgin goddess Athena had told to Pericles in a dream (Pliny, 1906). The plant was thereby named parthenium after Athena, who is also known as the “Maiden goddess,” or the “Parthenia.” 

Feverfew was considered an herb specific not just to women, but to mothers (Parkinson, 1640). Indeed, as with a handful of other herbs viewed as especial protectors of women, feverfew has carried the common name of “motherwort.” The idea that feverfew is an herb of the uterus is true of Eastern traditions as well; in Chinese medicine, feverfew—viewed as a uterine spasmolytic in Western traditions—is a uterine qi-regulator.

Feverfew has been traditionally turned to for the regulation of menstrual cycles marked by deficiency or excess. On the one hand this includes such issues as clots, delay, scanty flow, or amenorrhea—particularly when there is a dull pain prior to onset (Holmes, 2006). On the other hand, feverfew equally addressed painful cramps in general, PMS, or spasmodic dysmenorrhea. It has been suggested that its emmenagogic action is only effective with high doses (Bone & Mills, 2013). 

Although the herb was not introduced to North America until the middle of the 19th century, it is interesting to observe that the Cherokee people include feverfew in their “Medicines of the East and the North” for issues pertaining to young women’s menstrual cycles (Garrett, 2003). It’s interesting to note that there is also a plant with the common name of wild American feverfew or wild quinine (Parthenium integrifolium), also in the Asteraceae family, that does not resemble Tanacetum parthenium but does have a long history of Indigenous American use (NRCS, 2001).

Associated uses in the European tradition include vaginal steams with infusions of the herb (Parkinson, 1640). Feverfew has also been used traditionally for infertility. By the early 19th century it was being indicated for “hysteric” disorders (Hill, 1812).

Today, however, it is feverfew’s affinity for the head that is most well known and widely promoted; scientific research on the herb’s prowess in conquering migraines began relatively early and on a large scale. In the 1970s, newspapers in England were reporting the story of a Welsh woman who was nibbling three fresh feverfew leaves each day and thus rid her self of migraine headaches over a timeframe of several months (Mabey, 1988; Bone & Mills, 2013). Highly successful clinical studies followed, along with popularity in the UK. 

The clinical trial of migraine specialist Dr. Stewart Johnson subsequently confirmed that feverfew was useful in high percentages in the staving off of migraines, in reducing the length and severity of attacks, and in removing the nausea associated with some migraine episodes (Bone & Mills, 2013).

While feverfew rapidly became the “migraine herb” in the public eye, herbalists have taken time to point out how their practices employ feverfew for only certain types of headaches or migraines. Rosemary Gladstar, who likes to tincture feverfew with lavender, emphasizes that feverfew works on migraines over time—usually at least 1-3 months and when used proactively rather than after the migraine is already underway (Gladstar, 2001).

Meanwhile, herbalist David Winston (2003) asserts that feverfew best addresses yang migraines, or those of a specifically vasodilative nature. Furthermore, herbalist Michael Tierra (1998) records that the effectiveness in terms of migraines is probably only for those migraines marked by “genuine excess heat conformation” (p. 159).

There are other hints of historical usage of feverfew linked to headaches. Feverfew was especially remarked upon in the 17th century as an antidote to opium overdose (Parkinson, 1640; Culpeper, 1770). Although opium was often taken for headaches, overdosing or overuse of opium can cause them. This may be why feverfew was an herb of choice.

A direct indication of feverfew for issues pertaining to the head, however, goes back at least as far as the Middle Ages. In the 12th century herbal writings of St. Hildegard von Bingen (2001), we find listed amongst the virtues of feverfew—an herb that she strongly profiled as a general tonic that restored blood and health—the following:

“And a person who has much congestion in the head will have less congestion if feverfew is eaten frequently” (p. 24).

She found feverfew to be moderately warm, which is a departure from its usual energetic reading as cool. Gerard (1636) found it to be hot. And some five centuries later Parkinson (1640) wrote:

“It is very effectuall for all paines in the head, coming of a cold   cause…” (p. 85).

17th century herbal writers in particular wrote that the leaves were crushed and applied to the crown of the head as well as drunk warm before succumbing to the ague, or bruised and applied to the wrists for the fits of ague—that is, for fevers with intermittent sweating and chills (Gerard, 1636; Parkinson, 1640).

As a heat-clearing relaxant, feverfew is also analgesic and anti-inflammatory (Holmes, 2006), which is in part why it has been used for headaches, neuralgia, and fibromyalgia. With respect to its reputation in migraine support, it appears that feverfew inhibits granule secretion from platelets and is highly and uniquely anti-inflammatory due partially to the presence of the above-mentioned constituent parthenolide (Bone & Mills, 2013). That being said, feverfew possesses at least 30 other sesquiterpene lactones, indicating that its mechanism of action is even more complex (Pareek et al., 2011). 

Outside of its fame as “the migraine herb,” feverfew is perhaps not widely used in present-day practices. Its less-conspicuous place in contemporary materia medica isn’t a new phenomenon; in an early 19th century edition of Culpeper (1826), , we find the following remark about feverfew: “this herb, though not much used in the present practice, deserves the notice of physicians” (p. 59).

One might immediately think that feverfew is a standard herb of choice for fevers due to its common name. Feverfew may be derived from another common name in Latin: febrifuga, meaning the “the driving off of fever.” Gerard (1636) shared that the name “is taken from…[its] force of driving away Agues” (p. 653).

Feverfew does seem to have been historically used for fever occasionally, but a fever in Medieval or Renaissance times could also have been more in line with old humoral definitions of head pains with an accompanying cold rather than the contemporary definition of fever—that is, the exhibition of a high temperature (Barker, 2001). Feverfew is not widely used for fevers today.

Nonetheless, feverfew’s application to headaches combined with its antipyretic, analgesic, and antimicrobial actions may account for why it was traditionally used for fevers and colds with accompanying aches and pains. In Chinese medicine, feverfew may be used as a relaxing diaphoretic for intermittent fevers of the shao yang stage or acute wind-heat fevers, especially those which may present with both headaches and aches and pains (Holmes, 2006).

The anti-inflammatory aspects of feverfew may also account for its traditional use in asthma and aspects of bronchial congestion such as phlegm, coughs, and congestion of the chest, all of which are found as indications for feverfew in historical records (Parkinson, 1640). While largely used internally, the herb was also used in hot compresses for pain or inflammation with respect to lungs and stomach. 

One scientific study asserts that feverfew or, more exactly, its chemical constituent parthenolide, is not effective as an asthma therapy on its own (Arikan-Ayyildiz et al., 2017). This study, however, focused on animals and on isolating a chemical constituent, not upon the herb as a whole, and its applications and refined conclusions remain to be seen. 

Feverfew, which possesses a peculiarly strong scent, has also been used traditionally to purify the air (Grieve, 1971). It is this practice of air purification that Cherokee herbalist J.T. Garrett—who also says that feverfew is a great plant to grow indoors—mentions along with the alleviation of asthma and allergies, thus presenting another avenue of preparation to support these conditions.

With respect to the nervous system, feverfew has been used traditionally for a variety of indications on a spectrum including tinnitus, vertigo, nausea, and vomiting, all of which may have connections to the nerves (Arikan-Ayyildiz et al., 2017). In France, feverfew can be found popularly applied to those with cases of insomnia and herbalist Matthew Wood (2008) has mentioned it in relation to panic attacks.

Amelioration of low mood is also a recurrent feature of feverfew’s historical profile; Hildegard von Bingen (2001) remarked that it drew out bad humors when eaten often, creating good ones instead, while herbal authors of the Renaissance like Parkinson (1640) and Gerard (1636) tell us that it was used for melancholy and sadness, as well as for those who are “pensive, and without speech” (p. 653). These indications were generally echoed again in the early 20th century (Grieve, 1971). One scientific study, although thus far conducted only on animals, has confirmed these traditional anxiolytic and anti-depressant-like effects of feverfew and positively suggested the herb’s potential in this regard (Cárdenas et al., 2017).

Feverfew’s analgesic action has also been traditionally used for various types of pain support, for example, from toothache (Pareek et al., 2011). Grieve suggests an infusion of the flowers left until cold and then taken to dull sensitivity to pain in a nervous disposition (Grieve, 1971). The analgesic properties of feverfew have been recently studied with respect to extracts from the flowers alone and it was concluded that a flower extract is capable of acting as a powerful anodyne for various types of pain: inflammatory, articular, neuropathic, and acute (Di-Cesare Mannelli et al., 2015). 

Following upon these indications for pain and inflammation is a newfound focus in science upon feverfew and arthritic conditions. As discussed above, feverfew is anti-inflammatory and has been found to inhibit both degranulation and phagocytosis, which indicates that the herb might be used more widely for its anti-inflammatory properties (Bone & Mills, 2013). The fact that feverfew can inhibit phagocytosis as well as degranulation gives it increased potential as an anti-inflammatory agent; this may be why it was also used traditionally for allergies.

In Dr. Stewart Johnson’s clinical trial on migraines (mentioned above), one of the interesting outcomes was that those individuals taking feverfew who also had arthritis found their symptoms improved (Bone & Mills, 2013). It has been mentioned historically as employed for pains in the face and ears of rheumatic or dyspeptic individuals (Grieve, 1971). 

A later 1989 trial on rheumatoid arthritis, however, found no promising conclusions for feverfew and arthritis improvement (Pattrick, Heptinstall, & Doherty), but it should be noted that study participants only had exposure to dried feverfew, when fresh is commonly held to be far more effective. It has been suggested that arthritis would be similar to ague fits (see above) in humoral philosophy, that arthritis might share a common pathway with migrainous neuralgia in immunological terms, and that feverfew’s impact on platelets as well as its secretory action have possible implications for further study (Barker, 2001). 

Feverfew, which is also a vermifuge as well as antiparasitic, has been traditionally used as an insect repellent. This traditional Western use apparently carried over with the herb’s introduction to North America; a tribal elder reminded Cherokee herbalist J.T. Garrett (2003) that feverfew was earlier used to ward off insects:

“…[it] was used as a ‘bugbane’ to repel insects. The leaf would be placed in bean bread dough as a way to take it without upsetting the intestines” (p. 72).

The detail about the intestines is instructive; in a 1980s study of over 270 migraine sufferers, 21% ceased taking feverfew due to side effects, many of which pertained to gastrointestinal issues (Bone & Mills, 2013). Feverfew has in fact been traditionally used for digestive problems such as stomachaches (Pareek et al., 2001; Tierra, 1988). We find it mentioned in the 17th century as being used for wind and colic in the stomach, taken as a libation with wine or mixed with wine and oil and applied externally to the stomach (Parkinson, 1640).

This makes sense; feverfew possesses carminative and analgesic actions, as well as bitter principles. While the use of feverfew for digestive complaints has fallen out of prominence in Western herbalism, in Chinese medicine feverfew is notably associated with the stomach meridian/organ as well as the lung and liver (Holmes, 2006; Tierra, 1988). 

The insecticidal usage of feverfew is elsewhere extended to cover vermin, fleas, scabies, and lice bites (Wood, 2008). Mrs. Grieve (1971) supplies a recipe:

“A tincture made from Feverfew and applied locally immediately relieves the pain and swelling caused by bites of insects and vermin. It is said that if two teaspoonfuls of tincture are mixed with ½ pint of cold water, and all parts of the body likely to be exposed to the bites of insects are freely sponged with it, they will remain unassailable” (p. 310).

Like many other herbs, feverfew has received much recent attention in cancer research. As previously mentioned, the herb is high in sesquiterpene lactones—and these constituents have been shown as key fighters in cancer for several decades now. Bone & Mills (2013) remark further on feverfew’s sesquiterpene lactone parthenolide:

“A unique property is its ability to selectively induce cell death in cancer cells, sparing healthy cells. It also seems to have the potential to target certain cancer stem cells, such as breast and prostate” (p. 570).

In terms of preparations, feverfew is preferably used fresh either just before flowering or during flowering. It can also be found available as freeze-dried or dried herb, and in capsules, tablets, or liquid extraction. 

The amount of its very active constituent parthenolide fluctuates sometimes wildly; one study conducted in the United States focused on four different feverfew products and found that none of them contained the level of the constituent displayed on the label, although the instability of the constituent might have had a role to play in outcome of this study (Bone & Mills, 2013).

Feverfew has also found its way to the table: at one time it was cooked with eggs and eaten with gusto in Italy, rather like tansy (Tanacetum vulgare) (Parkinson, 1640). Feverfew is found in herbalist Michael Tierra’s (1988) Formula 9, which is composed of ten herbs and used for wind in the head and blood stagnation, or in colds/flus, headaches, and nervousness. 

Given the popular focus of the last half-century, it is easy to pigeonhole feverfew as “the migraine herb.” Despite an otherwise quieter presence in contemporary practice, the spectrum of feverfew’s many applications—showcased by its multi-faceted history, in which it was once a primary ally for uterine and reproductive wellbeing, a key balancer of the nervous and digestive systems, an easer of pain and the discomfort of infections, an aid for respiratory difficulty, and a useful repellent—leaves the door open to new discoveries. 

In addition, we’ll doubtless be hearing more about feverfew as its traditional  indications for inflammatory conditions such as arthritis and asthma are resurrected in current science and as more of its role in cancer support continues to be revealed.



Adult Dose

Infusion: 3-9 grams (Tierra, 1988); 8-14 grams (Holmes, 2006)
Tincture: 2-4 ml, 1:4 (Holmes, 2006)

With respect to migraines in particular, dosage will vary not only with quality of the herb, which can be considerable, but also with severity/frequency of migraine episodes (Bone & Mills, 2013).

Safety

Feverfew is generally considered safe but there are caveats for a number of reasons: the strength of the herb over time, allergic reactions due to the fact that the herb belongs to the Asteraceae/Compositae family, its uterine-stimulant action, impacts upon blood clotting, and interaction with certain drugs. 

Feverfew is generally considered an herb of medium-strength and it has been suggested that long-term use in high doses as a single should be avoided (Holmes, 2006). Otherwise there are no current restrictions for long-term use and some sources remark that in fact clinical conclusions suggest a usage of 4-6 months prior to any beneficial effects (Bone & Mills, 2013).

Some individuals who have taken feverfew long-term (such as migraine sufferers) have had the side-effect of “post-feverfew syndrome,” which encompasses a host of effects or an increase in conditions that are often the very opposite of issues that the herb initially aided: the syndrome may include headache, nausea/vomiting, stiff joints, and disturbed sleep (Bone & Mills, 2013).

Mouth ulcers are a commonly observed side effect of taking feverfew in dry, fresh, or tablet form (Brinker, 2010; Mabey et al., 1988). 

Prominent herbalists have also published cautionary advice from their own practices: herbalist Michael Tierra (1988) does not advocate feverfew for migraines in a person with a weak or deficient state. Herbalist Rosemary Gladstar (2001) asserts that women with heavy menstrual flow or cramps during menstruation should not use it. .

In terms of direct contraindications, there may be allergic hypersensitivity; the fresh plant causes contact dermatitis in some individuals because it is a member of the Asteraceae/Compositae family. As such, individuals with known sensitivities to this plant family should avoid feverfew.

Note also a direct contraindication in pregnancy due to the herb’s uterine-stimulating nature. 

Avoid at least one week before surgery or in bleeding disorders; there is speculative inhibition of platelet aggregation (Brinker, 2010). Therefore, there may be interaction with anticoagulant drugs (aspirin, Warfarin, etc.). 

Parthenolide, a major chemical constituent of feverfew, is known to interact with certain cancer drugs (Brinker, 2010).
