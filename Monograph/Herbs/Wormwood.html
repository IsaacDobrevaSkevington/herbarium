Wormwood

Artemisia absinthium. Synonyms: Artemisia absinthium var. insipida, Absinthium vulgare, Artemisia baldaccii, Artemisia inodora, Artemisia kulbadica, Artemisia rehan, Artemisia rhaetica

Geographic Distribution

Wormwood is native to temperate Asia, North Africa, and throughout Europe except the far north (Bruneton, 1995), and is widely naturalized across the northern United States, Canada, Greenland, South America, and Australia (Carey, 1994; Royal Botanic Gardens Kew [RBGK], n.d). Wormwood is cultivated in many parts of the world with temperate climates and commercially produced in regions including the former USSR, the Balkans, Hungary, and Poland (Evans, 2009).

Botanical Description

Wormwood is an herbaceous, perennial plant supported by a woody rootstock. The multibranched stem of this shrubby herb is firm, almost woody at the base, and grows up to 5 feet (1.5 m) tall (Kybal, 1980; Rose & O’Reilly, 2006). The alternate 1-4 inch (2.5-10.2 cm) leaves are covered with silky hairs and shaped with many blunt lobes of a greenish grey color. Tiny pale yellow tubular flowers occur in late summer in loose panicles that sit in the axils of the leaves. The leaves and flowers have an acrid smell when crushed and taste very bitter. Characteristic features of the microscopy are the T-shaped trichomes on both leaf epidermi (Evans, 2009). Seeds are small and dispersed by wind or animals. Each stem can produce up to 50,000 seeds. The herb is often found growing in dry waste places, roadsides, and poor soils (Deans & Kennedy, 2002)

Key Constituents

Sesquiterpene lactones (0.15-0.4%) (absinthin and artabsin) (European Scientific Cooperative on Phytotherapy [ESCOP], 2003; Evans, 2009), essential oils (0.2-1.5%): (α-thujone, β-thujone, trans-sabinyl acetate, cis-epoxyocinemene, chrysanthenyl acetate, linalyl acetate, chamazulene), flavonol glycosides, phenolic acids, tannins, and homoditerpene peroxides (Evans, 2009).

Sustainability

Wormwood is classified as an invasive species in the United States and easily invades open and disturbed sites where it can create a problem in native grasslands, pastures, and fields by outcompeting other desirable plants (Evans & Eckhardt, 1987). According to the IUCN Red List, Artemisia absinthium’s population is stable, although several other species bear sustainability concerns;  Madeira wormwood (Artemisia argentea) is listed as Vulnerable in its native range in Portugal and Artemisia molinieri is Endangered in its home in southern France.

Harvesting Guidelines

Wormwood can be harvested twice a year, during late spring and full bloom (Simon et al., 1984). The ideal time to harvest depends on the intended use of the herb. Research has confirmed that the largest amount of bitter compounds occur around September when the plant is in full bloom, while essential oils peak in June and July (Schneider & Mielke, 1978). However, the leaves deteriorate in quality once it starts to bloom (Tobyn et al., 2016). The perennial herb can be harvested in its entirety by cutting at the base. Alternatively, the leaves can be partially harvested in the spring and the flowering branches in the summer. In order to obtain good quality dried material, wormwood should be dried at room temperature because the essential oils already start to deteriorate at temperatures of 95 °F (35 °C) (Simon et al., 1984). The bitter compounds, however, are relatively stable after drying and storage.

Uses

Wormwood belongs to the genus Artemisia, which includes around 400 species (Wilcox, 2009). Many herbs in this genus are known for their health benefits and ritual uses, and are of great cultural importance. For example, sweet Annie (A. annua) is an antimalarial, and tarragon (A. dracunculus) is well known for its culinary purposes.

Wormwood, already a classic herb in late antiquity, has been used for many centuries as a bitter tonic, stomachic, vermifuge, and stimulant, but also in the production of liqueurs (e.g., vermouth and absinthe). With its powerful taste, wormwood is amongst the main bitter tonics used in modern Western herbalism (Mills & Bone, 2005). According to the European Medicine Agency (2017), its main uses include anorexia, atonic dyspepsia, and worm infestation.

The genus name Artemisia was named after the goddess Artemis, the Greek name for Diana, who, together with her brother Apollo, the god of healing, is said to have used Artemisia to eradicate a feverous plague in the city of Selinus, Sicily. This legend is commemorated on the Selinus coin (5th century BCE) (Wright, 2002).

However, according to Pliny the elder (23-79 CE), Artemisia was named Parthenis before Artemisia II of Caria (4th century BCE) named the plant species after herself (Bostock & Riley, 1855). The Persian satrap (governor) of Halicarnassus acquired sole royal power after her husband’s death, and was a feared ruler and military commander, leading the Carian forces and battleships (Penrose, 2016). However, Artemisia II is often described and depicted as the grieving wife of King Mausolus; according to legend, she imbibed his ashes in liquid as an expression of sorrow and devotion. Varieties of this legend, including that Artemisia II was a healer and botanist, are repeated in several modern herbal works, but this is rooted in the gender politics of influential Renaissance herbal writers such as Gerard and Dodoens (Laroche, 2009), and reveals how interpretations of plant names are rarely politically neutral.

Absinthium comes from the Greek word apsinthion meaning “unenjoyable,” reflecting the very bitter taste of the herb (Sauerhoff, 2001). Some translations state the meaning as  “without sweetness” (Nikhat, 2013) or “unusable” (Adams, 2004).

Often, the common names for herbs indicate their uses—the general consensus is that the name wormwood suggests its use against worms. However, the exact origin of the common name is unclear. According to one theory, wormwood is derived from the West Germanic word wermod (or other spelling variations) and represents a combination of the words for “man” and “courage,” indicating its use as an aphrodisiac (Weekley, 1921). This interpretation, however, was likely influenced by the absinthe epidemic of that time. Sauerhoff (2001) proposes that the name may refer to the warming properties of the herb, while other herbal writers have suggested that the origin of the name wormwood means “mind-preserver.” Sauer and Krischke (2009) offer an etymological explanation for the latter, but this is based on a very liberal interpretation and wordplay. The origin of the frequently cited common name “green ginger” is unclear but it may refer to the herb’s ability to promote digestion. 

The use of wormwood dates back centuries. The earliest records appear in the Ebers Papyrus (16th century BCE), the largest complete surviving medical scroll originating in Ancient Egypt. According to an English translation of the German interpretation of the scrolls, recipes containing wormwood were used to soothe headaches, constipation, diseased toes, itching of the skin, and pain in the abdomen when accompanied by tired eyes and a blocked nose (Bryan, 1930). However, these relatively detailed descriptions need to be regarded carefully since the University of Leipzig, where the original papyrus is stored, states that 70-80% of the preparation’s ingredients are not identified, which makes it difficult to reproduce.

Pliny the Elder (23-79 CE) recognized the versatile uses of wormwood, describing 48 preparations to ease nausea, reduce flatulence, strengthen the stomach, and mitigate scorpion bites (Bostock & Riley, 1855). The use of wormwood as an antidote to poison has also been recorded by Dioscorides (40-90 CE) who suggested it in instances of various kinds of poisoning, such as those caused by hemlock, mushrooms, shrew bites, and weever fish stings (Tobyn et al., 2016). Interestingly, a recent preliminary animal study showed that a methanolic extract of wormwood significantly inhibited snake venom-induced inflammation (Nalbantsoy et al., 2013).

Furthermore, Dioscorides suggested wormwood for earaches, to regulate menstruation, and for sore throat (Tobyn et al., 2016). Both Pliny and Dioscorides disapproved of using wormwood juice internally, as it would upset the stomach and possibly lead to a headache (Choffnes, 2016)—a view that would change significantly during the Middle Ages.

Wormwood was a common herb grown in the hortulus (herb garden) of medieval monasteries. Walahfrid Strabo (9th century CE) describes its use against headaches through applications of poultices drenched in the fresh juice mixed with wine (Stoffler, 2002). Saint Hildegard von Bingen (1098–1179 CE), a well-known German Benedictine abbess and scholar, called wormwood “the most important master against all exhaustions” (principales magistra ad omnes languores). One of her well-known recipes is the wormwood wine made with the juice of fresh wormwood herb, wine, and honey. This recipe is traditionally used as a tonic, digestive, liver and bile stimulant, and to lift melancholy (Hertza & Strehlow, 2003). The latter is rooted in the theory of humorism, which was dominant at that time. According to that theory, the melancholic temperament was believed to be accompanied by slow digestion, appetite, and metabolism.

Interestingly, in Bulgaria, wormwood, called pelin, is also the name of an alcoholic tonic traditionally made by macerating wormwood in red wine or vinegar as described by Saint Ivan Rilski (876 CE). This beverage has been used as a tonic for general strengthening (Nedelcheva, 2012), and is still part of folk traditions today.

In the traditional Greco-Arab medicine system of Unani Tibb, recognized in India and elsewhere in southern Asia, wormwood is used in formulas to support hepatitis, edema, and fever (Szopa et al., 2020). Chinese medicine employs wormwood for various uses including for its anticancer properties and ability to decrease angiogenesis (Szopa et al., 2020). In Pakistan, wormwood whole herb is employed as an antihelmintic for children (Batiha et al., 2020). Wormwood has been demonstrated in studies to show anti-malarial effects, a traditional use of the herb’s aerial parts in Cuba, Turkey, and Tunisia (Batiha et al., 2020). 

Wormwood is amongst the main bitter herbs used in Western herbalism and is used for the entire digestive tract (Fintelmann & Weiss, 2002; Mills & Bone, 2000). Sesquiterpene lactone dimers such as absinthin are one of the most bitter compounds known. Bitters stimulate the appetite and are useful in conditions associated with reduced appetite. The bitter taste triggers a sensory response in the mouth, sending a message to the gut. This gives rise to the release of the digestive hormone gastrin, and supports the release of digestive juices from the pancreas, duodenum, and liver (Baumann et al., 1975). According to McMullan et al. (2015), wormwood increases tonus in the vascular resistance vessels and facilitates rather than stimulates digestive activity. Wormwood has demonstrated the ability to aid in other cardiovascular concerns: it mitigates the symptoms of artherosclerosis by regulating inflammation and lipid metabolism (Yang et al., 2025). 

Preliminary murine studies have examined wormwood’s potential as an antimicrobial, non-bacteria-resistant alternative to antibiotics; though further research and human trials are needed, wormwood demonstrates broad-spectrum antibacterial and antifungal activity (Liu et al., 2023).

Wormwood is used to aid the liver’s detoxification process by increasing the flow of bile (Fintelmann & Weiss, 2002). This is especially important because many health problems have their roots in an overworked liver (Hoffmann, 2003). Wormwood is also traditionally used in Uighur medicine for ameliorating liver diseases (Amat et al., 2010). An in vivo study strongly implies the protective effect of wormwood extract against acute liver injury and attributes this to its antioxidative or immunomodulatory activity (Amat et al., 2010). Winston (2003) confirms that wormwood increases the production of pancreatic and small intestine enzymes and hydrochloric acid, and is useful in cases of liver stagnation, jaundice, anorexia, and dyspepsia. 

The health of the gut is highly correlated with mood and nervous system health. Interestingly, two clinical studies not only support the use of wormwood in digestive tract imbalance but have demonstrated wormwood’s ability to reduce inflammatory bowel disease and depression. One double-blind trial assessed 40 individuals with Crohn’s disease who were on steroids and found that those who received an herbal formula containing wormwood experienced an improvement in both their Crohn’s symptoms and in their mood and quality of life, as compared to the placebo (Omer et al., 2007). Another controlled trial examined the effects of powdered wormwood on 10 individuals with Crohn’s disease for 6 weeks, finding that those who took the herb had an accelerated clinical response and improved mood in comparison to the placebo (Krebs et al., 2010). These studies suggest that wormwood was superior to placebo in not only inducing remission of but also forestalling recurrence of post-operative Crohn’s disease (Krebs et al., 2010; Omer et al., 2007; Rahimi et al., 2013).

Bitters have a priming effect on the upper digestive tract and work by slow visceral reflex, therefore Mills & Bone (2000) suggest taking wormwood preparations 15 minutes before a meal, slowly sipping the liquid in order to prolong the stimulation of the reflex. However, ESCOP (2003) and European Medicine Agency (2017) suggest taking preparations for dyspeptic complaints after a meal, and before meals only to help with loss of appetite. If the preparations are to be taken as an anthelmintic, the powdered herb can be taken in pill or capsule form (Hoffmann, 2003). 

Preliminary in vitro and in vivo research suggests that wormwood may have a propensity for reducing blood sugar by blocking the action of pancreatic α-amylase enzyme (Hbika et al., 2022). One evaluation concluded that wormwood’s flavonoid content significantly blocked the proliferation of cancer cells and displays meaningful antitumor activity; wormwood has been used to inhibit the growth of colorectal, breast, and cervical cancer (He et al., 2023). Preliminary observations of wormwood on the human oral carcinoma cell line found that the herb also decreased the viability of the cancer cells but not the normal periodontal cells (Tsamesidis et al., 2024).) 

Furthermore, Hoffmann (2003) classifies wormwood as an antirheumatic herb that can improve the quality of life for those with rheumatic problems. A recent randomized double-blind controlled trial with 90 osteoarthritis patients indicated that topical effects of wormwood ointment significantly decreased pain scores and improved physical function similar to the conventional medication piroxicam gel (Basiri et al., 2017).

Strong bitter herbs are often associated with anthelmintic properties, and while Dioscorides or Galen do not mention this use (Tobyn et al., 2016), many classical and renaissance texts, as well as modern herbalists, do. While no clinical trials have been conducted, several animal studies have supported this use. For example, Esfandiari et al. (2007) showed that wormwood extract decreased the number of Syphacia parasite ova and the authors conclude that wormwood could act as a source of novel antiparasitic drugs. Tariq et al. (2009) suggest that A. absinthium extracts are a promising alternative to the commercially available anthelmintics for the eradication of nematodes in sheep. Interestingly, they note that an ethanolic extract worked better than the aqueous extract, mainly because alcohol helps solubility of the anthelmintic principles (Tariq et al., 2009).

In folk tradition, wormwood was often used to keep mice and other pests away from grain storage (De Cleene & Lejeune, 2000). Pliny writes that wormwood was added to ink in order to prevent mice from eating the paper (Bostock & Riley, 1855). The insecticidal and antifeedant properties of wormwood extract used as a biological control against some important crop pests such as Crocidolomia binotalis have been confirmed in field studies (Facknath & Kawol, 1993).

Wormwood is used in Cherokee tradition for gastrointestinal concerns, as an alterative for internal blood cleansing, externally for swelling in the feet and legs, and to stimulate the metabolism, according to Indigenous ethnobotanist J.T. Garrett (2003). Wormwood plays an important role in several Cherokee herbal blends. In formulas, it is used in combination with aniseed (Pimpinella anisum), angelica (Angelica archangelica), and Indian root (Aralia racemosa) as a digestive stimulant and to support the galbladder, and among members of coastal Indigenous tribes, it is blended with blue vervain (Verbena hastata), which Garrett calls wild hyssop, to do the same (Garrett, 2003). Blended with speedwell (Veronica officinalis), wormwood addresses bladder issues and gout (Garrett, 2003). According to Gladys Tantaquidgeon (1972), the Mohegan people use the leaves of wormwood for their vermifuge properties. Aboriginal rights advocate Dorothy Kennedy, with her colleagues, notes that the Okanagan-Colville Indians of British Columbia and Washington use wormwood roots for gastrointestinal distress (Turner et al., 1980). A tea or decoction of wormwood twigs is used internally while a poultice of the leaves is applied to the chest externally for the common cold or flu (Turner et al., 1980). According to Kennedy and her fellow researchers, the plant is applied vaginally postpartum to help repair after a birth, and a poultice applied as a wash to support mending of broken bones; the twigs, decocted or infused, are also used by the Mohegan people for tuberculosis and veneral disease (Turner et al., 1980).

Apart from its herbal uses, wormwood is mentioned in 21 different spells within the Papyri Graecae Magicae, Greek magical papyri that contain magic spells, rituals, and formulas (2nd century BCE to the 5th century CE). According to Rinella (2010), the herb was associated with the divine, and all plant parts were used, either worn during rituals, burned as incense, mixed with ink, drunk, or applied to the skin of the magician. In several European countries, wormwood has also been used for rituals of magic. It was one of the main herbs used for making the herbal bush, traditionally bound together on the 15th of August and believed to bring protection against witches, illness, and bad luck (De Cleene & Le Jeune, 2000; Teirlinck, 1926).

However, wormwood has not always been regarded in a positive light. Padosch et al. (2006) report that the Old Testament of the Christian Bible mentions wormwood several times where the herb represents a curse (Jeremiah 9:15), calamity (Lamentations 3:15), or injustice (Amos 5:7). It is not entirely certain that the herb mentioned is indeed Artemisia absinthium (Cleene & Lejeune, 2000). Nonetheless, there are recordings from medieval church traditions where after a period of hardship, such as prolonged drought, a procession of young women wore wormwood in their hair as a symbol of their bitter ordeal (De Cock, 1920).

In the late 18th century, wormwood became notorious as a result of the popular alcoholic drink, absinthe. Absinthe was developed by the Frenchman Henri-Louis Pernod in 1805, who took the original recipe from Switzerland to Pontarlier, France, and started a distillery. Originally, the wormwood drink was given to soldiers in the Algerian war (1844-1847) as a prophylaxis against malaria and intestinal parasites, and also to increase morale amongst the soldiers (Lachenmeier et al., 2004).

The pale green spirit dubbed “the green fairy” became popular with the aristocracy and artists in late 19th century Europe and it was said to evoke new views, different experiences, and unique feelings (Arnold, 1989). In the 1850s absinthe use started to come under scrutiny, because after regular consumption a syndrome named “absinthism” became apparent and included convulsions, blindness, hallucinations, and mental deterioration (Lachenmeier et al., 2004). By the 20th century, consumption of absinthe was so widespread that it was deemed an epidemic. Absinthe consumption was held responsible for all sorts of diseases, even if there was seemingly no connection to the spirit. Numerous posters were distributed depicting “the green fairy” as an evil figure bringing confusion and death. While these images were used to bring about the prohibition of the drink, it also added to the mysterious reputation of absinthe, which lives on to this day. 

For a long time, toxic levels of thujone were the suspected cause of absinthism, and this is often repeated in recent herbal literature. However, in recent years a more nuanced light has been thrown on this matter. While severe intoxications in humans have been reported after consumption of wormwood oil (Milett et al., 1981), based on the current available evidence, thujone concentrations in both pre-ban and modern absinthes are unlikely to cause detrimental health effects other than those encountered in common alcoholism. The only consistent conclusion that can be drawn from those 19th century studies about absinthism is that wormwood oil but not absinthe is a potent agent in instigating seizures. A critical literature review of studies concerning absinthism can be found in Padosch et al. (2006).

Several authors have pointed out that the popularity of the expensive liquor led to cheap adulterated absinthe flooding the market (Arnold, 1989; Lachenmeier et al., 2004; Nesbitt & Prance, 2005). Poisonous ingredients such as industrial alcohol were used and mixed with copper and antimony, among other harmful substances; copper is readily absorbed into the body, especially in combination with alcohol, with terrible consequences (Arnold, 1989). By 1912, public outcry against absinthe consumption led to bans in parts of Europe and the United States (Padosh et al., 2006). Under current U.S. laws, absinthe can be legally sold by licensed distillers as long it is “thujone free,” which is defined as containing no more than 10 parts per million thujone (Snider, 2018). However, because home distillation of alcohol is illegal in the U.S. and many other countries, making distilled absinthe recipes at home is illegal as well; absinthe enthusiasts insist that simple maceration of herbs in alcohol doesn’t make true absinthe because distillation is required to remove some of the most bitter constituents of wormwood (Wormwood Society, 2018). Many modern absinthe recipes are carefully guarded trade secrets, and different distilleries have their own specific combination of herbs (e.g., parsley, chamomile, peppermint, juniper, coriander, veronica, and angelica).

Adult Dose

Infusion: 1-1.5 g finely ground aerial parts in 150 mL boiling water, 2-3x/day (ESCOP, 2003).

Juice: 5 mL, 2x/day (European Medicines Agency [EMA], 2017).

Powder: 0.76 g, 3x/day (EMA, 2017).

Tincture: 1-4 mL (1:1, 25%) 3x/day (Hoffmann, 2003).

Safety

The ESCOP monographs (2003) consider the toxicological risk from conventional use of wormwood preparations to be very low, provided that the suggested dosage range is not exceeded. However, there are several known contraindications that need to be taken into consideration. Persons with hypersensitivity to the active substance(s) and other plants of the Asteraceae family need to take precautions. Clients with gallstones and other biliary disorders should seek medical advice before taking wormwood preparations (EMA, 2017). Hoffmann (2003) suggests that wormwood is also contraindicated for persons with stomach hyperacidity.

Several sources indicate that wormwood should not be used during pregnancy because it is an abortifacient; the active constituent thujone is known for its uterine stimulating activity (EMA, 2017; Mills & Bone, 2005). While there is no clinical research on the effects of wormwood in vulnerable groups (e.g., elderly people and children), the herb is not advised for children and should be avoided during lactation (EMA, 2017). The leaf and stem is noted as a possible galactogogue in The Nursing Mother’s Herbal and given a safety rating of ‘E’, advisable to avoid; the essential oil is toxic (Humphrey, 2003).

Toxicological characteristics of thujone, the active principle in wormwood that can have severe toxic effects, have been studied intensively. The isolated compound, thujone is a reversible GABAa receptor modulator; this results in excitation and convulsions (Hold et al., 2000; Pelkonen et al., 2013).

One of the difficulties with wormwood is that the content of thujone can vary widely in individual plants and depends on environmental factors, as well as chemotypes (Evans 2009; Lachenmeier et al., 2004). Thujone levels in the essential oil can range from 0 to 90% (Thi Huong et al., 2018).

According to Hoffmann (2003), the toxicity is cumulative. The European Medicines Agency (2017) set the daily exposure to less than 6 mg per day. To put this into perspective, research by Lachenmeier and Uebelacker (2010) confirms that dangerous levels of thujone intake are hard to reach; 2-20 cups of wormwood tea would represent 0.11 mg/kg body weight/day. Short-term supplemental use can be regarded as safe; however, wormwood should not be taken longer than 3-4 weeks (ESCOP, 2003) and Winston (2003) agrees that wormwood should never be taken long term. 

Acute renal failure has been reported in a man who ingested 10 mL of wormwood essential oil after mistaking it for the absinthe spirit (Weisbord et al., 1997). To date, there has been one established case of adverse drug interaction with warfarin (Acikgoz & Acikgoz, 2013).
