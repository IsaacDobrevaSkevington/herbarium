Blue Cohosh

Caulophyllum thalictroides, C. giganteum

Print← BloodrootBlue Lotus →

At a glance

Energetics

CoolingDrying

Taste

BitterSlightly acrid with a sweet aftertaste

Actions

Anti-inflammatoryAntispasmodicEmeticEmmenagogueOxytocicUterine tonic

Ways to use

CapsulesDecoctionFluid ExtractTincture
Common name
Blue cohosh, blueberry root, papoose root, caulophyllum, blue ginseng, yellow ginseng
Family
Berberidaceae
Parts used
Root and rhizome.

Native To

Blue cohosh is native to North America (Romm & Upton, 2012) and thrives in mixed hardwood habitats (Cech, 2002), especially in areas where there is plenty of shade and rich, moist soil (United Plant Savers [UpS], n.d.). The plant grows well on north-facing slopes and in the shade of mature trees and rocky cliffs since even morning sun exposure can negatively affect plant growth (Cech, 2002).

Geographic Distribution

Blue cohosh can be found mostly in the southern portions of the eastern hardwood forest in North America but reaches as far north as Manitoba (UpS, n.d.). Plant populations are most concentrated in the Allegheny mountains and New England (Cech, 2002) but can also be found throughout the Midwest, and south into South Carolina and Arkansas (UpS, n.d.).

Botanical Description

Blue cohosh is an upright, bushy perennial that grows up to 3 feet tall; it has large, three-lobed leaves which are purplish in color in the spring (University of Texas, 2019) and covered in a distinct bluish film (Foster & Duke, 2014). Plants flower from April to June (Foster & Duke, 2014) with purplish-brown to greenish-yellow blooms (University of Texas, 2019) which are grouped in loosely branched clusters. Blue cohosh has striking, fleshy, blue berries (Chevallier, 2016) roughly the size of a pea, and these contain a single seed (Cech, 2002). The cylindrical root is light brown and resembles a crooked finger covered in small rootlets that radiate from all directions; when plants are dormant, the main bud is found on the upper surface of the root and looks like a cat’s claw (Cech, 2002).

Key Constituents

Alkaloids including caulophylline (Skenderi, 2003), magnoflorine (UpS, n.d.), and sparteine (Bone, 2003); saponins (caulosaponin); resins (Skenderi, 2003).

Sustainability

AT-RISK PLANT. SEEK CULTIVATED SOURCES OR ALTERNATIVE PLANTS. United Plant Savers (UpS), a United States-based plant conservation organization, includes blue cohosh on its “At-Risk” plant list with a threatened status comparable to ramps (Allium tricoccum), American ginseng (Panax quinquefolius), and goldenseal (Hydrastis canadensis) (UpS, 2022). 

While some commercial cultivation exists, blue cohosh is mainly wild harvested (Chevallier, 2016). Forest logging is a threat to plant populations since blue cohosh requires moist soils and the deep shade found mainly in mature forests (UpS, n.d.). Blue cohosh spreads mainly by seed in its natural environment and plants require at least 5 years to reach maturity (Cech, 2002). The slow maturation means that overharvesting is also a threat since plant numbers recover slowly from disturbance and the root is the part of the plant harvested for herbal use (UpS, n.d.).

Since the majority of blue cohosh available on the market comes from the wild and demand can negatively impact plant populations, some herbalists use herbal analogues which have a similar action and encourage the same end result (Bothwell, 2002). When a uterine tonic is desired, both motherwort (Leonurus cardiaca) aerial parts and raspberry (Rubus idaeus) leaf can be used as replacements in formulas (Bothwell, 2002). Both of these plants have significantly less complicated side effects, so these may be safer and easier alternatives to use instead of blue cohosh.

Harvesting Guidelines

Please consider cultivating blue cohosh if possible to ensure healthy populations of this important herb. For those with access to cultivated plants, blue cohosh is best harvested in the autumn before the plant goes dormant for the winter (Cech, 2002). The roots should be dug, washed, and dried in a warm place with good airflow, turning them often until they can be snapped cleanly; when dried properly, the roots retain their potency for up to 2 years (Cech, 2002).

While blue cohosh reproduces mainly by seed in the wild, plants can also be propagated by the root division of mature plants. This hastens the development of plants to mature in 3 years compared to 5 years when plants are propagated by seed (Cech, 2002).

Uses

So often plant names tell a story regarding who has used the plant, its appearance, or its habitat, and blue cohosh is no different. The species name thalictroides denotes that blue cohosh’s feathery foliage resembles the leaves of meadow rue (Thalictrum spp.) (University of Texas, 2019). The word cohosh means “rough” in the Algonquian languages spoken by Indigenous groups living in the plant’s habitat range and refers to the root’s gnarled appearance (Salmón, 2020). The Cherokee have used blue cohosh in a number of different ways; the root has been taken to stimulate menstruation, combat dehydration during labor, and for rheumatic complaints in the elderly (Garrett, 2003). The Mohegan of Connecticut have traditionally used blue cohosh for kidney issues, though even historically it was noted as being a rare plant (Tantaquidgeon, 2001). Amongst other Indigenous communities of the eastern United States, blue cohosh has been used as a laxative and febrifuge, and the leaves are applied topically for skin irritation due to poison oak exposure (Salmón, 2020).  

Amongst the Eclectic physicians practicing in the United States in the early 1900s, blue cohosh was considered to have a similar action to black cohosh (Actaea racemosa) (Felter, 1922). Although not interchangeable, the plants are synergistic and are often used together (Gladstar, 2008). Both were used by the Eclectics for rheumatoid pain and to facilitate contractions during childbirth (Felter, 1922). Previously, ergot (Claviceps spp.) was popular for facilitating labor, and both black and blue cohosh were seen as much safer and less damaging alternatives to use (Felter, 1922).

Since blue cohosh acts strongly on the uterus, it has been used for an array of chronic uterine issues (Foster & Duke, 2014). The Eclectic physician Dr. Harvey Felter (1922) used it specifically for uterine heaviness with soreness in the legs, and in other cases involving pelvic congestion. Modern herbalists such as Sharol Tilgner (2009) use the herb in much the same way. In her book Herbal Medicine From the Heart of the Earth, Tilgner (2009) suggests using it when individuals present with heavy, achy, and spasmodic uterine pain, alongside congestion and prolapsed tissues, pain in the lower back and thighs, and a dull frontal headache with thirst. She asks us to “consider this herb when there is reproductive tract debility arising from chronic inflammatory conditions [since it is] both tonifying to atonic uterine tissue while it is also relaxing to spastic uterine muscles” (Tilgner, 2009, p. 52). 

Naturopathic doctor Jill Stansbury (2018) considers blue cohosh a uterine emmenagogue capable of stimulating menstrual flow and uses it when menses are sluggish, slow to start, or scanty. This matches its traditional use for amenorrhea and dysmenorrhea amongst herbalists in the United Kingdom, denoted by its inclusion in the British Herbal Pharmacopoeia (British Herbal Medicine Association, 1983). It is also specifically indicated for conditions associated with uterine atony (Newall et al., 1996). The British herbalist David Hoffmann (2003) mirrors some of these indications when he classifies it as a uterine tonic. Although blue cohosh is primarily considered an ovo-uterine herb, it is also used for issues with the male reproductive tract (Chevallier, 2016). In an animal study involving isolated vas deferens tissue, blue cohosh caused low levels of muscle contraction due to its interaction with adrenergic receptors in the tissue (Leafbled, 2011, as cited in Romm & Upton, 2012). Its actions on the male genitourinary system have many applications ranging from orchitis to impotence (Tilgner, 2009). 

Blue cohosh has long been considered a classic partus preparator (Romm, 2014) and is a crucial ingredient in Mother’s Cordial (Felter, 1922), a formula historically taken in the final weeks of pregnancy to prepare the body for labor. Its reputation as a useful herb in late pregnancy resulted in its classification in the United States Pharmacopoeia from 1882-1905 as a labor inducer (Gladstar, 2008). Over 100 years later, blue cohosh is still sometimes used to facilitate labor, although significant safety concerns have been raised due to potential adverse effects on the fetus when used during pregnancy; see the safety section for more information. 

Some midwives use blue cohosh to help induce labor in cases of postterm pregnancy and to stimulate contractions during labor when they have stalled (Romm, 2014). A survey of midwives in 1999 showed that a tincture of blue cohosh was used frequently for labor induction with a typical dose of five drops taken every 4 hours or 10 drops every 2 hours to bring on contractions (McFarlin et al., 1999). Yet, this survey also noted that a number of midwives noticed increased meconium, fetal tachycardia, and the need for infant resuscitation when blue cohosh was ingested (McFarlin et al., 1999). Given the number of variables in the labors referenced by the midwives in the survey, it is difficult to distinguish whether these adverse effects were the direct result of taking blue cohosh or due to other factors (Romm, 2009). 

There is very limited clinical data that supports the traditional uses of blue cohosh. Some animal research demonstrates blue cohosh’s stimulating action on uterine contractions (Berger & DeGolier, 2008), and one of the plant’s isolated constituents (likely caulosaponin) was shown to increase muscle contractions in the uterus (Ferguson & Edwards, 1954; Romm, 2009). By its nature, muscle contraction encourages blood flow and may contribute to blue cohosh’s reputation as an herb that can promote blood flow to the pelvic region and help to lessen menstrual cramps (Gladstar, 2008). Sparteine has been shown to be oxytocic (Stubblefield et al., 1963) although it occurs in very low concentrations in most blue cohosh plants, and it’s unknown how much this constituent impacts the herb’s actions (Romm & Upton, 2012). 

Although blue cohosh is mostly associated with its action on the uterus, it also has an anti-inflammatory action and is used in cases of chronic inflammation as is seen in arthritis (Tilgner, 2009). Due to its complicated action on the heart, it has demonstrated both hypertensive and hypotensive actions (Romm, 2009), and has been used by some herbalists to normalize both systolic and diastolic pressure (Hoffmann, 2003). Its antispasmodic action on smooth muscles can benefit menstrual cramps as well as other types of abdominal cramping (Bone, 2003). 

Blue cohosh is frequently formulated with other herbs. For menstrual cramps, it may be combined with wild yam (Dioscorea villosa), hops (Humulus lupulus), and black haw (Viburnum prunifolium) (Hudson, 2008). Since fresh blue cohosh tends to be very acrid, combining it with a demulcent herb such as mullein (Verbascum thapsus) can make it more palatable (Cech, 2002).  

In its natural habitat, blue cohosh can be found growing alongside other shade-tolerant and water-loving understory plants such as Solomon’s seal (Polygonatum spp.), trillium (Trillium spp.), goldenseal (Hydrastis canadensis), ramps (Allium tricoccum), Jack-in-the pulpit (Arisaema triphyllum), spikenard (Aralia racemosa), and stoneroot (Collinsonia canadensis) (Cech, 2002). Along their more western range, plants can be found growing under alder (Alnus rubra) and big leaf maple (Acer macrophyllum) (Cech, 2002). Plant germination can be complicated and the seeds require consistent moisture, as well as the removal of the germination-inhibiting skin around the berry (Cech, 2002). Even with these ideal conditions, germination can still be unpredictable and take many months (Cech, 2002). 

The various species in the Caulophyllum genus are quite similar to each other and even interchangeable. The vast majority of the herbal literature is on C. thalictroides and C. robustum, the latter of which is native to China and elsewhere in East Asia (Xia et al., 2014). C. thalictroides is often confused with giant blue cohosh (C. giganteum), which has a similar leaf shape and growth pattern, but was recognized as its own species in 1997. Despite its similar appearance and range in eastern North America, C. giganteum is significantly larger in size, has consistently dark purple flowers, and flowers earlier than blue cohosh (UpS, n.d.). Dr. Jeanine Davis, who has done extensive research on blue cohosh and its growing habits and methods, notes that C. thalictroides and C. giganteum may be used interchangeably in herbalism (Davis & Persons, 2014). The two species have a history of interchangeable use for as long as the herb has been in use, and the American Herbal Pharmacopoeia and Therapeutic Compendium (Romm & Upton, 2012) states that their phytochemistry is the same. United Plant Savers (n.d.) hypothesizes that C. giganteum may contain fewer of the active compounds found in C. thalictroides, but conclusive researach has not yet been done.

Adult Dose

Low-dose herb for use only under professional supervision.

Tincture: 0.5-2.0 mL (1:5, 60%) up to 4x/day (Easley & Horne, 2016).

Liquid Extract: 0.5-1.0 mL (1:1, 70%) up to 3x/day (Newall et al., 1996).

Decoction: ½-1 cup (30g dried root in 1 liter water) 3x/day (Easley & Horne, 2016).

Capsule: 500 mg 2x/day (Easley & Horne, 2016).

Safety

Blue cohosh has complicated and potent phytochemistry and should only be used by skilled practitioners (Romm, 2014). Research indicates that the herb contains compounds that may restrict blood flow to a fetus and numerous adverse reactions have been documented (Jones & Lawson, 1998; Romm, 2014). Perinatal stroke, heart failure, and the failure of other organs have been reported in infants born with the use of blue cohosh during or before labor (Gardner & McGuffin, 2013).  

Caulosaponin, one of the constituents found in blue cohosh, stimulates contractions of the uterine muscles (Skenderi, 2003), so the herb should be avoided by those trying to become pregnant and pregnant individuals prior to their time of labor (Bone, 2003; Brinker, 2010). Aviva Romm (2014) advises that avoiding blue cohosh entirely during pregnancy is the safest course of action for most people; in cases of overdue pregnancy where labor stimulation is warranted, expert guidance of an experienced professional is needed to navigate the use of this herb due to safety issues. Blue cohosh should be avoided during lactation (Skenderi, 2003) and during heavy menstrual bleeding (Easley & Horne, 2016). Caution should be exercised with individuals with blood pressure concerns, as blue cohosh can act as both a hypo- and hypertensive (Romm, 2009). 

Handling fresh blue cohosh can cause contact dermatitis so care must be taken when harvesting and processing the plant (UpS, n.d.). The berries are poisonous and should not be ingested (Gladstar, 2008). When powdering the root a mask should be worn since the dust can severely irritate the upper respiratory tract (Cech, 2000). 

Overdose symptoms include nausea, vomiting, headache, dilated pupils, muscle weakness, and uncoordinated movement, and large doses can lead to convulsions, constriction of coronary blood vessels, and cardiovascular collapse (Tilgner, 2009).
