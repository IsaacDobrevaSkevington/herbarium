Oyster Mushroom

Pleurotus ostreatus

Native To

P. ostreatus is native to China (Piska et al., 2017).

Geographic Distribution

Oyster mushrooms are distributed throughout the world in both temperate and tropical forests (Stamets, 2000) wherever it can also meet its requirement for high light conditions (Piska et al., 2017). It is found all over the world with the exception of Antarctica due to its frigid climate (Piska et al., 2017).

Botanical Description

A distinguishing characteristic of oyster mushroom is that it is almost always found growing in clusters. In general, oyster mushrooms prefer broadleaf hardwoods (Stamets, 2000) and one may also find them growing on logs and stumps rather than upon the ground. Its favored woods include elm, oak, cottonwood, alder, and sycamore. Its fall and winter host in the United States is very often a maple tree, more specifically sugar maple, while its far less frequent spring hosts are likely to be aspen and poplar (Spahr, 2009).

The rather flat, fan-shaped, and sideways-growing cap of oyster mushrooms may range from 1 to 12 inches in diameter, while generally registering ½ to 1½  inches in thickness (Spahr, 2009). The cap of P. ostreatus is often white and can become brown or tan in color with age, while other Pleurotus species have a wide range of colors that may include grayish, pink, and grayish-blue (Sphar, 2009) as well as a sort of cinder brown (Becker, 1983). Beneath the caps are gills that are close, whitish but becoming yellowish with age, and descend all the way down to the base of the mushroom.

P. ostreatus overlaps in bunched layers with a very short—almost non-existent and sometimes completely absent—foot or stalk that is lateral or off-center, whitish in color, and solid. While young, the mushroom emits a kind of anise- or almond-like aroma but in ageing its odor may be described as unpleasant (Spahr, 2009; Stamets, 2000); the scent of almond/anise scent is due to its benzaldehyde content (Piska et al., 2017). The spores range from white to pale lilac or grey in color (Stamets, 2000).

Key Constituents

amino acids, lovastatin, ergothioneine, polysaccharides, ß-glucan pleuran (Piska et al., 2017)

Sustainability

Oyster mushrooms are often touted as a positive way to magnify healthy and sustainable lifestyles at an individual level; discarded waste from the kitchen, for example coffee grounds, can be used to grow the mushrooms with relative ease. The mushroom may also be used for restoring nutrient pathways in depleted environments and ecosystems, and breaking down toxic waste (Stamets, 2005). Oyster mushrooms are now shown to contribute to the richness of a forest ecosystem as they give back minerals and elements to the soil via the decomposition of dead wood (Stamets, 2000). Nonetheless, a concern that the mushroom might damage the forest has resulted in New Zealand (where P. ostreatus is not native) banning its importation (Hall, 2010).

Harvesting Guidelines

There are several oyster mushroom look-alikes, and extra caution is required when harvesting them from the wild—oyster mushroom is for advanced-level foragers only. Note that the following toxic mushrooms look similar to oyster mushroom: Pleurocybella porrigens, which may be distinguished as it is smaller and thinner and grows on conifers; and Clitocybe dealbata, which may be distinguished as it grows from the ground; and Omphalotus nidiformis, which grows in Japan and Australia, and may be distinguished by its bioluminescence.

Mainly wild harvested in the autumn, the mushrooms are quite perishable and generally have a poor shelf life—about 3 days in duration—and they should be placed in cool storage (between 0 and 2 degrees Celsius) within 5 hours of harvest (Satankar et al., 2018). They may also be dried, pickled, or canned post-harvest. 

The mushroom should be harvested when young and preferably when appearing in clusters, before they become acrid , dry, wilted, or cracked with age; once spores are produced in the gills, storability decreases sharply (Jones, 2013). Due to a high incidence of allergic reaction to spores, harvesters are advised to use masks as protection from potential allergic response (Stamets, 2000). When harvesting, aim to look for mushroom shelves that are slightly dry (Stamets, 2000) and use a knife and a basket to collect specimens as they are fragile.

Oyster mushrooms are renowned for absorbing both environmental toxins and heavy metals, so be extra vigilant as to where you collect specimens.

Uses

In terms of etymology, pleurotus originally comes from ancient Greek: pleur- is a prefix indicating “side”, derived from πλευρόν /pleuron, meaning both “rib” and, by extension, “sideways” orientation. Hence pleur- may refer to the notably sideways growth pattern of the mushroom. The second portion of the word, –otus, appears to derive from τό οὖς, ὠτός/otos referring to a resemblance to an “ear”, and is perhaps in relation to the shape of the fruited body. 

Ostreatus, meanwhile, is derived from the Latin, meaning “covered with oyster shells”, or “oyster-shaped”, and is another reference to the shape of the mushroom body. Hence Pleurotus ostreatus translates roughly as something like “sideways ear-like oyster-like” mushroom. It is commonly held that oyster mushrooms even taste a bit like oysters or something vaguely reminiscent of seafood, and their common grayish-white color can also be similar to that of raw oysters.

The oyster mushroom seems to have been described as early as 1774 in Austria (Stamets, 2005). Pleurotus, however, contains a huge number of species and strains that only began to receive new categorization and taxonomy in the mid-1990s and early 2000s (Stamets, 2005). In part because there was no surviving sample of its original collection, the taxonomy of oyster mushrooms remains unclear and in-progress, and P. ostreatus is so similar to other species such as P. pulmonarius that distinguishing them is nigh impossible macroscopically (Stamets, 2011).

Oyster mushroom is a popular culinary mushroom. Pleurotus ostreatus is commonly cultivated oyster mushroom, however a handful of different species are cultivated (e.g., “the king oyster” is a cultivated Mediterranean species, P. eryngii). Another species is P. populinus, which is also found in Maine and was previously considered a spring variant of P. ostreatus (Spahr, 2009). 

Oyster mushroom has been historically valued; a poem of the Sung Dynasty (CE 420-479) apparently praised the oyster mushroom as “the mushroom flower of heaven” (Hobbs, 1986). Even today its production is predominantly in Asia: China leads in the production of oyster mushrooms, with Korea, Japan, Taiwan, Thailand, and the Philippines following, although Italy is also now a grower (Satankar et al., 2018). Because it adapts to a wide variety of substrates, oyster mushroom now ranks as the second- or third-most widely cultivated mushroom in the world (Petre, 2016; Satankar et al., 2018).

The culinary use of oyster mushroom is frequent in Asian cuisine, where it is deemed a delicacy (Piska et al., 2017) and appears in various stir-fry, soup, and sauce recipes. In Asia, oyster mushroom has often been included as a meat substitute not just for its texture but also for its high protein content (Isokauppila, 2017). Oyster mushrooms are used in Chinese cuisine for the purposes of strengthening the qi of both Stomach and Spleen as well as for improving metabolism, and its calcium, phosphorus, and iron content seems to be about double the amount found in beef, chicken, or pork (Yang & Guo, 2015).

There is rather less cultural information available outside of Asia, although in Slovakia or the Czech Republic oyster mushroom is also valued as a meat substitute (Piska et al., 2017). In the Swat community of Pakistan, Pleurotus spp. is one of the most widely eaten and most highly ranked–in terms of taste appeal–wild mushrooms, where it is commonly consumed alongside vegetables, cooked in fat, or mixed into a sauce or soup dish (Hussain et al., 2023). Its use in cuisine in North America is extensive despite somewhat of a deficit in documentation on the subject, but the Kashaya Pomo group of Native peoples in what is today California are documented as using P. ostreatus as a food cooked on hot stones, fried, or baked in the oven (Goodrich & Lawson, 1980). 

In Mexico the mushroom has been cooked in stews or served fried (Hobbs, 1986). In an ethnomycological assessment of the town of Tlayacapan, Mexico, where wild mushrooms are commonly sold in the street markets, P. djamor or pink oyster mushroom and its varieties had the most cultural significance and recognizability of any local mushroom (Álvarez-Farias et al., 2016). 

Oyster mushroom remains a valued food of the Indigenous people of California, like the Karuk tribe; they take great care in gathering the fungi from dead and decaying hardwoods like alder (Anderson & Lake, 2013). An elder among the Central Sierra Me-Wuk people, Phyllis Montgomery, compared the closely related P. cornucopiae to a chewy steak (Anderson & Lake, 2013). Sean Sherman, an award-winning Oglala Lakota chef, Indigenous food activist, and co-author of The Sioux Chef’s Indigenous Kitchen uses oyster mushrooms in his recipe for venison stew (Sherman & Dooley, 2017). 

Oyster mushroom has built up a reputation as a savior when it comes to a dearth of food availability. During World War I, oyster mushroom was cultivated in Germany—marked by severe scarcity at this time—as a food for human subsistence (Eger et al., 1976), and today renewed attention has now been given to Pleurotus for alleviating global food shortages in part because it is so easy to grow and because its high content of organic compounds and mineral salts makes it of significant dietary importance for human consumption (Piska et al., 2017).

There is little concrete information available on oyster mushroom’s traditional applications outside of Chinese medicine; while its ethnobotanical background is quite scant, multiple species of the Pleurotus genus have received a great deal of ever-increasing scientific scrutiny both for their potential uses for human health—much of which is fueled by their polysaccharide content—and for their unique environmental applications. 

In Chinese medicine, oyster mushroom is regarded as sweet and warming and associated with the organs of Spleen, Liver, and Stomach (Yang & Guo, 2015). It may be indicated for those with hepatitis and the Chinese materia medica book known as Ming Yi Bie Lu also indicates the oyster mushroom for enhanced intestinal and kidney functioning (Yang & Guo, 2015). Its slightly warming nature contributes to the dispersal of coldness and dampness as well as to the soothing of sinews (Yang & Guo, 2015).  

Oyster mushroom may be indicated in Chinese medicine as a decoction or stew for general enhancement of health, particularly for those with nutrient deficiency that may lead to frailty or rickets, and for those who are coping with menopause (Yang & Guo, 2015). It has also been suggested—and explored in animal studies—that oyster mushroom may be an ideal iron supplement for blood building, as it contains some 19 milligrams/100 grams of iron when dried (Hobbs, 1986).

When it comes to applications for human health, the oyster mushroom has also been commercialized expressly for its polysaccharides, which are used both for immune support and general health maintenance (Powell, 2014). Oyster mushroom’s primary applications today concern its cholesterol-regulating, immune-modulating and antitumor actions, all of which can be connected back to its polysaccharide content; there is also increasing attention being focused on its neuroprotective actions.

Oyster mushroom is perhaps best known and most widely studied for its use in lowering blood cholesterol levels. Hypoglycemic and hypolipidemic, oyster mushroom lowers triglycerides and LDL cholesterol and regulates lipid metabolism (Piska et al., 2017). Species other than just P. ostreatus are used both for this purpose, in particular P. eryngii, P. tuberegium, and P. citronopileatus (Powell, 2014). The mushroom’s cholesterol-lowering action impacts the levels of blood lipids, which can be helpful in those with hypercholesterolemia, as well as glucose levels, which can be helpful in those with diabetes  (Mowsumi & Chowdhury, 2013). Due to their fiber content, consumption of oyster mushrooms leads to increased excretion of bile acids; to replace the lost bile acids, the liver increases its uptake of cholesterol from the blood, resulting in decreased circulating cholesterol.

Oyster mushrooms naturally produce constituents very similar to the cholesterol-lowering drug lovastatin, which works to lower cholesterol levels. Lovastatin was formulated as a drug in the late 1980s specifically in order to reduce blood cholesterol (Stamets, 2000) and to address dyslipidemia (Piska et al., 2017). The mushroom’s constituent ergothioneine may also play a role, as the compound is antioxidant and is used for cardiovascular diseases (due to its triglyceride-reducing action), strokes, and neurodegeneration (Piska et al., 2017). 

For several decades now, animal studies have demonstrated oyster mushroom’s impact on blood lipids and cholesterol (Bobec et al., 1991). Some human studies have also begun to indicate that oyster mushrooms reduce cholesterol in diabetic individuals. One study of 89 hospital patients with diabetes concluded that the mushroom significantly lowered blood pressure, blood glucose, triglycerides (TG), and cholesterol without negative effects on liver and kidneys, but suggested a study of a larger sample and longer duration to further explore its potential and any toxicities (Khatun et al., 2007). 

A later placebo-controlled study focused on a pool of 5,000 diabetic but otherwise healthy stay-at-home women of the Southeast Asia region, all between the ages of 30-50. The non-placebo group took 200 grams daily of oyster mushroom for 360 days, which was found to play a significant role in lowering total cholesterol, blood glucose, TG, and LDL with no adverse effects in bone marrow, kidney, or liver (Sayeed et al., 2015). This confirmed findings from other studies that asserted the antilipidemic and antihyperglycemic actions of oyster mushroom (Sayeed et al., 2015).

Oyster mushrooms are also powerful immune modulators and have been explored for their antitumor properties both in traditional practices and in scientific inquiries. Their indication for cancer is found in Chinese medicine, where oyster mushroom is particularly known for controlling tumors, potentially due to its amino acids (Yang & Guo, 2015). Other species in addition to P. ostreatus, such P. eryngii, P. tuberegium, and P. citronopileatus, are also used for immunomodulation (Powell, 2014). 

In part, the mushroom’s anticarcinogenic action also comes from the presence of its potent beta-glucans (Isokauppila, 2017), which are a group of glucose polysaccharides that appear naturally in the mushroom’s cell walls. Oyster mushroom’s polysaccharides, which have largely been studied only in animal models or on cell lines, are largely given credit for its antitumor effects (Cao et al., 2015; Wisbeck et al., 2017; Kong et al., 2014) and its beta-glucans have been found most useful in breast and colon cancers (Isokauppila, 2017). Its extracted beta-glucan pleuran is an immunomodulatory polysaccharide that can be used for cancer, infections, and immune system imbalances (Piska et al., 2017).

One extracted polysaccharide of oyster mushroom, named POMP2, has been considered significant for arresting gastric cancer cell growth both in vivo and in vitro (Cao et al., 2015). Another study seeking to evaluate the antiproliferative, cytotoxic, and anti-invasive effects of oyster mushroom on leukemia cell lines utilized gradient dilutions of a P. ostreatus extract and has suggested that it was useful enough to be used in alternative care for leukemia patients experiencing the side effects of chemotherapy (Ebrahimi et al., 2018).

Oyster mushroom, meanwhile, also has a reputation as an antimicrobial agent. A study undertaken in 1997 demonstrated that its antibacterial actions appear to arise from several constituents in the mushroom that work to reduce the levels of bacteria within the body (Beltran-Garcia et al., 1997). Some reviews, however, suggest that because such high concentrations are needed to get antibacterial effects, its antimicrobial action may pale in comparison to the mushroom’s other more marked actions in terms of efficacy (Piska et al., 2017).

Pleurotus ostreatus was tested for its effect upon fungal and bacterial growth using alcohol and water solvent extracts in three different forms (Younis et al., 2015). The broth and mycelial biomass were found to inhibit bacteria at a moderate level while the alcohol solvents were much less effective, but the water extract of the fruiting body was most effective in inhibiting the majority of fungi, including Candida albicans, Staphylococcus aureus, and Escherichia coli, with the latter two being particularly sensitive (Younis et al., 2015). 

The polysaccharides of oyster mushroom, meanwhile, may play an immunomodulating role with respect to respiratory infections (Jesenak et al., 2017). Indeed, oyster mushrooms have been said to be antiviral (Piska et al., 2017). There is an underdeveloped connection to lung health as well; in a study of children with allergic inflammation, it appears that a beta-glucan isolated from the mushroom, pleuran, is a potential anti-allergy agent (Jesenak et al., 2014).

It should be noted again that other species besides P. ostreatus show promise of further research and indications. For example, P. pulmonarius is also anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and antitumor (Jose et al., 2002) as well as hypoglycemic (Badole et al., 2006), although studies have thus far only been based on animal models.

Oyster mushroom also has potential application to support the nervous system. It contains many B vitamins, and notably B6, and therefore can potentially help with stress and sleep (Isokauppila, 2017); it is sometimes found in commercial products for use as a nerve tonic (Rogers, 2011). Furthermore, the phospholipids in the mushroom may prove useful in supporting the myelin sheath and various nerve conditions (Rogers, 2011) and the mushroom may prove of use in degenerative conditions like Alzheimer’s and Parkinson’s diseases, which are the subject of very recent research on the mushroom’s neuroprotective actions (Ćilerdžić et al., 2019). Chinese medicine indicates oyster mushroom for muscular relaxation as well, particularly with respect to tendons and joints, for which a “tendon-easing” powder is made (Hobbs, 1986). 

Oyster mushrooms may also be increasingly utilized in the future for cosmetic reasons; as they contain a high level of the skin-lightening agent kojic acid, an extract may be used for removing freckles (Isokauppila, 2017). The mushroom also contains a high level of what are called sphingo-glycolipids—or vegetal ceramides—which may be of use for protecting and moisturizing the skin (Rogers, 2011). Its ergothioneine may also be used for anti-aging purposes (Piska et al., 2017). In the meantime, oyster mushroom remains under steady scientific appraisal for its array of health benefits while offering valuable nutrition and unusually significant environmental promise.

In addition to being a boon to human health, it can also benefit our environment, as the mushroom is also seen as a potent tool in mycoremediation for environmental contamination. Pleurotus spp. have the ability to break down hydrocarbon molecules—found in various chemical pollutants originating from  petroleum and plastics—and in doing so the mushroom actually breaks down contamination from the soil (Isokauppila, 2017). This makes the oyster mushroom a key ally for natural disasters such as oil spills. Some studies have already examined how the mushroom degrades oil contamination via enzymes (Yateem et al., 1998) and how the toxicity levels of soil contaminated by crude oil could be reduced in less than 3 months simply by using a spent compost of the mushroom (Mohammadi-Sichani et al., 2017).

Oyster mushrooms have also been largely studied for their ligninolytic aspects, or the ability to break down polymers such as those found in wood (Satankar et al., 2018). Oyster mushroom is also one of some ten gilled mushroom species whose mycelia can both kill and then digest nematodes (small roundworms), which appears to be how the mushroom maintains a nitrogen supply (Thorn & Barron, 1984). 

In terms of practical applications of the mushroom outside of health and wellness, the oyster can be used in dyeing fabrics and papers: a mix of ammonia and iron with the mushroom results in a gray-green color for wool (Spahr, 2009). Two university students, meanwhile, used oyster mushrooms grown under their dorm beds in a search to find out how to use them to recycle farm waste and create an alternative to plastics such as styrofoam—the results were ultimately successful enough to form a company, Ecovative, that now uses mushrooms for just this purpose in a material called Myco Board or Myco Foam (Mills, 2014).

Adult Dose

3-9 grams/day (Hobbs, 1996)

Safety

It has been known for several decades now that spores may provoke allergic responses  (this is a common complaint of harvesters), the symptoms of which may include fever, coughing, congestion, headache, nausea, sneezing, and a general malaise (Stamets, 2005). While the spores may initially be tolerated by the body, there seems to be a pattern of increased sensitivity over time (Stamets, 2005). 

A 1993 study also demonstrated that about 10% of Europeans and Americans are allergic to P. ostreatus extracts—otherwise, most of the time there are few reactions to oyster mushrooms that have been cooked or otherwise heat-treated. Oyster mushrooms also contain a sugar alcohol known as arabitol which is responsible for provoking gastrointestinal upset in some individuals (Mowsumi & Choudhury, 2013) and they also contain a protein known as pleurotolysin, which is cytolytic (causing a cell to burst, releasing its contents into the extracellular environment) (Rumack & Spoerke, 1994). This toxin is inactivated by heat, so the mushrooms should be cooked prior to ingestion (Isokauppila, 2017).
