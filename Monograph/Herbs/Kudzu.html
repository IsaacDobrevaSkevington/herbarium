Kudzu

Pueraria montana, P. montana var. lobata, P. thunbergiana, P. thomsonii

Native To

Pueraria montana is native to Asia to north-central Australia, including China, Japan, Korea, Bangladesh, India, Cambodia, Inner Mongolia, Laos, Myanmar, New Guinea, Philippines, Taiwan, Thailand, Tibet, and Vietnam (Royal Botanic Gardens Kew [R.B.G.K], n.d.a). Pueraria montana var. lobata is native to China to north-central Australia, but doubtfully present in Vietnam or Thailand (Royal Botanic Gardens Kew [R.B.G.K], n.d.b).

Geographic Distribution

Native to India and East Asia, and China and Japan in particular, kudzu grows abundantly in both subtropical and temperate climates. Kudzu can now be found where it has naturalized worldwide in parts of Europe, Africa, South America, and Oceania. Thriving in woodlands, along hillsides, and in open, sunny spaces, kudzu’s amazing ability to adapt to various climates and soils has enabled it to spread far beyond its native range (Invasive.org, n.d). It thrives in poor soils, roadsides, and forest edges, where its vigorous growth easily overcomes native species (Frank, 2021).

Kudzu was first introduced to the United States as an ornamental in the late 19th century and was later utilized for agricultural purposes. Kudzu is now prolific across the southeastern United States, especially in North and South Carolina, Georgia, Alabama, Mississippi, and Tennessee, where the warmth and humidity allow it to flourish. Kudzu stands have also been reported in parts of the Midwest and Northeast U.S., though its growth is more moderate in cooler climates (Lynn, 2018).

Globally, kudzu can now be found naturalized in parts of Europe, Africa, South America, and Oceania. It thrives in disturbed soils, roadsides, and forest edges, where its vigorous growth can outcompete native vegetation (Frank, 2021).

Botanical Description

A vigorous perennial vine in the Fabaceae family (legumes), remarkable kudzu can grow up to 60 feet in a single season (Invasive.org, n.d). The binomial Pueraria montana encompasses several varieties which thrive in different ecosystems, including Pueraria montana var. lobata, a species identifiable because of its hardy woody stems and penchant for mountainous habitats, though it also grows prolifically in lowlands. This particular variety features large, trifoliate leaves with ovate to lobed leaflets, hairy stems, and speedy climbing abilities and is particularly dominant in the southeastern United States.

Conversely, P. montana var. thomsonii features smaller, more delicate leaves with smooth edges, thinner stems, and a less aggressive growth pattern. Both varieties produce beautiful purple to lavender flowers with yellow markings and woody, gnarled roots, though P. lobata‘s rapid growth often overshadows its counterparts. Pueraria thunbergiana, on the other hand, showcases slightly smaller, less lobed leaves and a less invasive pattern of growth.

Key Constituents

Root: isoflavones: puerarin, 3′-hydroxy puerarin, daidzin, daidzein, genistein; starch (carbohydrate); triterpenoids; tannins (Xuan et al., 2023)

Leaf: flavonoids: rutin, quercetin (Xuan et al., 2023)

Stem: saponins; triterpenoids; tannins (Xuan et al., 2023)

Sustainability

Kudzu is considered one of the most invasive plant species in many regions, particularly in the southeastern United States. Its aggressive growth rate—up to 1 foot per day in optimal conditions—allows it to overwhelm native vegetation, smother trees, and disrupt ecosystems (Invasive Species Specialist Group, 2023). For this reason, kudzu is often viewed as a pest plant rather than a resource in these areas. Harvesting kudzu for food, herbal, craft, or other purposes is generally encouraged as part of management efforts to control its spread, and there are no concerns about overharvesting in the United States (Invasive.org, n.d). Its resilience and ability to grow aggressively make it notable both as a beneficial herb and as an invasive species in the United States (Invasive Species Specialist Group, 2023). Because of its tendency to spread unchecked, no part of kudzu should be composted (Hernandez & Wood Buzzard, 2025).

Harvesting Guidelines

In regions where kudzu is abundant and regarded as an invasive species, its roots can be harvested at any stage without sustainability concerns because of its aggressive growth habits. However, in its native habitats, such as East Asia, or in diverse ecosystems where it is not invasive, it should be harvested in moderation or left undisturbed to protect the integrity of the native plants and wildlife.

Uses

Kudzu is a climbing perennial vine in the legume family which boasts many beneficial compounds for human health and a rich history that spans its East Asian roots. Pueraria montana is a classification of kudzu that includes multiple varieties, including Pueraria montana var. lobata. P. montana var. lobata is the most widely recognized variety of Pueraria montana. Its introduction to North America and the evolution of its beneficial and agricultural uses over centuries across the globe have made it a plant of historical, ecological, and health significance. Kudzu’s storied history of use in Chinese medicine, food, culture, and as a natural resource is a testament to its lasting cultural and historical significance linking it to its origins in East Asia. The root (tuber), leaves, flowers, and vines are used for food, health, and textiles worldwide.

In 1876, kudzu had its debut in the United States at the Philadelphia Centennial Exposition, where it was promoted as an ornamental and agricultural plant (Shurtleff & Aoyagi, 2021). In the Dust Bowl era of the 1930s and 1940s, the U.S. Soil Conservation Service advocated for kudzu to combat soil erosion; its rapid growth and substantial roots make it capable of stabilizing eroded areas (Invasive Species Specialist Group, 2023). It is a known nitrogen fixer, converting atmospheric nitrogen into a form that is usable by plants and thereby enriching the soil around it. Interestingly, kudzu forms symbiotic relationships with soil bacteria, particularly Rhizobium species, which enable kudzu to enhance soil fertility (Liu et al., 2022).

Because of its tendency toward unchecked growth kudzu can be considered an ecological nuisance, and its use in land restoration projects must be carefully managed to avoid a kudzu invasion. It earned a reputation as “the plant that ate the south” in the southeastern United States, once it began spreading over millions of acres of land. Harvesting kudzu for culinary, herbal, and industrial purposes can help control its spread and allocate more resources to native species. Of course, in kudzu’s native habitats, sustainable harvesting practices include avoiding digging too many mature roots in order to maintain ecological balance (Invasive Species Specialist Group, 2023).

In East Asia, particularly in China, Japan, and Korea, kudzu was first used in Chinese medicine as documented in classical Chinese texts such as the Shennong Bencao Jing (Divine Farmer’s Materia Medica). Kudzu root, known as gé gēn, has been utilized to support various conditions. In this ancient Chinese pharmacological text, kudzu is classified as a “superior herb” (Yang, 1998), one considered safe for long-term use and beneficial for promoting overall health and longevity due to its wide range of uses and low toxicity (Bensky et al., 2004).

Kudzu root is categorized as cooling and sweet in Chinese medicine, harmonizing the meridians in the Spleen and Stomach. It is often suggested to ‘release the exterior’ in colds, flu, and the associated symptoms like fever, headache, neck stiffness, and gastrointestinal distress. In Chinese medicine, ‘releasing the exterior’ refers to the expelling of pathogens from the surface of the body, thereby stopping them from penetrating deeper into the tissues. This release resolves “hot” symptoms like fever, itching, and incomplete rash expression, correcting imbalance by in fact promoting the eruption of rashes and facilitating the outward movement of pathogenic heat trapped within the body. One of the ways these types of skin conditions may present as ‘releasing the exterior’ is as measles, red, raised eruptions that may spread across the body, often accompanied by heat, irritation, and itching (Bensky et al., 2004).

Kudzu root is also valued for its ability to generate fluids in the body, alleviating thirst and dryness caused by fever; this quality makes it a key herb for supporting conditions where fluids are depleted due to illness or environmental factors (Chen & Chen, 2004). Kudzu root aids high fever, thirst, dry mouth, and dehydration; in such cases, these conditions are observed as internal heat consuming the body’s fluids, and kudzu works to generate moisture and cool the body (Bensky et al., 2004). Kudzu generates fluids by cooling internal heat, nourishing yin, and stimulating the production of saliva and digestive fluids. Kudzu’s active constituents, namely puerarin, help promote hydration and reduce inflammation, making it especially effective for conditions in which a fever, dehydration, and heat is present (Bensky et al., 2004).

In Chinese tradition, yin deficiency is such a state; in a yin-deficient body, the cooling and nourishing elements are depleted, leading to dryness, heat, and inflammation. Kudzu is useful in addressing yin deficiencies, particularly in conditions like diabetes mellitus, where thirst and dryness are hallmarks (Chen & Chen, 2004). Western research also highlights kudzu’s ability to modulate blood sugar levels and improve insulin sensitivity (Chen et al., 2018).

From a scientific perspective, kudzu root’s pharmacological properties are mainly attributed to its high concentration of isoflavones like puerarin, daidzein, and genistein, which demonstrate antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and anticancer effects; these compounds help promote heart health and brain function, neutralize free radicals, reduce chronic inflammation, decrease symptoms of menopause, and inhibit tumor growth, with implications for cardiovascular and metabolic health (Bhathena & Velasquez, 2002; Xuan et al., 2023). 

Kudzu root is also renowned for its antispasmodic, analgesic, and vasodilatory properties, which make it particularly effective for aiding stiff neck and upper back pain associated with hypertension, tension headaches, and musculoskeletal pain (Chen & Chen, 2004). Both the root and stem of kudzu also contain saponins that boost immunity and aid metabolism (Xuan et al., 2023). 

A survey of 235 cluster headache patients investigated the use of alternative approaches, including kudzu. According to Dr. R.A. Sewell (2009), out of 16 individuals who used kudzu, 11 reported a reduction in headache intensity, nine experienced decreased frequency, and five noted shorter headache duration, all with minimal side effects. While these findings use a small sample group to suggest that components in over-the-counter kudzu products may help manage cluster headaches, further research is necessary to confirm its efficacy (Sewell, 2009).

Kudzu’s root’s isoflavones, including puerarin and daidzein, have been shown to stimulate circulation, lower inflammation, and ease symptoms of cardiovascular diseases (Wong et al., 2011). Gao et al. (2016) explains how the isoflavones in kudzu root also possess potent free radical scavenging activity, which neutralizes reactive oxygen species (ROS) and mitigates oxidative stress, a major contributor to the development of atherosclerosis and other cardiovascular disease. Kudzu root may lower the risk of heart disease, hypertension, and stroke by protecting cellular structures and reducing oxidative damage to lipids, proteins, and DNA (Gao et al., 2016). It also lessens hypertension and angina in traditional and modern practice (Bensky et al., 2004).

One of the active compounds in kudzu root, puerarin, has been shown to improve endothelial function—the health of the blood vessel lining—which is essential for promoting proper blood flow and supporting cardiovascular health (Gao et al., 2016). The leaves of kudzu also have flavonoids like rutin and quercetin, which protect blood vessels (Xuan et al., 2023). According to Gao et al. (2016), kudzu root may reduce blood pressure by inhibiting the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines, which contribute to inflammation in the vascular system; additionally, kudzu’s antioxidant properties may reduce plaque buildup in the arteries and lower LDL cholesterol oxidation. 

Research from Zhang et al. (2005) indicates that puerarin may also have mild phytoestrogenic properties, referencing its tonic effects on the endometrial lining and uterus and ability to promote uterine health, which supports this use. Kudzu’s phytoestrogenic compounds, including daidzein, mimic estrogen in the body, potentially reducing bone loss and easing menopausal discomfort (Zhang et al., 2005). In Chinese medicine, kudzu promotes menstrual health and regulates blood flow, both nourishing yin and balancing hormones (Chen & Chen, 2004).

Puerarin and daidzein’s anti-inflammatory and antispasmodic properties may also help reduce intestinal inflammation, stabilize bowel movements, and restore gut homeostasis (Chen & Chen, 2004). Easley and Horne (2016) note kudzu’s efficacy for leaky gut syndrome. Kudzu root is high in nourishing starch which contributes to its ability to soothe the digestive system by providing a protective coating to irritated mucosa, reducing irritation, and encouraging health (Chen & Chen, 2004). Both kudzu root and stem contain anti-inflammatory triterpenoids and while tannins which promote gut health and balanced blood sugar (Xuan et al., 2023).

In Chinese medicine, kudzu is used in cases of diarrhea and dysentery, particularly when these conditions are associated with heat or damp heat in the intestines and in instances of fever or food poisoning (Bensky et al., 2004). In fact, the roots are known for their ability to neutralize poisons and viruses (Easley & Horne, 2016). Bensky et al. (2004) describes its cooling and fluid-regulating properties which make it effective in clearing pathogenic heat while promoting the restoration of proper fluid balance in the digestive system. Kudzu is often used with other herbs to address abdominal pain, cramping, and loose or bloody stools. Gé Gēn Huáng Lián Huáng Qín Tāng is a patent herbal formula that clears heat and ‘releases the exterior’ while addressing damp heat in the intestines, indicated for conditions with fever, sweating, and diarrhea (Bensky et al., 2004).

Kudzu is considered a nutritious food, and historically has been valuable during times of scarcity or famine in Asia (Shurtleff and Aoyagi, 1977). Its root starch is substantially nutritive and may be processed into porridge, noodles, and jellies (Shurtleff & Aoyagi, 1977). Kudzu root starch is still consumed in parts of Asia, where it is used to make a jelly-like substance and incorporated into traditional dishes. The leaves and heavily scented flowers of kudzu are also nutritious sources of protein, vitamins, and minerals and typically boiled or prepared in soups (Gulizia & Downs, 2020). 

Kudzu root starch is ground into a flour-like powder and used in cooking and baking, especially for thickening gravies, soups, and stews. It is often paired with ginger, garlic, meat, and vegetables to create a nutritive and nourishing stirfry (Davidson, 2014). Kudzu starch can also be used to make noodles and are often used in Asian dishes, such as broths (Davidson, 2014). The starch also makes a delicious porridge, commonly combined with coconut milk and a sweetener (Ohnuki-Tierney, 1993). It is often used as a substitute for cornstarch or arrowroot powder in traditional Japanese and Chinese desserts (Davidson, 2014) like the jelly-like sweet called kudzu-mochi. Kudzu root starch is mixed with water and sugar and heated until it forms a thick, smooth gel, then chilled until hardened, cut into small pieces, and often served with syrup or red bean paste (Davidson, 2014).

Tender, young kudzu shoots, stems, and leaves can make a mild, sweet tea by boiling in water. This tea is known to help with cold symptoms, easing congestion and inflammation (Ohnuki-Tierney, 1993). Early shoots are a choice edible and can be eaten either cooked or raw (Hernandez & Wood Buzzard, 2025). Kudzu leaves are used for both their culinary appeal and health benefits, and may be added to soups or stir-fries. The leaves have an earthy, mild taste that complements other vegetables. The fragrant flowers of the kudzu plant can be used to make jams, jellies, and syrups. Kudzu flower simple syrup is made by infusing the flowers in sugar and water, and can be used as a sweetener for beverages, desserts, or pancakes (Davidson, 2014).

Kudzu vines have also been used in textile production for their thick fiber. The woody vines are woven into durable baskets, ropes, and mats. This use of kudzu is still significant in some areas where the plant is harvested for local textile and craft production, which has helped kudzu maintain its value beyond its health and ecological benefits, particularly in rural communities (Shurtleff & Aoyagi, 1977).

In the United States, kudzu has been integrated into herbalism as a natural aid for alcohol dependence (Chen & Chen, 2004). It is commonly used in herbal formulas indicated for reducing alcohol cravings or to lessen the likelihood of relapse. According to Chen and Chen (2004), kudzu-based supplements are often marketed as a natural way to manage alcohol addiction and can be incorporated into detoxification programs. Kudzu root has been used in Chinese medicine for alleviating the dryness and heat caused by excessive alcohol intake, which depletes yin and damages internal fluids, which makes it a traditional preparation for supporting hangovers, helping rehydrate tissues and ease discomfort (Chen & Chen, 2004). From a Chinese medicine perspective, kudzu’s ability to detoxify alcohol and reduce cravings may be related to its fluid regeneration and alleviating heat, which enhances the liver’s efficiency (Chen & Chen, 2004). 

Modern clinical trials suggest that kudzu’s isoflavones may reduce alcohol consumption by modulating neurotransmitter pathways in the brain and helping to manage alcohol dependence (Lukas et al., 2005). Keung and Vallee (1993) indicate that kudzu’s isoflavonoids, particularly puerarin, daidzin, and daidzein, interact with enzymes involved in alcohol metabolism, such as aldehyde dehydrogenase (ALDH). This process slows the breakdown of acetaldehyde, a toxic byproduct of alcohol, reducing the urge to consume alcohol by creating a feeling of aversion (Keung & Vallee, 1993). Several studies suggest that kudzu root may reduce alcohol cravings by interacting with the dopamine and serotonin systems, which influence the reward pathways in the brain (Rooke et al., 2000).

While kudzu continues to be studied for its benefits for individuals with alcohol dependence, it is important to always consult a healthcare professional before incorporating kudzu or other herbs into a recovery plan, especially when dealing with dependence or withdrawal symptoms. Alcohol dependence is a complex condition that often requires a multifaceted clinical approach. Kudzu may serve as a supportive tool but should not replace a conventional medical approach to alcohol addiction.

Adult Dose

Tincture: Dried root 1-4 mL (0.2-0.8 tsp) (1:5, 50%) 3x/day (Easley & Horne, 2016)

Powder or Capsules: 1,000-3,000 mg up to 3x/day (Easley & Horne, 2016) or 1.2 g of kudzu root extract per day over 1 week, or a single dose of 2 grams before drinking alcohol, without noted side effects (Penetar et al., 2012; Penetar et al., 2015).

Note that standardized extracts are available for alcohol cravings or cardiovascular health.

Decoction: The typical dosage of the dried root ranges from 6 to 12 g daily (Jennes & Flaws, 2004).

Safety

According to Chen and Chen (2004), kudzu is generally considered safe, but excessive intake may cause mild gastrointestinal upset. Avoid for individuals with cold or yang deficiency conditions, as kudzu is cooling in nature. Gé gēn should be used cautiously in individuals with cold conditions, as it possesses cooling properties that may exacerbate such conditions. Chen and Chen (2004) suggest kudzu be used cautiously during pregnancy or breastfeeding due to lack of safety data, although The Nursing Mother’s Herbal rates kudzu an ‘A’ for safety, noting its use as a galactagogue (Humphrey, 2003). Kudzu may interact with medications for blood pressure, diabetes, or hormone-sensitive conditions due to its isoflavone content; kudzu may slow blood clotting, potentially increasing the risk of bruising and bleeding when taken with these medications. Kudzu has the potential to act like estrogen in the body, which could interfere with estrogen medications (Chen & Chen, 2004).

Kudzu seeds and seedpods are inedible and best disposed of (Hernandez & Wood Buzzard, 2025).

While kudzu continues to be studied for its benefits for individuals with alcohol dependence, it is important to always consult a healthcare professional before incorporating kudzu or other herbs into a recovery plan, especially when dealing with dependence or withdrawal symptoms. Alcohol dependence is a complex condition that often requires a multifaceted clinical approach. Kudzu may serve as a supportive tool but should not replace a conventional medical approach to alcohol addiction.
