Self heal

Prunella vulgaris

Native To

Although self heal’s exact native range is obscure (Wiersema & León, 2016), it is believed to be native to most regions of the temperate and subtropical Northern Hemisphere to Central America (Royal Botanic Gardens of Kew [RBGK], n.d.).

Geographic Distribution

Self heal is widely distributed throughout its native ranges, including: across North America, throughout Europe (from Scandinavia in the north to the Mediterranean countries in the south), in much of Asia (including Siberia, China, Japan and the Indian subcontinent), the Middle East (including Turkey, Iran, Iraq, and Syria), and Northwest Africa, including Morocco and Algeria; self heal has also naturalized in South America and New Zealand (RBGK, n.d.).

Botanical Description

Prunella vulgaris is a scentless perennial found in moist soils of woodlands, lawns, and waste areas, with a creeping, prostrate growth habit, simple leaves in opposite arrangement, and the square stem characteristic of the mint family (CABI, 2022; Foster & Duke, 2000). Self heal is a resilient herb that can grow in partial shade or full sun (NC State Extension, n.d.), and spreads rapidly through its creeping rhizomes. The bee-pollinated purple flowers are hermaphroditic (have both male and female organs) with a lipped corolla, and are arranged in spikes (Grieve, 1931/1971; Smith, 2019). Fruit develops at the base of the flower in the form of four small, spherical nutlets; these dry fruits are light brown to black in color and each contain a single seed that can easily propagate (Blamey & Fitter, 2013). After flowering, the calyces are persistent, leaving a spike that somewhat resembles an ear of corn (Grieve, 1931/1971).

 

Key Constituents

Triterpenes (e.g., ursolic acid, oleanolic acid, arjunolic acid, betulinic acid), flavonoids (e.g., quercetin, rutinoside, luteolin), phenolic acids (e.g., rosmarinic acid), polysaccharides (prunellin), saponins, phytosterols, volatile oils (Bai et al., 2016; Skenderi, 2003).

Sustainability

No known sustainability issues, although the plant may be considered noxious or weedy in some areas of the American Northeast and Great Plains (Young-Mathews, 2012).

Harvesting Guidelines

Self heal’s floral spikes have been shown to be highest in key constituents (rosmarinic, ursolic, and oleanolic acids) during the pre-flowering stage when the floral spikes are forming; one Chinese study determined that the plant is best harvested in early May, though this may vary depending on the region in which it is growing (Chen et al., 2012).

Uses

Self heal has a long history of use as both a food and an herb in Europe, Asia, and North America. Its Chinese name, xia ku cao, means ‘‘grass that withers in summer” (Bai et al., 2016, p. 4). The plant has traditionally been used in China both as a tea and a vegetable; in fact, it has been used for over a millennium and was included in one of the earliest Chinese materia medica texts, the Shennong Bencao Jing, and has been included in the Chinese Pharmacopeia since 1963 (Bai et al., 2016). As a food, self heal is prepared in a variety of ways throughout China; in the Hunan province, the spring buds are eaten in steamed rice cakes, while in the province of Guangdong, it is used as an ingredient in soup or tea (Bai et al., 2016). The seedlings are commonly washed and soaked to remove their bitterness, then pickled with oil and salt (Lu, 2015). Foragers also use self heal as a food; the leaves and flowers can be added to salads, and the flowering tops can be cooked into soups or greens or made into an infused vinegar.

According to 17th-century herbalist Nicolas Culpeper (1653/2007), self heal is an herb associated with Venus, which represents beauty, love, harmony, and health. Culpeper states, “Self-heal whereby when you are hurt, you may heal yourself” (1653/2007, p.170), emphasizing the plant’s ability to aid in restoring one’s own health; he further highlights that self heal makes an excellent vulnerary for both internal and external wounds. While Culpeper (1653/2007) regards self heal as moderately hot, dry, and binding, most herbalists feel the herb has cooling energetics. 

Self heal possesses both soothing and astringent properties which make a strong tea or diluted tincture of the herb an ideal choice for wound washes (Cech, 2016). A juice of the fresh plant can be applied directly to insect bites (Moore, 1993). Anishinaabe people have traditionally used a self heal poultice to draw out foreign objects from the skin and clear infection; an Anishinaabemowin name for the plant is ingijibinaa, which translates as “the great drawer-outer” (Geniusz, 2015, p. 253). Herbalist Matthew Wood (2008) highlights the mucilaginous nature of self heal and suggests applying a poultice of the plant’s leaves directly to wounds to effectively draw out heat and protect against infection.

Medieval herbalist John Gerard (1597, p. 633) shared the virtues of self heal as such: “The decoction of Prunel made with wine or water, doth join together and make whole and sound all wounds, both inward and outward…” Herbalist Deb Soule (2005) discusses the power of self heal in addressing emotional wounds; she believes adding a small amount of the herb to formulas enhances their usefulness and herself uses the herb to help support people through emotions of anger, hopelessness, sadness, grief, and despair.

In Turkish folk tradition self heal is also used to address complaints of the physical heart such as heart disease and cardiac disorders (Yesilada et al., 1993). One in vitro study observed an ethanol extract of Prunella vulgaris to have protective effects on human aortic smooth muscle cells against vascular inflammation through several pathways: the extract reduce the adhesion of monocyte/macrophage-like THP-1 cells to the heart tissues, which is significant as excessive adhesion can promote inflammation; the extract decreased the production and expression of certain molecules which increase inflammation and promote plaque formation; and it reduced the activity of a signaling pathway called p38 MAPK/ERK, which is involved in the inflammation process (Park et al., 2013). These findings support the traditional use of the herb for atherosclerosis. Another in vitro study on human aortic smooth muscle cells showed an aqueous extract of Prunella vulgaris to have inhibitory effects on high-glucose stimulated vascular smooth muscle cell proliferation, a significant finding as abnormal proliferation of such cells is part of the disease process of vascular disorders, including diabetic atherosclerosis (Hwang et al., 2013). Regarding diabetes, there are some precautions and considerations to keep in mind while using self heal; the above study results suggest that it may have potential hypoglycemic effects and there is always potential for herbs to interact with drugs and blood sugar levels, so it is necessary to monitor glucose levels closely and consult a trusted healthcare provider. 

Before being referred to by the Latin genus, Prunella, self heal was originally referred to by the German name, Brunella, which pertained to self heal’s efficacy in addressing the uncomfortable inflammation of the mouth and throat known as die Bruen (Soule, 2005). As both an astringent and antiseptic, self heal has been used in traditional Western herbalism as a gargle for inflammation and ulcers of the mouth and throat (Grieve, 1931/1971); for these purposes, herbalist Richo Cech (2016) suggests using a strong brewed tea or one dropperful of the tincture in ½ cup of cold water. For cases of canker sores, Cech (2016) suggests direct application of the strong tea or undiluted tincture. According to Cherokee herbalist J.T. Garrett (2003), a traditional Cherokee cultural use of self heal is in combination with other herbs for sore throats; this is also a use in Iranian herbalism (Morteza-Semnani et al., 2006). Herbalist William Cole, who was a strong proponent of the Doctrine of Signatures, mentions the example of self heal, as the flowers resemble the mouth and throat and could be seen as a signature for the plant’s application in those areas (as cited in Grieve, 1931/1971). Herbalist and California Indian elder Josephine Peters notes that among the Karuk people, the raw herb is chewed in cases of bleeding gums (Peters & Ortiz, 2010). Herbalist Michael Moore (1993) suggests the fresh juice of the herb for gum sores. A clinical trial showing a reduction in symptoms of gingivitis with the use of a toothpaste containing extracts of Prunella vulgaris and Macleaya cordata corroborates self heal’s use in maintaining oral health (Adámková et al., 2004). 

Self heal has been shown to be antiviral as well as antibacterial, exhibiting in vitro activity against HIV (Oh et al., 2011), herpes simplex virus (Nolkemper et la., 2006), and influenza (Tian et al., 2011), as well as inhibiting the growth of pathogenic bacteria such as Escherichia coli, Staphylococcus aureus, and Klebsiella pneumoniae (Rasool et al., 2010). The herb’s antiviral properties combined with its traditional use for the mouth and throat indicate its use in cases of cold sores (Maier, 2021). Richo Cech (2016) suggests using self heal directly on herpes lesions (both oral and genital) while simultaneously taking a tincture or tea of the herb internally; self heal helps to manage the herpes outbreak and allays pain. 

With astringent, anti-inflammatory, and demulcent properties, self heal is also used for inflammation and disturbances of the gastrointestinal tract such as irritable bowel syndrome, colitis, and diarrhea (Maier, 2021). Self heal also has a history of traditional use in Western herbalism for hemorrhoids (Grieve, 1931/1971), and it is used among the Cherokee people as a carminative to ease gas, colic, diarrhea, and dyspepsia (Garrett, 2003). Culpeper (1653/1880) indicates self heal for ulcers, and Karuk Indian herbalist Josephine Peters mentions a preparation for bleeding ulcers made by adding gelatin to self heal tea (Peters & Ortiz, 2010).

As a cooling herb, self heal has also been used to soothe fevers in various traditions. It was commonly used as a febrifuge in Europe during the 17th century, and has also been used in Chinese folk herbalism for this purpose (Psotova et al., 2003). Some Native American groups including the Delaware and Mohegan have also traditionally used self heal for fevers, both as a tea and as a cooling wash for the body (Tantaquidgeon, 2001). In Turkish folk tradition, self heal is used as an anti-inflammatory aid to assist in fighting off colds (Yesilada et al., 1993).

In Hong Kong, self heal is used to soothe children with a “hot qi” presentation that may be associated with infections or allergies (Kong et al., 2006). Self heal is also traditionally used in South Korea for scrofula, goiter, edema, and kidney disorders (World Health Organization, 1998). Herbalist Matthew Wood (2008) specifically indicates self heal for weakened kidneys; he discusses how the plant helps improve diuresis through fortifying the kidneys, rather than through active stimulation. Herbalist William LeSassier specifically indicated self heal for people who presented with both weakened kidneys and dental decay; Wood reasons that this combination of signs could indicate an underlying issue with protein processing, leading to a build up of waste products that stress the liver and kidneys and also breaks down calcium, hence the accompanied tooth decay (Wood, 2008). 

In Chinese medicine, self heal is associated with the liver and gallbladder meridians (Pan et al., 2022). Self heal is used in Chinese medicine for imbalances associated with the liver meridian, including conditions of the breast such as hyperplasia of the mammary glands, acute mastitis, and breast cancer (Pan et al., 2022), though it’s important to note that while there are numerous animal studies, there are few human studies regarding the use of self heal for cancerous masses. One in vitro study showed that an aqueous extraction of self heal was able to suppress cell invasion and cell migration in human liver carcinoma cells; the mechanism of action was through inhibiting activities of metalloproteases, specifically MMP-2 and MMP-9, which are enzymes involved in the degradation of the extracellular matrix, and play a crucial role in cell migration and invasion; cell viability was not affected, indicating the inhibitory effects were not due to cell death (Kim et al., 2012). 

Self heal is considered an lymphatic tonic and can be helpful for swollen breasts, fibrocystic breast tissue, mastitis, and sore nipples; herbalist Deb Soule (2005) suggests combining self heal with other lymphatic herbs such as violet (Viola odorata), calendula (Calendula officinalis), or red clover (Trifolium pratense) for internal use and also recommends infusing these herbs along with chickweed (Stellaria media) into an oil to be used for external massage. One study examined the efficacy and safety of combining Prunella vulgaris with taxane, a conventional breast cancer drug (Zhao et al., 2018). The study involved 424 participants with breast cancer, who were divided into two groups; the experimental group received oral Prunella vulgaris extract and taxane, and the control group received oral placebo and taxane. The results showed that combined Prunella vulgaris and taxane treatment improved the pathological complete response (pCR) rate and overall survival compared to the control group,and the experimental group also reported reduced side-effects of chemotherapy, specifically neutrophil-reduced fever and anemia, suggesting Prunella vulgaris to be safe and useful in conjunction with conventional treatment (Zhao et al., 2018). 

Self heal also has a traditional use in Chinese medicine and Iranian herbalism for inflammation of the eyes; it is used for cases in which the eyes present as red and swollen, and is specifically indicated for eye pain that occurs at nighttime (Hempen & Fischer, 2009)) or presents with headaches (Soule, 2005). In Chinese medicine, eye inflammation is indicative of rising liver heat, which self heal can help dissipate with its cooling properties; a tea can be used as a wash for cases of conjunctivitis (pink eye) and styes (Soule, 2005). 

Self heal, known for its anti-inflammatory properties, has traditionally been used in Turkish folk tradition to address rheumatism (Yesilada et al., 1993). Herbalist Michael Alfs (2003) discusses how self heal is indicated for autoimmune conditions which present with joint pain, low-grade fever, and fatigue. 

One of the herb’s predominant constituents, rosmarinic acid, has been studied for its antioxidant and antimicrobial properties (Psotova et al., 2003). Rosmarinic acid is thought to be responsible for many of self heal’s biological activities, including photoprotective properties. In vitro research has demonstrated that self heal extract and rosmarinic acid significantly suppress the production of reactive oxygen species induced by UVA rays, suggesting that its use in skin care or dermatological preparations could confer protection against oxidative stress induced by UVA radiation and may be beneficial as a photoprotective (Psotova et al., 2006).

Adult Dose

Tea: 1 cup (1-2 teaspoons dried herb in 8 fl oz water) 2x/day (Winston, 2003). 

Tincture: 1.5-5 mL fresh herb (1:2, 65%) 3-5x/day (Cech, 2016); 5-10 mL dried herb (1:4, 40%) 1-3x/day (Coffman, 2021).

Safety

The herb sanicle (Sanicula europaea) is sometimes also called self heal (Grieve, 1931/1971); be certain to clarify plant species identity before use. Allergic reactions to self heal including rashes, swelling, and gastrointestinal symptoms have been reported (Gardner & McGuffin, 2013). Self heal may lower blood sugar, so those with diabetes are advised to use with caution and monitor blood sugar levels closely (Coffman, 2021).
