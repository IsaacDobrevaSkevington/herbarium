Native To

Cyprus, Lebanon-Syria, Palestine, Turkey (Plants of the World Online, n.d.)

Geographic Distribution

Eastern Mediterranean, Morocco, Oman, Tunisia, Southern Europe, Central Asia, India, Bangladesh, Laos, China, northern Argentina, southern Brazil, Venezuela, Guatemala, Trinidad-Tobago (Flora of China, 2008; Plants of the World Online, n.d.)

Botanical Description

Anise is a potently aromatic annual plant that grows 10-50 cm tall and produces a slender taproot (Flora of China, 2008). It is scantily clad with short hairs throughout the plant (Flora of China, 2008). The lower leaves are simple with a broad, ovate form and serrate margins (1-3 x 1.2-2.8 cm) and are shaped somewhat like cilantro leaves, while the upper leaves take on a lacy appearance, becoming bipinnate with very narrow lanceolate to linear leaflets (Flora of China, 2008). The compound umbels (1.5-6 cm wide) form many tiny, white flowers in early summer, while the late summer fruits are oblong to egg-shaped (3-5 mm x 2-2.5 mm) and covered with flattened, bristly hairs (Flora of China, 2008).

Key Constituents

Volatile oils (α-pinene, β-pinene, α-terpineol, anethole, cis-anethole, trans-anethole, dianethole, anisaldehyde, anisic-acid, anisyl-alcohol, azulene, bergapten, camphene, carvene, carvone, β-caryophylline, ar-curcumene, estragole, eugenol, limonene, linalool, myristicin, cis-ocimene, para-anisaldehyde, para-methoxyphenylacetone, phellandrene, sabinene, α-zingiberene), coumarins (5-methoxy-psoralen, bergapten, para-coumaric acid, scopoletin, umbelliferone, xanthotoxin), flavonoid glycosides (apigenin, apigenin-7-0-glucoside, cynaroside isoorientin, isovitexin, luteolin,  luteolin-7-0-glucoside, quercetin, quercetin-3-0-β-D-glucuronide, rutin), fatty acids (propionic acid), salicylates, acetyl-choline, β-amyrin, caffeic-acid, chlorogenic acid, and furfural (Duke, n.d.; Hoffmann, n.d.)

Sustainability

No known sustainability issues.

Harvesting Guidelines

Anise is an annual and may require at least 130 days to grow after germination before the seeds are mature and ready to harvest. Harvest the seeds as the tips of the fruits turn grey but before they turn black (“Anise,” n.d.).

Uses

Anise is a widely used and beloved culinary spice that is used to flavor many foods, including baked goods like spice cakes. The essential oil has a long history of use in flavoring confections, candy, mouth care products, pharmaceuticals, lozenges, tobacco, and alcoholic beverages and cordials, such as absinthe and anisette (Grieve, 1931; Keville & Green, 2009; Romm, 2000).

Anise has been used for digestive, respiratory, and menstrual and menopausal conditions by traditional herbalists in many regions since antiquity, and was cultivated by the ancient Egyptians (Grieve, 1931). It now only occurs in cultivation (Flora of China, 2008). Some common names include sweet cumin and Roman fennel (Duke, n.d.; Ibn Sina, 1025/1998).

Renowned Physiomedicalist William H. Cook (1869) described anise in his influential work, The Physio-Medical Dispensatory, as:

 …one of the most pleasant and sweet of the aromatics, nearly a pure relaxant, and valued for its carminative and nervine influence in flatulent colic, both of children and adults. Few carminatives are so reliable; and it is equally excellent to prevent the griping of stimulating cathartics, and to cover the taste of bitter and nauseating medicines. The stomach receives it gratefully; and if suitably diluted, it not unfrequently allays nausea and vomiting (p. 404).

Cook, a master pharmacist of his time, claimed that warm water only moderately extracts the volatile oil from anise seed, and that for this reason he preferred the distilled oil for internal preparations, which he recommended mixing with sugar or making into an “essence” (an official preparation of his time), prepared by combining the essential oil with a large portion of alcohol (Cook, 1869). Herbalist Maude Grieve described the usage of hot water cordials containing anise essential oil to palliate bronchitis and spasmodic asthma (Grieve, 1931). Modern herbalists recommend consulting with an experienced aromatherapist before considering internal use of any essential oil, which can have dangerous side effects.

Persian physician and polymath Hakim Ibn Sina, also known as Avicenna, wrote of anise at length in the seminal Canon of Medicine (1025 CE), describing it as hot and dry, and balanced energetically by fennel (Ibn Sina, 1025/1998). According to him, anise is deobstruent, meaning it agitates stuck humors and opens the passages in which they are found. He stated that anise removes obstructions of the liver and spleen (Ibn Sina, 1025/1998), and is deobstruent for the kidneys, bladder, and uterus. He noted that it promotes the discharge of white fluid from the uterus, thus cleansing it (Ibn Sina, 1025/1998); in fact, this plant was among the herbs most commonly mentioned in six different important Iranian Traditional medical texts for addressing leucorrhea (Dehdari & Hajimehdipoor, 2016).

Ibn Sina knew anise to be mildly astringent, analgesic, and carminative. The latter quality was said to be accentuated by roasting. He noted it had diuretic effects, and was useful for edema and swellings of extremities. The smoke, which would carry the volatile oils, was known to ease headache and vertigo. Ibn Sina wrote that it also facilitated respiration as well as the formation of milk, and had aphrodisiac effects. He also flagged it as useful for chronic vascular keratitis of the eye, chronic fevers, and for removing poisonous effects of insect bites. He suggested  a dosage of half a dirham (1.75 gm) (Ibn Sina, 1025/1998).

Ethnobotanical surveys of Kurdish herbalists in modern-day Iran (where anise is called vaveh shing in Elam Kurdish or badian romi in Persian (Pirbalouti et al., 2013)) and Iraq (called yansun by the Kurdish, and anîsun in Arabic) (Mati & de Boer, 2011) describe anise as being cultivated in communities that commonly wild harvested most of their herbs (Pirbalouti et al., 2013). It is used extensively for easing menopausal complaints, increasing breast milk, and supporting female fertility (Ahmed, 2016; Mati & de Boer, 2011). Anise is also cited as a stomachic and carminative that is used to promote digestive health and to address indigestion, flatulence, abdominal pain, children’s diarrhea, ascaris worms, and colonitis (Ahmed, 2016; Pirbalouti et al., 2013; Mati & de Boer, 2011). Anise is also employed for respiratory health (flu, cough, asthma) (Ahmed, 2016; Mati & de Boer, 2011), nervous system health and pain (sleep, analgesic, anxiety), other concerns (hypertension, urolithiasis) (Ahmed, 2016; Mati & de Boer, 2011), and culinary use (Ahmed, 2016; Pirbalouti et al., 2013).

An ethnobotanical study among people of Beni-Sueif of Egypt found that anise was a critical herb for respiratory, gastrointestinal (GI) concerns, and spastic conditions, and most often was prepared as an infusion or decoction (AbouZid & Mohamed, 2011).

Anise was among the most frequently mentioned species in an ethnobotanical study of Turkish-speaking people of Cyprus living in Cyprus and London, who called it anason. Anise was recorded as having carminative, calming, tranquilizing, diuretic, and urinary tract antiseptic effects that benefited stomach upset, stomach ache, colic, flu, common cold, and “wind”  (Yöney et al., 2009).

Outside of its natural range, anise also made big impacts. The Garifuna, Afro-Caribbeans of Guatemala, who refer to the plant as anis, have been recorded by ethnobotanists as utilizing the seed decoction for diarrhea, stomach pains, phlegm, coughs, and fever (Girón et al., 1991). It has been cultivated in the Guatemalan highlands (Girón et al., 1991).

In folk herbal traditions of Mexico and the southwestern United States, anise (anis in Spanish) tea was used for liver obstructions and tuberculosis, menstrual pains and regulation, colics and stomach ailments, as well as colds and coughs (Linares & Bye, 1987).

In places where anise has been introduced into herbal traditions, in some cases local plants that are used as substitutes have been called by the same name—for example, in northern Mexico and southwestern United States, the name hierba anís (“anise herb”) can also refer to locally derived or other imported plants including Tagetes lucida, T. filifolia, T. micrantha, Artemisia dracunculus, Pimpinella anisum, and Illicium verum (Linares & Bye, 1987). Illicium verum, also known as star anise, is commonly confused with Pimpinella anisum internationally, though they have different uses and contraindications.

Modern herbalists apply anise for many of the same indications as their predecessors; its sweet and pleasant flavor may be part of its enduring appeal, and it is frequently included in tea blends. Anise has antispasmodic and aromatic qualities, possibly accounting for some of its beneficial effects in the digestive and respiratory systems (Sinadinos, 2008). In ayurvedic terms, anise decreases vata and kapha, and increases pitta (Frawley & Lad, 1988).

Commonly reported gastrointestinal tract indications include nausea, griping, colic (Romm, 2000; Sinadinos, 2008; Stansbury, 2018a), and especially gas and bloating (Stansbury, 2018a). It is considered an “excellent” carminative (Stansbury, 2018a, pg. 86) and a corrigent that mitigates side effects caused by laxative, cholagogue, and stimulant herbs (Stansbury, 2018a).

Recent research affirms these actions. Consumption of 3 grams of anise powder after each meal (3 times/day) for 12 weeks was strongly effective in reducing total scores of functional dyspepsia in a double-blind, randomized, controlled trial (RCT) of 107 people experiencing postprandial distress syndrome. There were significant improvements in epigastric discomfort and fullness, early satiety, belching, loss of appetite, bloating, pain, and burning (Ghoshegir et al., 2015). Anise also improved quality of life scores in both mental and physical health domains among the same study group (Ghoshegir et al., 2014).

In another study, enteric coated anise capsules improved irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) symptoms (abdominal discomfort or pain, bloating, diarrhea, constipation, gastroesophageal reflux, headache, tiredness, and overall satisfaction and quality of life) more effectively than placebo or Colpermin (delayed-release peppermint oil capsules) in an RCT of 120 patients with IBS (Mosaffa-Jahromi et al., 2016).

Anise may also support those with other GI complaints. It is known to demonstrate antimicrobial action against Helicobacter pylori, a bacteria that can cause peptic ulcers and gastritis (Stansbury, 2018a). Herbalists suggest drinking a beverage-strength anise seed tea (1 teaspoon per 8 ounce cup) upon waking to ease morning sickness and during or after meals to soothe heartburn during pregnancy (Weed, 1986). Avoid use of the essential oil or alcohol extracts during pregnancy (Gardner & McGuffin, 2013).

Naturopathic physician Dr. Jillian Stansbury notes anise tea may be given to a nursing mother to drink as she settles down to nurse as it can be passed in the breast milk for infants with colic. The tea has been given directly to the infant as well (Stansbury, 2018a; Weed, 1986). Physician, midwife, and herbalist Aviva Romm suggests anise tea for children suffering stomach aches from gas, anxiety, or indigestion (Romm, 2000). She suggests an appetite-stimulating blend for children that combines anise with dandelion (Taraxacum officinale) root, agrimony (Agrimonia eupatoria), and ginger (Zingiber officinale), and a blend for children’s gut health containing anise, licorice (Glycyrrhiza glabra), marshmallow (Althaea officinalis), chamomile (Matricaria recutita), nettle (Urtica dioica), and yarrow (Achillea millefolium) (Romm, 2000).

In the respiratory system, the aromatic and antispasmodic properties of anise are again appreciated by modern herbalists, with the constituents carvone and antheol attributed with “bronchodilating, expectorating, antimicrobial, antispasmodic, and anti-inflammatory properties” (Stansbury 2018b, pg. 176).

Anise may benefit lung conditions in both adults and children (Romm, 2000; Sinadinos, 2008; Stansbury, 2018b). It has been used by herbalists to open the airways and address bronchospasms and coughs in the form of teas, tinctures, or essential oils in inhalants, steams, or applied topically (Stansbury, 2018b). Teas, tinctures, glycerites, and syrups can ease children’s coughs or bronchitis  and facilitate expectoration (Romm, 2000), especially supporting a cough once it “has turned loose and productive” (Bove 2001, p. 86).

Some formulas suggested by herbalists include adding anise to thyme (Thymus vulgaris), marshmallow, red clover (Trifolium pratense), echinacea (Echinacea spp.), mullein (Verbascum thapsus), and elecampane (Inula helenium) for coughs, or with elderflower (Sambucus nigra), ginkgo (Ginkgo biloba), astragalus (Astragalus membranaceus), and licorice for allergies in children (Romm, 2000).

Because anise may relax lungs and facilitate expectoration, herbalists also value its support for chronic asthma in children and adults (Bove, 2001; Cabrera, 2002; Romm, 2000; Stansbury, 2018b). Stanbury (2018b) combines anise with lobelia (Lobelia inflata), thyme, eucalyptus (Eucalyptus globulus), peppermint (Mentha x piperita), licorice, mullein, and ginkgo for asthma, while for children’s asthma Romm (2000) suggests a combination of anise with elecampane, fennel, fenugreek (Trigonella foenum-graecum), and thyme.

Anise has played a role in addressing allergies characterized by damp symptoms, as it has diaphoretic and drying qualities (Bergner, 2005). Herbalists have utilized it for earache due to congestion (Romm, 2000). Modern research affirms its use for nasal congestion. In a study of 48 patients with chronic rhinosinusitis without polyps, those receiving 2 drops of a Pimpinella anisum-based herbal medicine in each nostril every 12 hours experienced greater improvement in postnasal drip and nasal obstruction than those receiving a fluticasone nasal spray in a single-blinded randomized trial (Vazifehkah et al., 2016).

Anise is valued by herbalists for its galactagogue action (Frawley & Lad, 1988) and it is commonly included in formulas to support breast milk production. Some common herbs it is combined with for this application include blessed thistle (Cnicus benedictus), raspberry (Rubus spp.) leaf, and nettle leaf (Weed, 1986).

Anise has a folk history of supporting menopausal complaints which has been verified by modern research as well. In an Iranian randomized, controlled clinical trial, consumption of capsules containing 330 mg Pimpinella anisum extract 3 times a day for 4 weeks decreased frequency and subjective severity of hot flashes compared to placebo in 72 postmenopausal rural women who were within the first 3 years of menopause (Nahidi et al., 2012).

Anise may also benefit the cardiovascular system due to its content of various coumarins, which have shown hypotensive action and hypolipidemic effects (Stansbury, 2018b). Stansbury writes that umbelliferone, found throughout the Apiaceae family, may “act on capillary, venule, and vein permeability, increasing fluid uptake and return from the tissues to general circulation… and may inhibit aberrant clotting by having anti-inflammatory and platelet antiaggregating effects,” (p. 79).

Traditional Persian medicine texts describe anise as capable of elating the mood and ameliorating depression, and serving as a tonic for the heart and spirit. This may be related to its reputation as an aphrodisiac, as well (Sinadinos, 2008).

A modern clinical trial showed that enteric coated capsules of 200 mg anise oil taken for 4 weeks improved depression scores in comparison to an active control of 187 mg Colpermin or placebo in a double-blind placebo-controlled clinical trial of 120 patients with mild to moderate depression and irritable bowel syndrome (Mosaffa-Jahromi et al., 2017). Another study (mentioned above) found anise improved quality of life scores related to mental health among patients with postprandial distress syndrome (Ghoshegir et al., 2014). This relationship between the mood and digestive health is likely not a coincidence. Iranian medical researchers Maryam Mosaffa-Jahromi et al. (2017) provide the following cogent analysis drawing upon traditional medical theory to explain their results:

The mechanism of action by traditional Persian medicine practitioners’ view is clear. Depression is an ailment related to imbalance of psychic faculty (Quwwat-e-nafsaniya) and preponderance of black bile. Quwwat-e-nafsaniya obtains its matter from spirit of the heart (Rooh-e-haivaniya). Anise oil is a proper tonic for heart and spirit of the heart. According to traditional Persian medicine treatment procedures, depression has cold nature and the spirit is almost motionless in this mental disorder. Anise oil is warm enough to heat the spirit and elevate the mood (Mofarrih). Therefore, the treatment approach should be focused on strengthening of psychic faculty and/or purifying excess black bile. Consequently, herbal medicines such as saffron and anise with such a quality could be effective in alleviating the symptoms of depression ( para. 19).

Topical administration of the essential oil in a base oil like olive oil  (diluted to about 2%, or 10-12 drops essential oil per ounce of base oil) may ease menstrual cramps, distension, nausea, tight muscles, and coughs (Keville & Green, 2009; Stansbury, 2018a). Essential oils must always be diluted before application to the skin to avoid the potential for injury. Aromatherapists diffuse the essential oil to support emotional balance, ease overwork-related stress, as an aphrodisiac, and to soothe heartache (Keville & Green, 2009); for this application, about 3 drops can be added to a bowl of hot water to diffuse into the room (Keville & Green, 2009).

Adult Dose

Tea: 8-12 fl oz 4x/daily of a hot infusion, cold infusion, or decoction (Sinadinos, 2008) made with 1-2 tsp anise seed infused in 8 fl oz water just off the boil, 3x/day (Hoffmann, 2003).

Tincture:15-60 drops dried seed (1:5, 60%) or fresh seed (1:2, 70-95%) 4x/day  (Sinadinos, 2008); 1-4 mL (1:5, 40%) 3x/day (Hoffmann, 2003).

Glycerite: 15-60 drops (1:5, 40% glycerin, 30% alcohol, 30% water) 4x/day (Sinadinos, 2008).

Capsules: 2-4 “00” capsules filled with powdered seed, up to 3x/day (Sinadinos, 2008).

Safety

Anise is a common culinary spice used globally. Avoid use of the essential oil or alcohol extracts during pregnancy (Gardner & McGuffin, 2013). There are no known contraindications of the crude seed and its preparations in lactating women, children, and infants (Romm, 2000; Sinadinos, 2008).

Avoid internal use of anise in individuals with sodium-related hypertension and those taking diuretic medications or blood pressure-lowering agents (Sinadinos, 2008).

Topical allergic reactions or cross-reactivity among Apiaceae plants are uncommon but possible; anethole may be responsible (Keville & Green, 2009). Anise has phytoestrogenic constituents (Keville & Green, 2009).
