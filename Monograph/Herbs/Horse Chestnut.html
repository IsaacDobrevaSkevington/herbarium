Horse Chestnut

Native To

The Balkan peninsula, the Republic of Türkiye, and Turkmenistan (Royal Botanic Gardens Kew [RBGK], n.d.).

Geographic Distribution

Widely distributed throughout the temperate zone including Europe, North America, parts of Russia, and the South Island of New Zealand (RBGK, n.d.).

Botanical Description

The European horse chestnut is a large, deciduous, broad-leaved tree with a wide-spreading oval crown that can grow up to 128 feet (39 m) tall (Ravazzi & Caudullo, 2016). The trunk can measure up to 74 inches (190 cm) in diameter and is covered with a dark, gray-brown bark that becomes scaly with age, while.the twigs are pale gray to brown, smooth, and covered with pale lenticels (Thomas et al., 2019). The resinous buds are about 1-2 inches (2.5-5 cm), red-brown, and very sticky. The opposite, dark-green, palmate leaves can grow up to 23 inches (60 cm) wide and have a long petiole; while each leaf consists of 5-7 irregularly serrated leaflets that are 3-10 inches (8-25 cm) long (Thomas et al., 2019). The large pyramidal inflorescences are andromonoecious, meaning that the tree produces male flowers at the top of the panicle and hermaphrodite flowers below (Thomas et al., 2019). Flowers (0.8 inches or 2 cm) are zygomorphic, having one symmetrical plane, with five sepals forming a tubular, toothed calyx and white petals decorated with yellow basal spots that turn pink. Only a few basal flowers of each inflorescence develop the almost globe-shaped spiny fruit (5-8 cm) containing one to sometimes three seeds (Ravazzi & Caudullo, 2016). The reddish-brown seeds are shiny, smooth, and with a large, white mark, which is the hilum, or point of attachment (Thomas et al., 2019).

Key Constituents

Seed: Saponins: crypto-, α- and β-escin (3-5%) (Dudek-Makuch & Studzińska-Sroka, 2015); coumarin glycosides: esculetin, fraxin, scopolin; flavonoids: kaempferol, quercetin, astragalin, isoquercetin, rutin, leucocyanidin (Barnes et al., 2007), troxerutin, diosmin, hesperidin, proanthocyanidins (Dudek-Makuch & Studzińska-Sroka, 2015); tannins, allantoin, amino acids, choline, citric acid, phytosterol (Barnes et al., 2007), and starch(Dudek-Makuch & Studzińska-Sroka, 2015).

Bark: Coumarin glycosides: esculin, fraxin, scopolin, esculetin, fraxetin, scopoletin; tannins (up to 2%); flavonoids: anthocyanins, catechin derivatives; traces of escin (European Medicines Agency [EMA], 2012); quebrachitol, allantoin, citric acid, fatty oil (Bellini & Nin, 2005).

Flower: Flavonoids including kaempferol and quercetin derivatives (Dudek-Makuch & Matławska, 2011); amino acids (Bellini & Nin, 2005).

Leaf: Flavonoids, amino acids, phenolic acids, α-carotene, vitamin C, tannins, resin, quebrachitol, traces of fraxin and esculin (Bellini & Nin, 2005).

Sustainability

The horse chestnut is a common ornamental tree in the temperate climate zone. Wild populations of trees are endangered in Bulgaria and Greece, and critically endangered in Albania (Rivers et al., 2019). In its native region, both cultivated and wild trees have been frequently infested by the horse chestnut leaf miner (Cameraria ochridella), an invasive species that causes early leaf browning followed by defoliation and tree weakening, which may interfere with tree reproduction in natural populations (Ravazzi & Caudullo, 2016).

Harvesting Guidelines

Horse chestnut seeds fall to the ground when ripe in the early autumn and can easily be collected (Whitten, 2004). However, the synthesis of saponins and flavonoids varies during the maturation of the seed. Research by Kędzierski et al. (2016) showed that the highest content of active compounds was measured between 13 and 16 weeks from the beginning of blooming and that escin increased by 90% between 12 and 20 weeks, and flavonoids by 120% between 7 and 12 weeks after blooming. To facilitate the drying process, the seeds are removed from their prickly case, split or chopped into small pieces, and dried at a temperature of up to 113°F (45°C) (Whitten, 2004).

The bark is usually stripped from young branches(EMA, 2012). However, esculin content is higher in mature bark (Stanić et al., 1999); however when harvesting from any tree it is important to consider the health of the tree and never harvest from the trunk, but opt for smaller branches. The bark is harvested in the spring before the sap flow starts, and can be dried by spreading into a single layer on screens stored in a well-ventilated room at a maximum of 122°F (50°C) or dried in the shade (Neshev & Landzhev, 1989).

Uses

Horse chestnut is widely known as an ornamental tree in parks, gardens, and boulevards. With its beautiful flowers and dense leaf canopy in the summer, it is a characteristic tree in German beer gardens where it used to help keep the beer cellars cool once upon a time (Loenhart, 2002). The seeds are also known as conkers, the same name as the children’s game popularized in the mid-19th century in England and still played today. The horse chestnut is attached to a string, and two players strike each other’s conkers until one is broken (Prance & Nesbit, 2005; Watts, 2007).

This majestic tree is also a valued botanical both in herbalism and official medicine; escin, the main bioactive principle of the seeds, is still extracted from horse chestnuts on an industrial scale for its pharmacological applications (Kedzierski et al., 2016). According to the European Medicines Agency (2020), extract of horse chestnut seed is internally and topically indicated for chronic venous insufficiency (CVI), a condition where the blood flow from the leg veins to the heart is inhibited and is characterized by edema, varicose veins, heavy legs, pain or cramps, itching, and/or skin changes in the legs.

Almost every part of the horse chestnut is applied in traditional herbalism, but the seeds and bark are mostly commonly used. Different plant parts are characterized by a dominant class of bioactive compounds; escin, a complex mixture of saponins found in the seeds, flavonoids in the leaves and flowers, and coumarin glycosides such as esculin and fraxin in the bark (Stanić et al., 1999). Many of the bioactive properties of the seeds are attributed to escin, and its anti-inflammatory, anti-edematous, antioxidant, and vascular tonic properties are well established (Dickson et al., 2004; Gallelli, 2019).

The Aesculus genus includes up to 25 species native to North America, the Balkans, East Asia, and India (Bellini & Nin, 2005). The horse chestnut is not related to the edible sweet chestnut (Castanea sativa), and its raw seeds are astringent and should not be consumed due to its toxic principles. The popular name “horse chestnut” is often applied to other species of the genus Aesculus, including the California buckeye (A. californica) and Ohio buckeye (A. glabra). However, while some of the bioactive compounds are commonly present in several species of the genus Aesculus (Zhang et al., 2010), they should not substitute A. hippocastanum, as some species, such as A. glabra, are considered highly toxic to humans and animals (Nesom, 2006).

Several species of the Aesculus genus have a wide variety of uses. For example, Aesculus californica (Californian buckeye), or disǎ’ kalǐ (brittle tree) in Northern Pomo language, is native to North America and has a history of use by several Indigenous Californian peoples including the Pomo, Costanoan, Salinan, Kitanemuk, Serrano, Wappo, Sierra Miwok, Coast Miwok, Chumash, Kawaiisu, and Northern Maidu (Anderson & Roderick, 2001). Historically, the seeds were used as a staple food, especially when few other plant resources were available (Welch, 2013), and had to be prepared via a lengthy leaching and heating process to remove toxic compounds (Anderson & Roderick, 2001). Gladys Tantaquidgeon (1942), a Mohegan medicine woman and anthropologist, recorded several traditional uses of A. glabra, “big acorn tree” or “fish peyote” by the Delaware peoples, including its use as a fish poison, making it easier to catch them, and the seeds, ground and mixed with sweet oil or tallow, could be made into a poultice for earache. 

Aesculus indica (Himalayan chestnut), locally called khanor, grows in the forests of northwestern Himalaya and is used in some parts of Himachal Pradesh where its seeds have culinary use after leaching them in running water for several days to remove toxic compounds, then drying them and grinding into a flour called tattwakhar (Rajasekaran & Singh, 2009). This flour is used to make chapatti or a sweet porridge called halwa, often served at weddings, while the seeds are sometimes applied to aid wound healing, and to ease symptoms of rheumatism (Rajasekaran & Singh, 2009).

The seeds of the native Chinese species Aesculus chinensis and A. wilsonii were first recorded in Ben Cao Gang Mu (Xi & Gong, 2017), also known as the Compendium of Materia Medica or Great Pharmacopoeia, published in the late 16th century during the Ming Dynasty. In Chinese traditional medicine, the seeds are applied to rectify liver-stomach qi stagnation, ease pain, and kill intestinal parasites (Xi & Gong, 2017). Chinese horse chestnut is listed in the Chinese Pharmacopeia to soothe gastrointestinal complaints such as bloating and pain. Research has shown that the bioactive compound escin has strong anti-inflammatory properties and can help protect and stimulate the motility of the digestive tract (Yang et al., 2020).

In its native range, the European horse chestnut is mainly applied for issues related to venous insufficiency and hemorrhoids (Ezberci & Ünal, 2018; Neshev & Landzhev, 1989; Iatrou & Kokkalou, 1997). Interestingly, in traditional herbalism of the Düzce Province in Türkiye, the peeled and crushed seeds are mixed with water and taken internally for colds, stomachache, toothache, and constipation (Gürbüz et al., 2009), while in the Amasya Province a decoction of the seeds is used for arteriosclerosis (Ezer & Arisan, 2006). In Bulgaria, the ground seeds are mixed with Rakia, a local brandy made of grapes or plums, and applied to the skin to ease rheumatic pains and sciatica (Neshev & Landzhev, 1989), while the beaten fresh leaves are indicated for ulcers and podagra (gout) (Stoicheva et al., 1986). In Greece, several uses have been recorded including for dysentery, ulcers and boils, rheumatism, and tuberculosis (Iatrou & Kokkalou, 1997). 

The genus name Aesculus finds its roots in the Latin esca, meaning “food” (Barton & Castle, 1845). Interestingly, the Romans used this name for an oak species (Quercus aesculus), but it is not clear why the name transferred to the horse chestnut. The common name is derived from the botanical species name hippocastanum, meaning horse chestnut in ancient Greek. Several theories have been proposed regarding the origin of the tree’s name. For example, Watts (2007) explains that “horse” as part of a plant name often refers to coarseness or largeness. Others have noted that when a leaf breaks from the twig, it leaves a scar in the shape of a horseshoe, including its nails (Comstock, 1911/1967). However, the most frequently cited explanation of its name is connected to the way the tree became introduced to Western Europe. In 1557, Flemish botanist and physician Willem Quackelbeen (1527-1561), who resided in Constantinople (present-day Istanbul), described in a letter to his colleague, Italian botanist physician Pietro Andrea Mattioli (1501-1577) how the alleys of the city were planted with these interesting trees, the seeds of which the Turkish people used to feed their horses, three to four at a time, and ease cough, shortness of breath, and intestinal parasites in the animals (Lack, 2002).

Mattioli received a horse chestnut seed and leaf specimen along with the letter and, based on Quackelbeen’s description, called the tree Castanea equina, equus being the Latin name for a horse. The physician proceeded to make a woodcut of the tree, which was the first printed illustration of the horse chestnut in Europe, and included it for the very first time in his herbal New Kreüterbuch (Mattioli, 1563; Lack, 2002). From then on, the horse chestnut made an appearance in European herbals including The Herball by John Gerard (1633/2015), where he repeats Mattioli’s description of uses, without additional observations. 

The Flemish botanist and director of the Viennese herb garden, Carolus Clusius (1526-1609), was key to the further distribution of the horse chestnut to the West by sending seeds to all his relations throughout the continent. By the 17th century, the horse chestnut was widely cultivated throughout Europe as an ornamental (Lack, 2002), and considerable efforts were made to find uses for its abundant seeds. These uses included cattle fodder, which was said to make their milk very rich. Importantly, due to the toxic compounds, seeds had to be steeped in lime water, washed, and boiled before they were edible (Barton & Castle, 1845; Bellini & Nin, 2005; Grieve, 1931/1971). Other applications included the creation of rot-resistant glue, used by bookbinders for an adhesive board of herbaria (Bellini & Nin, 2005), and the saponaceous substance released by seeds boiled and steeped in water which substituted soap, and was used to bleach hemp, flax, and wool (Barton & Castle, 1845). Interestingly, Barton & Castle (1845) also reported that as a dye for wool, the leaves produce a pale chestnut brown color, while the bark produces a yellow color. Saponins are great surfactants, and horse chestnuts are still used today to make a zero-waste, dermatologically well-tolerated laundry detergent (Neodoma, 2015).

Interestingly, horse chestnut was an indicator plant for weather forecasting in some regions. For example, in Wales, it was said that the leaves would spread like a fan when warm weather was coming and would point downwards when rain was to be expected (Watts, 2007). While in Bulgaria, if the horse chestnut flowers a second time in the same year, a long and severe winter would lay ahead (Nedelcheva & Dogan, 2011).

Other magical practices recorded in several countries, including England and North America, revolve around carrying a horse chestnut seed in your pocket to prevent or relieve rheumatism (Watts, 2007). The “negative energy” was believed to be absorbed by the seed. There are several variations of this practice; sometimes a stolen seed should be used, or healthy children should collect the seeds and make them into a necklace (Watts, 2007). It’s not clear where this practice comes from, but it may be rooted in the doctrine of signatures; perhaps people saw a resemblance between the conkers and rheumatoid nodules.

Escin and other horse chestnut extracts are commonly used in cosmetics. Their tonifying and astringent properties make it a frequent additive in shampoos, foaming footbaths, and various gels and ointments for rosacea, cellulitis, and hair loss (Bellini & Nin, 2005). Horse chestnut also played a role in the development of sunscreens. In 1911, Dr. Unna, a German dermatologist, developed a thick paste containing esculin derivatives, used to intercept and filter out ultraviolet radiation (the Modern Materia Medica, 1912; Urbach, 2001).

Solar ultraviolet radiation (UV) is responsible for the increased production of cellular reactive oxygen species that can lead to DNA damage. It is known that horse chestnut preparations contain high levels of antioxidant compounds (Vašková et al., 2015). In vitro research by Makino et al. (2017) found that an alcohol extract of horse chestnut seed shell contained a higher phenolic content than green tea, and confirmed the potential use against UVB-induced DNA damage. De Pinho et al., (2013) looked into a commercial 50% hydroalcoholic horse chestnut extract and also confirmed a high level of radical scavenging and photoprotective potential in vitro, suggesting that the extracts can indeed be used to develop sunscreen products.

Traditionally, various parts of the horse chestnut have been applied for gastrointestinal complaints including diarrhea, to ease pain caused by conditions such as neuralgia, rheumatism, and muscle aches, as well as phlebitis, hemorrhoids, and an enlarged prostate (Barnes et al., 2007; Grieve, 1931/1971; Neshev & Ladzhev, 1989; Stoicheva et al., 1986). A decoction made from horse chestnut bark was also tried as a cheap replacement for the quinine-rich bark of cinchona (Cinchona pubescens), which was highly sought after in Europe during the 18th century for its strong febrifugal properties and applications in malaria (Bellini & Nin, 2005; Barton & Castle, 1845). This use became obsolete due to a lack of activity (Barton & Castle, 1845; Schneider, 1968), but is still suggested in some herbals.

In modern herbalism, horse chestnut preparations are valued for their vascular protective and venotonic actions, and are indicated in venous problems for edema of the lower limbs, and as an antiecchymotic, for external applications to ease hematoma or to reduce swelling in bruises (Mills & Bone, 2000; Fintelmann & Weiss, 2002). Horse chestnut’s anti-inflammatory and analgesic properties were discovered in the early 20th century, and since then horse chestnut is widely used for chronic venous insufficiency (Bellini & Nin, 2005).

The efficacy of horse chestnut in these conditions is well established in clinical research. A widely cited meta-review by Pittler & Ernst (2012) of seventeen randomized, placebo-controlled trials suggests that standardized horse chestnut extract can reduce itching, leg pain, edema, leg volume and circumference associated with chronic venous insufficiency (CVI). Dudek-Makuch & Studzińska-Sroka (2015) add that subjective symptoms such as calf muscle spasms and fatigue were also reduced. The researchers suggest that horse chestnut preparations can be useful as a good alternative to wearing compression socks. The active compounds of horse chestnut preparations are believed to act synergistically in CVI; the anti-inflammatory properties of flavonoids, astringent tannins to help strengthen the vessel walls, and the saponin escin can increase venous tone by inhibiting the enzymes elastase and hyaluronidase, which are raised in those with CVI (Dickson et al., 2004).

Herbalist Matthew Wood (2004; 2008) explains that horse chestnut seeds increase circulation and improve the tone of the veins by uniting a strong warming stimulation with astringency. Wood describes the tree as a classic herbal for passive venous congestion, where veins are enlarged, and full of blood that cannot flow freely through them. This poor circulation can result in neuromuscular tension, phlebitis in the legs, hemorrhoids, congested liver, and pelvic and uterine congestion, and may trigger the heart to pump with more strength, resulting in high blood pressure. Sometimes this is also accompanied by asthmatic problems due to the stasis in the pulmonary veins (Wood, 2004; Wood, 2008). Furthermore, he suggests combining the pungent, stimulating astringent horse chestnut with Collinsonia and Hamamelis for hemorrhoids and vein and heart issues.

The popular application in hemorrhoids might be explained by the flavonoid content of horse chestnut preparations, which some studies have concluded reduce bleeding, pain, and itching associated with the condition, and decrease the risk of persistent symptoms. However, a meta-study by Alonso-Coello et al. (2006) concluded that better designed trials are needed due to lack of high quality evidence. Accessible clinical research into the efficacy of horse chestnut in hemorrhoids is lacking, but one study involving 80 patients who used a gel containing escin three times per day suggested that 81% of participants experienced decreased hemorrhoidal bleeding and edema after 6 days, and 94% of the participants after 2 weeks of use (Pirard et al., 1976 cited in Ezberci & Ünal, 2018).

According to the doctrine of signatures, the horse chestnut stands for self-control, light in the darkness, seriousness, and cheerfulness, and can be applied when thoughts are circling round and round, and a lack of inner guidance is establishing physical complaints (Kalbermatten, 2021). Herbalist Matthew Wood (2008) proposes that horse chestnut preparations are particularly helpful when there is no room between thoughts, especially when this is reflected in the pulse, where it appears that there is not enough room between heartbeats. According to Wood (2008), this is a clear sign of mental tension and high blood pressure for which he recommends the horse chestnut.

Horse chestnut bark is traditionally applied externally in the form of baths or compresses for discomfort and heaviness in the legs due to minor vascular insufficiency (EMA, 2012), and internally to reduce diarrhea (Bisset, 1994). In traditional herbalism of several countries including Bulgaria, Russia, and Wales, a decoction made with bark and sometimes leaf is used for varicose veins, and also to make suppositories for hemorrhoids (Neshev & Landzhev, 1989; Zevin, 1997; Watts, 2007). Interestingly, while the venotonic properties are often ascribed to escin, the bark, which contains only traces, is also applied in conditions related to vascular insufficiency. However, its mechanism of action is less well-researched. The efficacy of the bark is mainly attributed to its polyphenolic content with a supporting role of coumarin glycosides, of which the former has strong antioxidative activity (Owczarek et al., 2021), and also plays a role in other vasoprotective preparations like grape seed extract (Bijak et al., 2012). In addition, oxidative stress created by too many free radicals in the body, and hemostasis disorders connected with inflammation, often occur concurrently with vascular damage including varicose veins and hemorrhoids (Owczarek et al., 2021).

Furthermore, the antibacterial properties of active compounds in horse chestnut have been demonstrated in various studies. In-vitro research has shown that β-escin significantly inhibits Gram-positive bacteria Staphylococcus epidermis and S. aureus in a concentration-dependent manner (Dahash et al., 2021).

Adult Dose

Decoction: 1-2 g/day dried seed (Mills & Bone, 2005); 275 mg bark 3-6/day (EMA, 2012).

Tincture (dried seed): 1-4 mL (1:5 in 40%) 3x/day (Hoffmann, 2003).

Safety

Horse chestnut seed and bark preparations are generally considered safe for adults when the recommended daily dosage is not exceeded (EMA, 2020). While preparations are usually well tolerated, a few cases of side effects have been noted during clinical studies, including headache, gastrointestinal complaints, vertigo, itching, and allergic reactions (Pittler & Ernst, 2012; Dudek-Makuch & Studzińska-Sroka, 2015).

The European Medicines Agency (2020) suggests that preparations should be taken at least 4 weeks before desirable effects are noticed. However, persons with thrombophlebitis, swollen legs, and inflammation, conditions for which horse chestnut is traditionally indicated, should consult a medical professional before using horse chestnut preparations, especially when suffering from cardiac or renal insufficiency (EMA, 2020).

Herbalists Mills and Bone (2005) add that no preparations should be used on broken and/or ulcerated skin, and caution that persons with celiac disease, fat malabsorption, vitamins A, D, E, and K deficiency, and cholestasis should avoid horse chestnut due to the high amount of saponins. The use of horse chestnut preparations is not recommended during pregnancy and lactation, as well as in children and adolescents, as safety has not been established (EMA, 2020).

Furthermore, horse chestnut preparations are known anticoagulants, and may lead to an increased risk of bleeding when used in combination with warfarin or other anticoagulant and antiplatelet pharmaceuticals (Argento et al., 2000).

Cases of poisoning in humans are rare but have been reported when the seeds were confused for edible chestnuts (Yi & Lee, 2021), and after nasally ingesting horse chestnut snuff powder (Zając et al., 2014). Both instances required hospital treatment but had no lasting effects.

According to the Veterinary Teaching Hospital of Colorado State University (2022), plant parts of several Aesculus species are toxic to animals due to the glycosides esculin and fraxin. The nectar of the Californian buckeye (A. californica) is poisonous to honeybees, and hives should not be kept in the vicinity of the trees. Horse chestnut is toxic to poultry and can cause death (Bellini & Nin, 2005).
