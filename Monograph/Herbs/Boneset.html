Boneset

Eupatorium perfoliatum

Geographic Distribution

Eastern United States and Canada

Botanical Description

Boneset grows in wet soils in the eastern half of the United States and Canada in low-lying woods, stream banks, along ponds and lakes, marshes, swamps meadows, prairies, thickets, and wet roadside ditches (Keener et al., 2020; Missouri Botanical Garden, n.d.). The plant grows in clumps 3-4 feet wide with stems that reach 4-6 feet tall (Missouri Botanical Garden, n.d.). The hairy stems bear pairs of opposite, wrinkled, lanceolate leaves with serrate margins and distinctive leaf bases that fuse around the stem, appearing as if the stem grew through the leaves (Keener et al., 2020; Missouri Botanical Garden, n.d.). This characteristic, called perfoliate from the word perfoliatus in Latin, contributed to the plant’s botanical name.

The tiny, fluffy, white flowers are arranged into small heads, which in turn are arranged into compound corymbs (flat topped clusters) (Keener et al., 2020) which bloom from July to September (Missouri Botanical Garden, n.d.).

Key Constituents

Sesquiterpene lactones (e.g., eufoliatin, eufoliatorin, euperfolin, euperfolitin); flavonoids (e.g., astragalin, quercetin, eupatorin, rutin); polysaccharides (methylglucuronoxylan) (Buhner, 2005; Hoffmann, 2003).

Sustainability

Boneset is commonly found, and beyond general concern for wetland habitat health in the United States, there are no known sustainability issues (Sinadinos, 2008).

Harvesting Guidelines

For preparation of tincture, boneset stems are typically harvested soon after flowers open in the months of August and September (Hoffmann, 1998). If harvested for dried herb, the leafy stems are cut just before flowering, as the flowers degrade into fluff upon drying (Buhner, 2005). Boneset sometimes grows in polluted areas such as roadside ditches or low areas subject to toxic agricultural runoff; these areas should be avoided when wild harvesting (Sturdivant & Blakley, 1999).

Uses

A plant with a rich history in eastern Canada and the eastern United States, boneset has been tagged with many common names, including: common boneset, thoroughwort, common thoroughwort, agueweed, sweating plant, and feverwort. The names refer to its distinctive leaves as well as its use as a diaphoretic herb for fevers and ague, the latter being defined as a “fever (such as malaria) marked by paroxysms [sudden fits] of chills, fever, and sweating that recur at regular intervals” (Merriam-Webster, n.d., para. 1).

While the origin of the name “boneset” is somewhat debated, most historical references say it likely derives from observations that the plant eases pain in the limbs and muscles associated with febrile diseases, including a viral epidemic referred to as “break-bone fever”(Boericke, 1927; Cook, 1869; Grieve, 1931/1982). This said, historical records exist of folk practitioners, including both rural white and indigenous practitioners, reporting success when using the plant to support the healing process of broken bones (Wood, 1997).

Boneset has historically been a very popular, widely used plant in the United States, primarily due to its febrifuge action; herbalist Maude Grieve (1931/1982) noted that “probably no plant in American domestic practice [had] more extensive and frequent use” (p. 118). Herbalist Matthew Wood (1997) writes it was “one of the few native herbs used by doctors of every school—allopathic, Thompsonian, eclectic, and homeopathic—representing one of the few therapeutic commonalities of the era” (p. 260).

Physiomedicalist Dr. William Cook (1869) claimed boneset “deserves to be esteemed as one of the most useful medicines of the people” (p. 239). Eclectic physician Dr. Harvey Wickes Felter (1922) stated, “In every epidemic of influenza it has been used with great advantage. During the severe pandemic of 1918-19 it was one of the safest and most successful remedies employed and contributed much to the successful management of the disease under Eclectic treatment” (para. 9). Modern herbalist Paul Bergner (2003b) notes boneset was “used to treat influenza and other viral respiratory infections continuously in the U.S. since before first contact with the Europeans” (p. 16).

Famous homeopathic physician William Boericke (1927) also notes boneset’s popularity in “miasmatic districts, along rivers, marshes, etc., and in all conditions where there is a great deal of bone-pain”(p. 274), while Felter (1922) writes that it was well known for its antiperiodic properties in “ague districts” (para. 8). These terms sometimes referred to wetland areas that were prone to febrile diseases carried by mosquitoes, and coincidentally were also potentially favorable habitats for the moisture-loving boneset.

Boneset was popular in the United States during a period in which herbal practitioners were meticulously documenting the constellation of symptoms that best indicated the use of a specific plant. Boericke (1927) described the following ultra-specific indications for boneset tincture in his Pocket Manual of Homeopathic Materia Medica: throbbing, periodical headache with pressure “as if a cap of lead [is] pressed over the whole skull” (p. 274); vertigo; vomiting; cracks in the corners of the mouth; yellow coated tongue; strong thirst preceding morning chills; inflamed sinuses and sneezing; chronic loose cough that is relieved by getting on hands and knees; a sore chest that requires support; aching in the back and deep in the bones of extremities; edema; and inflamed joints. For a more extensive list of Boericke’s indications, see the reference.

These types of practitioners strived to address the client as a whole, rather than focus only on the imbalance, thus the aim was to recognize patterns of symptoms in order to choose the precise herbal approach for the unique presentation of each client.

Cook (1869) characterized boneset as an almost pure relaxant (according to the tissue state model) with slow, persistent action, that especially acts on internal smooth muscles but also peripheral nervous system and skin. He suggested a cold infusion for conditions such as irritable dyspepsia, and to relax the hepatic system and promote bile production and flow, leading to a mild laxative effect that benefits habitual constipation with thirst and dry feces (Cook, 1869). Indications included “skin diseases of hepatic origin” and recovery from febrile conditions, “especially intermittent and bilious fever” (Cook, 1869, p. 239). In these cases he suggested combining boneset with stimulating tonics such as gentian (Gentiana lutea) root, goldenseal (Hydrastis canadensis) root, wormwood (Artemisia absinthium) leaf and flower, or small amounts of cayenne (Capsicum annuum) fruit (Cook, 1869).

Cook (1869) discouraged the use of boneset for those with “cold and sluggish states of the stomach, to torpor of the liver and bowels with accompanied with flaccidity of the tissues, to low intermittents, nor as a tonic in any case where the bowels are inclined to free action” (p. 238).

Cook (1869) also noted boneset’s “decided influence upon the lungs” (p. 238), useful for dull lung aches or chronic cough when combined with stimulating herbs. He reported a soothing and toning influence on respiratory organs when imbibed as a warm or cold infusion. Felter (1922) affirmed the use of boneset for easing cough, and stated the herb acted “best in that occurring in the aged and debilitated, where there is an abundance of secretion, but lack of power to expel it” (para. 10).

Cook (1869) wrote that a warm infusion reportedly brought about slow, gentle, persistent diaphoresis useful for febrile illness, including those marked by shivering chills. Boneset was observed to be antispasmodic especially when combined with a diffusive stimulant like prickly ash (Zanthoxylum americanum) bark(Cook, 1869).

Modern usage is strongly informed by boneset’s historic applications. Dr. Jillian Stansbury (2018a) states, “Eupatorium is specific for coated tongue and cracks at the corners of the mouth, for aching back and muscles associated with the flu, and for unusual thirst, chilliness, and nausea associated with the flu” (p. 242)—indications that evoke those of Boericke. Like the herbalists of the past, modern herbalists also differentiate between the actions elicited by boneset when consumed in preparations that are hot versus cool in temperature, and hope to match the herb to the person, as opposed to the imbalance.

Conditions for which herbalists suggest cool preparations of boneset are typically subacute, chronic, or constitutional in nature. Boneset is an alterative, defined as an herb “having a steady, moderate, reliable, long-term effect on specific organs/systems or on the entire body: improves assimilation of nutrients, enhances detoxification, [and] corrects ‘Bad Blood’ syndrome” (Ganora, 2015, p. 1).

Boneset’s profound bitter taste is put into action by herbalists as a short-term digestive bitter, consumed prior to a meal to stimulate gastrointestinal secretions (Sinadinos, 2008). Its tonifying effect on mucous membranes can be especially supportive to effectively retrain the digestive system during the recovery period after gastrointestinal infections or febrile illness such as intermittent fever (Sinadinos, 2008; Wood, 1997). Boneset may also benefit elders suffering from decreased digestive secretions, or those with nervous system-related gastrointestinal issues, as it can support parasympathetic stimulation (Sinadinos, 2008).

Boneset has aperient action; stimulation of the secretion of bile can soften hard stools (Sinadinos, 2008). Its cholagogue and choleretic actions also aid in fat digestion and support the liver’s detoxification process, which may benefit skin issues such as eczema and psoriasis (Hoffmann, 1998; Sinadinos, 2008).

For acute conditions, boneset is most commonly consumed hot, either as a hot tea or tincture in hot water; this activates its diaphoretic action, which is of special value in supporting those experiencing febrile illnesses such as influenza (Sinadinos, 2008). Diaphoresis relaxes peripheral tension and brings circulation to capillaries, thus improving ventilation through the skin, and increases secretions of dry mucous membranes (Bergner, 2003a; Sinadinos, 2008). The diaphoretic action has been employed to break fevers, especially those accompanied by intermittent chills and/or with pain in the muscles, joints, or bones (Hoffmann, 1998; Sinadinos, 2008; Wood, 1997). Boneset combines well with other diaphoretic herbs, such as yarrow (Achillea millefolium) leaves and flowers, elder (Sambucus nigra) flowers, cayenne (Capsicum annuum) fruit, or ginger (Zingiber officinale) rhizome, in either tea or tincture formulas (Hoffmann, 1998).

Paul Bergner recruited boneset during the West Nile encephalitis outbreak in Colorado in the early 2000s (Bergner, 2003a; Bergner, 2004). He cites the use of a formula including boneset with echinacea (Echinacea angustifolia) root, osha (Ligusticum porteri) root, red root (Ceanothus velutinus), and licorice (Glycyrrhiza glabra) root in tincture form in a case study of a client suffering from West Nile encephalitis during an outbreak in Colorado (Bergner, 2004).

One case study from Bergner’s (2004) Medical Herbalism Journal discussed a 33-year-old female with assumed West Nile virus who had been discharged from the hospital. She experienced improvement to head and back pain within a few hours and diminishment of other symptoms over the course of 10 days after consuming frequent doses of a tea blend. This blend consisted of equal parts of boneset, peppermint (Mentha x piperita) leaf, yarrow (Achillea millefolium) leaf and flower, skullcap (Scutellaria lateriflora) leaf, and lavender (Lavandula angustifolia) flower, which was formulated to provide tonic and relaxant diaphoretic actions to relax the extreme tension in her system, especially at the level of the meninges. She resumed the same blend and experienced resolution of her symptoms after a relapse at day 40 (Bergner, 2004).

The hot preparation of boneset also has an expectorant action (Sinadinos, 2008), and it may be helpful as an upper respiratory decongestant (Hoffman, 1998). Boneset is recognized as an anti-inflammatory mast cell stabilizer that may be helpful for allergic airway disorders, perhaps due to its sesquiterpene lactone content (Stansbury, 2018b). Herbalist Matthew Wood (1997) writes that boneset is supportive when someone who is experiencing lung congestion has “no cough, no attempt to bring up mucus, and when given, it renews the cough reflexes and starts bringing this old material up” (p. 264).

In general, boneset likely supports the immune response by increasing host resistance, benefiting individuals experiencing a variety of viral infections, including infections that cause intermittent fevers—which boneset was made famous for in the late 18th to early 20th centuries (Bergner, 2005). It may have been used to support immunity during historical influenza epidemics, and also may currently have applications for post-influenza syndrome (Bergner, 2005). For these purposes, boneset pairs well with echinacea (Echinacea angustifolia) root or red root (Ceanothus velutinus) (Bergner, 2005). Polysaccharides from Eupatorium perfoliatum have been shown to increase phagocytosis, a mechanism wherein specialized immune cells in the body will engulf and deactivate an infectious pathogen such as a virus or bacteria (Bergner, 2005).

Buhner (2005) asserts that boneset has immune-enhancing activity that may benefit those experiencing Babesia and Bartonella coinfections of chronic Lyme disease. David Winston (2006) has also included boneset in a protocol to address the Borrelia spirochete, which is known to cause Lyme disease. Boneset may protect bone marrow macrophages and stimulate their production, and benefit ratios of lymphocytes, thus reducing “incidence and severity of periodic fevers, pains, and disease symptoms” (Buhner, 2005, p. 186). In clinical practice, “it is one of the most potent herbs for enhancing immune function especially in periodic diseases like bartonellosis. It will reliably counter bacterial or viral immune-suppression in diseases that present as periodics” (Buhner, 2005, p. 187). For this, Buhner (2005) suggests “one cup of the mildly hot tea, 2-4x daily for as long as necessary: usually two to six weeks” (p. 187).

Buhner (2005) further notes that boneset tincture may not have the same immunostimulating effects as the tea. He suggests adding the tincture to hot water for situations calling for hot preparations. For acute upper respiratory infections, he suggests 10 drops of tincture in hot water every half hour up to 6x/day; in post-acute conditions or relapse with continued chronic fatigue, 10 drops of tincture in hot water 4x/day (Buhner, 2005).

Boneset may also soothe those experiencing viral exanthem, which are skin eruptions associated especially with childhood viral or bacterial infectious illnesses, such as measles, chicken pox, and fifth disease in adults or children (Stansbury, 2018a). In these cases, Stansbury combines boneset with astragalus (Astragalus mongholicus, formerly known as A. membranaceus) root, catnip (Nepeta cataria) leaf, elder (Sambucus nigra) flower, peppermint (Mentha x piperita) leaf, chamomile (Matricaria chamomilla) flower, and licorice (Glycyrrhiza glabra) root (Stansbury, 2018a).

Traditionally, boneset has also been applied topically to soothe herpes viral infections (Bergner, 2005). Modern practitioners have reported success from internal use in individuals with herpes simplex viruses I and II, specifically when taken during the prodromal stage to prevent full  expression of the virus (Daucus & Bergner, 1996).

A case study from Kathy Plache (2003), a founding member of the North American Institute of Medical Herbalism, described the use of boneset along with osha (Ligusticum porteri) root, echinacea (Echinacea angustifolia) root, red root (Ceanothus virginicus), and ginger (Zingiber officinale) rhizome taken in conjunction with nutrient supplementation and other personalized suggestions. This herbal plan was given to a 40-year-old woman with Epstein-Barr virus who suffered chronically swollen lymph nodes in her neck. The client experienced reduction in the size of the nodes within a week, and the issue fully resolved within 3 weeks. In addition, she experienced improvement in her energy levels and a diminishment of other acute symptoms (Plache, 2003).

Adult Dose

Infusion: 4-8 fl oz (1 oz dried aerial parts in 1 quart water), 3-4x/day (Sinadinos, 2008). For acute use, 1-2 tsp dried aerial parts in 8 fl oz water, consumed every half hour (Hoffmann, 1998).

Tincture: 20-60 drops fresh aerial parts (1:3, 95%) or dried aerial parts (1:5, 60%) up to 4x/day (Sinadinos, 2008); 2-4 mL (1:5, 45%) 3x/day (Hoffmann, 2003).

Glycerite: 3-5 mL dried aerial parts (1:6, 60% glycerin, 40% water) 3-4x/day (Easley & Horne, 2016).

Safety

Avoid consumption of boneset during pregnancy (Sinadinos, 2008). Very high doses of boneset can lead to nausea, vomiting, or diarrhea (Sinadinos, 2008), especially when the preparation is consumed as a hot tea or tincture in hot water. Cooler preparations may be less likely to induce nausea (Buhner, 2005).

Boneset contains potentially toxic pyrrolizidine alkaloids, and more rigorous testing is required to evaluate the severity of potential toxicity (Colgate et al., 2018). There are no known reports of hepatotoxicity (Yance, 2020).

Naturopathic physician and herbalist Jillian Stansbury (2018b) suggests it is best to reserve boneset for acute situations and discontinue consumption after a week or two.
