Native To

The cultivated rose is supposed by some to have originated in ancient Persia (Grieve, 1931/1971). Today, the Rosa genus is considered native to the temperate and subtropical regions of the Northern Hemisphere (Royal Botanic Gardens Kew [RBKG], n.d.).

Geographic Distribution

In addition to their native range of much of the Northern Hemisphere, Rosa species can be found in parts of Australia, Africa, and South America (RBGK, n.d.).

Botanical Description

Rose is a deciduous climbing or shrub-like perennial. Its stems are usually thorny to protect against predators, and the thorns are also useful in determining a species as they vary in color, size, and shape (Pemberton, 1920). A rose will send up fresh shoots called surculi for next year’s growth, and in summer varieties, these shoots often appear after the flowering period has come to an end (Pemberton, 1920).

Flowers have five sepals and at least one five-petal layer. These sepals serve as an exterior protection for the reproductive organs until they are mature, and the five petals work as an interior covering; at maturation, there is an unfurling to allow fertilization (Pemberton, 1920). Flowers come in a range of colors, although most herbalists today use red and pink varieties.

The fruit of the rose, often referred to as the rose hip, is also useful for identification as the hips also vary in color, shape, and size. They tend to be of orange or red hues but may also appear as a deep purple or even black. Like apples, the hips are false fruits; inside its cup-shaped vessel are the true fruits, which are achenes (often mistaken for seeds), which in turn contain the seeds (Barker, 2001).

Rose’s leaves are alternate and oddly numbered. As has long been noted by botanists but not always commonly known, the leaves of different species may vary so much in shape, color, and texture that one can often identify a particular rose as easily by leaf as by bloom (Pemberton, 1920). While botanical classification can present challenges due to the number of hybrid varieties and differences in opinion among botanists, roses may be classified into roughly 16 categories—from which all modern hybridizations are derived—according to characteristics such as foliage, fruit, flowers, etc. (Pemberton, 1920).

Key Constituents

Flowers: Volatile oils, tannins, phenolic compounds (quercetin, kaempferol, flavonoids), beta-sitosterol, and polysaccharides (Elizabeth, n.d.).

Fruits (rose hips): Carotenoids (lycopene), vitamins C, E, K, B3 (Elizabeth, n.d.), B9, and A, minerals (potassium, calcium, manganese, and magnesium) (Nutrition Data, n.d.a), phenolic compounds (flavonoids), tannins, and polysaccharides (pectin) (Mármol et al., 2017).

Sustainability

Conventional roses are heavily sprayed with chemicals. Choose organically grown roses only.

Harvesting Guidelines

Harvest rose petals on a dry morning when they are at their most fragrant. This should be done immediately after blooming (the petals should not be widely open).

Rose hips can be harvested in fall when they have ripened and changed color. Some herbalists have suggested harvesting them just after they have begun to change color and yet are not quite fully colored, or just after the first frost of the year—in other words, in a state of near ripeness (Barker, 2001). 

Take care when pruning roses not to remove the surculi (new growth shoots) of summer varieties, which will provide for next year’s blossoms. Remember not to deadhead all blossoms if you are hoping to harvest rose hips.

Uses

A longtime symbol of love and beauty, the Rosa genus boasts over 100 species and more than 18,000 cultivars (Boskabady et al., 2011) growing throughout Asia, Europe, North America, and northern Africa. Although most commonly known today for its prominent role in aromatherapy, both rose blossoms and hips have been used in herbal traditions around the world for many thousands of years. 

Various species have historically been favored in herbal practice and in scientific studies, as well as in the perfume and essential oil industries, chief among them Rosa x damascena (Damask or Persian rose); R. canina, or dog rose; and the original wild roses R. gallica (apothecary or French rose, also considered the European prototype from which many varieties were descended) or R. centifolia (cabbage rose) (Tobyn et al., 2016). Sometimes as many as 10-12 different species of roses were recognized for herbal purposes in antiquity and the Renaissance periods (Parkinson, 1640; Pliny, 77/1906). The cultivation of roses happened early in the East—around 2200 BCE in Persia and 2700 BCE in China (Tobyn et al., 2016)—while hybridizations appear to have taken off in Western Europe in the 18th century (Barker, 2001).

The oldest extant records show that rose not only predates human beings (D’Andrea, 1982; Tobyn et al., 2016) but was very early on in human history used for its scent and has long been connected to love and passion. Their blossoms were employed in ancient Greece for their perfume, and the flower itself was associated with passion in poetry and particularly with Aphrodite/Venus, the goddesses of love, although roses eventually became a symbol of excess in the Roman Empire (D’Andrea, 1982). Many varieties were used in ancient days for feasts, wreathing, worship, and for honoring the dead (D’Andrea, 1982), sometimes in a type of rose festival known as the Rosalia (Phillips, 2012).

Rose also found its way into the works of the earliest botanical writers such as Theophrastus and played a role in the healing arts in antiquity, often as an astringent and vulnerary, but also in addressing a variety of different body systems and issues; some of these uses have continued to the present day. Rose was used in unguents in ancient Greece and in plasters and eye unguents in the Roman Empire (Pliny, 77/1906). By the 1st century, preparations were already varied: oils, poultices, lotions, ointments, rinses, suppositories, and more (Dioscorides, 70/2000). Dioscorides (70/2000) recorded recipes for rose including oil preparations and remarked upon its astringency and cooling nature; many of his indications demonstrate a vulnerary use, which continued for centuries.

As mentioned above, rose has been widely valued for its aromatic properties since antiquity. While rose water is said to have been first prepared in the 10th century by the Persian physician Ibn Sina (Barker, 2001), herbalist Maud Grieve (1931/1971) reports that the idea to distill the essence of rose (i.e., essential oil) originated at the wedding of Persian Princess Nour-Djihan to the Emperor Djihanguyr in 1582. The newlyweds were rowing in a rose water-filled canal created especially for the wedding feast, when the couple observed the heat from the sun had separated out the volatile oil of rose on the water’s surface. Soon after, distilleries in Persia began producing and exporting the exquisite “otto of roses” as a perfume (Grieve, 1931/1971).

In the 1600s, herbalists and botanists such as Culpeper (1653/1850) and Parkinson (1640) wrote about the energetic differences between varying colors of rose flowers (although the Greek physician Galen (129-210 CE) had apparently done so long before them): white and red roses were considered cooling and drying, with white being the stronger in both regards, despite its uncommon use as an internal preparation (Culpeper, 1653/1850). 

The medieval and Renaissance periods largely saw a continuation of ancient uses for roses, with broad applicability to different body systems and ailments. Perhaps less known today is that roses have been employed since antiquity and across cultures for eye complaints, both physical and perhaps even energetic (Dioscorides, 70/2000; Pliny, 77/1906; Wood, 2009). Prior to the medieval period, German Benedictine abbess and herbalist Hildegard von Bingen (1155/2001) mentions using rose leaf to clear the eyes, and rose was mixed with other preparations for the eyes in the Renaissance period (Parkinson, 1640). Rose is also indicated for eye complaints such as farsightedness in the Persian tradition (Tobyn et al., 2016). 

In many Indigenous cultures, roses have come to be symbolic of vitality, resilience, survival, and even staving off ghosts (Salmón, 2020). In North America, the Ojibwe people, also known as the Chippewa tribe, have historically used rose root for cataracts and inflammation (Erichsen-Brown, 1989). The Okanagan-Colville Indigenous people have traditionally used a decoction of rose species, including prickly rose (Rosa acicularis) or woods’ rose (Rosa woodsii), leaves and branches for bathing after sweat lodge ceremonies; they also use a poultice of the leaf for bee stings (Turner et al., 1980). Prickly rose is also used by the Thompson Indigenous people for diarrhea, vomiting, syphilis, and athlete’s foot (Turner et al., 1990). Mothers in labor chew rosehips to encourage the delivery, and would consume a decoction of astringent rose roots after childbirth (Turner et al., 1990). Those in the Bella Coola tribe use an infusion of nootka rose (Rosa nutkana) roots and shoots as an eyewash (Turner, 1973). Among the Cherokee Indigenous people, an infusion of the bark and root of swamp rose (Rosa palustris) is considered effective against worms, while a decoction of the roots was used for dysentery (Hamel & Chiltoskey, 1975). Among the Cherokee people, Rosa palustris, also termed Cherokee rose, is gargled with wild cherry (Prunus serotina) bark to fight inflammation in the oral cavity, and drunk to quell fevers in children (Garrett, 2003). 

Members of the Lakota group use prairie rose (Rosa arkansana) hips, petals, and roots to make a tea for refreshment, and use the hips as a nutritive food or as a tea for kidney and bladder support (Salmón, 2020). A rose root or bark infusion is used for joint inflammation, diarrhea, and gastrointestinal upset, while a bark decoction is used for pain. The Lakota people also employ the rose hip or root infusion as an eyewash (Salmón, 2020). 

It seems that rose’s use for eye complaints lapsed somewhere between the 17th century and contemporary herbalism. Herbalist Matthew Wood (2008) points out that the old literature on this indication is difficult to interpret, and few modern studies have followed up on these traditions. However, at least one clinical trial has been undertaken concerning an herbal eye drop formula. Utilizing herbs from the Ayurvedic tradition, the formula includes R. damascena among its eight herbal ingredients and was ultimately deemed useful for inflammatory, infective, and degenerative ophthalmic disorders (Biswas et al., 2001). 

Interestingly, rose leaves were once commonly used in herbalism, although their use has largely been discontinued in modern herbal practice. Older texts remind us that we do not always turn to the same plant parts today and that there may be ample room for exploration and rediscovery. Fresh, dried, and powdered leaves were once widely used, not just by Europeans but also in Arabian practices (Parkinson, 1640; von Bingen, 1155/2001). One of the surviving English records for wild rose indicates its crushed leaves were used for cuts in Essex (Allen & Hatfield, 2004). Bark and thorns were also once used (Erichsen-Brown, 1989; Wood, 2008).

In Western Europe, preparations based on the aforementioned most commonly used species of roses remained widespread through the 18th century, not only in herbalism but also within pharmaceutical, cosmetic, and other industries (Holmes, 2007). It is also in the 18th century that some Western texts begin to define rose as primarily useful for the heart and nervous system (Tobyn et al., 2016). By the 19th century, however, Western hybridizations between Eastern tea roses and Western cabbage roses created modern cultivars and resulted in thousands of new varieties for the garden which, along with cultural changes, caused the common use of rose to dissipate (Barker, 2001). While rose remained ever popular in Eastern traditions, it does not seem to have been much used in the United States during this period, and indeed by the 20th century, aside from Grieve (1931/1971), it does not appear commonly in herbal literature (Tobyn et al., 2016). 

In Chinese medicine, rose flowers affect the Liver and Spleen channels (Yin Yang House, n.d.). This seems in some ways parallel to specific Western Renaissance indications for stomach and liver conditions (Culpeper, 1653/1850; Parkinson, 1640). However, in Chinese medicine rose is considered bitter and warming rather than cooling; the flower is used to harmonize blood and move qi, break up stagnation, ease breast tenderness, menstrual pain, and belching, and improve the appetite (Yin Yang House, n.d.). With respect to rose’s long-standing relationship with the liver, it appears to act as a cholagogue and therefore is applicable to liver issues with qi stasis and heat (Holmes, 2007; Parkinson, 1640). Some scientific exploration on the connection between roses and liver health have only just begun in the realm of in vivo studies. At least one in vitro study, meanwhile, has explored the hepatoprotective and antioxidant effects of R. damascena and concluded that the fresh juice of the flower shows possible antioxidant potential that might protect against certain types of hepatotoxicity, perhaps via free radical scavenging (Achuthan et al., 2008).

Ayurvedic practitioners David Frawley and Vasant Lad (2001) consider rose flowers to have sweet, bitter, pungent, astringent, and cooling qualities, as well as a balancing effect on all three doshas, but potentially increasing kapha if taken in excess. As in Chinese medicine, Ayurvedic use of rose is indicated for the ovo-uterine system and the circulatory system, and as in Western herbalism, for the nervous system (Frawley & Lad, 2001).

Throughout the centuries there have been different perspectives on whether rose’s energetic profile is essentially cooling or warming. It is most often noticed today that Chinese traditions classify rose as warming, while rose is generally seen as cooling in Western perspectives. This is not always so distinct a cultural or geographical difference; there is some indication that Galen considered rose to have warming qualities, and the medieval Salernitan herbal classified rose as hot (as cited in Tobyn et al., 2016). In addition, looking east to Arabic traditions, Ibn Sina viewed wild rose as hot (as cited in Tobyn et al., 2016).

Herbalist Kiva Rose Hardin (n.d.) interprets the warming aspect discussed in Chinese medicine as rose’s ability to promote movement of qi, and the cooling aspects as its anti-inflammatory action. Hardin (2011) also clarifies that in terms of its drying nature, rose has a contracting quality that helps keep moisture in the tissues, rather than causing the tissues to lose moisture. This intuitive information is reflected in the long history of rose in topical moisturizing creams and cosmetics.

Matthew Wood (2008) also highlights a difference between Western and Eastern traditions regarding the energetics of rosehips, which are viewed as drying and cooling in modern Western traditions. The flavonoid-rich hips are seen as checking discharges caused by heat as well as nourishing the system in a tonic fashion: major herbal actions of rosehips accordingly include astringent, refrigerant, and antioxidant (Wood, 2008). He notes, however, that Chinese herbalism finds varying energetics among species as well as plant parts; the hips of R. laevigata are neutral, for example, while the petals of R. rugosa are viewed as warming. Meanwhile, other Eastern traditions describe all parts of the rose as warming, so there is a great deal of variation regarding rose energetics (Wood, 2008). 

Much of the research on different rose species is still in the preclinical phase, with R. damascena appearing to receive the most interest among investigators. R. damascena has undergone preclinical studies to evaluate its action on everything from memory problems to gastrointestinal disorders. Its reported pharmacological effects include “anti-HIV, antibacterial, antioxidant, antitussive, hypnotic, antidiabetic, and relaxant effect on tracheal chains” (Boskabady et al., 2011). Some clinical attention has followed, but thus far only in the area of rose essential oil and in terms of emotional and physical pain: easing pain and labor anxiety (Hamdamian et al., 2018), premenstrual syndrome (Heydari et al., 2018), and migraine headaches (Niazi et al., 2017).

Rose has also been used since ancient times as a vulnerary that promotes tissue repair. To investigate rose’s purported anti-inflammatory and analgesic properties, Houseinpour et al. (2011) held a randomized double-blind placebo-controlled study that evaluated the efficacy of R. damascena to treat recurrent mouth lesions. The researchers concluded that the mouthwash made from an aqueous extract of rose was found to be more effective than placebo (Hoseinpour et al., 2011). Kalim et al. (2010) evaluated the potential antioxidant effects of herbs commonly used in the Unani system of traditional medicine and concluded that R. damascena “showed significant potential for preventing oxidative DNA damage and radical scavenging activity” (Kalim et al., 2010). The powdered hips of R. canina used internally in a randomized controlled trial, meanwhile, were considered successful in sustaining the youthfulness of the skin (based on markers such as wrinkles, elasticity, etc.) (Phetcharat et al., 2015).

Rose is also valued today for its ability to act as a reproductive restorative, particularly in Chinese medicine, where it is used for impotence, infertility, or a lack of libido arising from hormonal imbalances, and to address menstrual cycles (delayed periods, dysmenorrhea, painful periods, and pelvic blood congestion) (Holmes, 2007). This indication for regulating the menstrual cycle may be traced back at least as far as the Renaissance period and probably much further; both Parkinson (1640) and Culpeper (1653/1850) indicated dried and powdered roses infused in water or wine to help “stay women’s courses” (p. 152). 

A clinical trial by Bani et al. (2014) evaluated the effect of R. damascena extract in those with primary dysmenorrhea (menstrual cramps not related to a reproductive disorder). A group of 92 participants were separated into two groups. One group received a conventional prescription of mefenamic acid, a non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug, and the other group received 200 mg of R. damascena extract every 6 hours. At the end of the study, the authors concluded that the rose extract had similar effects as mefenamic acid in reducing pain without the side effects of the conventional drug (Bani et al., 2014).

Rose hips have a fairly similar profile to that of the blossoms in terms of use; they have been an important part of rose’s use in various herbal traditions and are particularly valued for boosting the immune system. The hips of R. canina garnered appreciation during World War II, when citizens were encouraged to collect them for their vitamin content (Allen & Hatfield, 2004). Historical folk traditions in England commonly made use of them in syrup for sore throats and colds, and in Donegal they were used for coughs (Allen & Hatfield, 2004). Indeed, the hips are high in vitamin C, for which reason they are popularly used today. Nearly 200% of the recommended daily value is found in just 1 ounce of wild rose hips; they are also a good source of vitamin A, manganese, calcium, and magnesium (Nutrition Data, n.d.). Rose hips may be distinguished further from the petals by their taste, which while also somewhat sweet, includes sourness, astringency, and a sense of coolness (Wood, 2008). 

It has been surmised that the health benefits of rose hips are primarily due to their high vitamin C content, but a 2008 study found that the vitamin C content in rose hips contributed very little to free radical scavenging and “that other, yet unidentified, lipophilic constituents might play a more important role for the observed in vitro inhibitory activity on arachidonic acid metabolism” (Wenzing et al., 2008). A 2012 clinical study of the effects of a drink made from powdered rose hips on the risk markers of diabetes and cardiovascular disease found that after 6 weeks, cardiovascular risk was significantly reduced through the lowering of systolic blood pressure and cholesterol levels (Andersson et al., 2012).

Rose hips are also increasingly of interest in research on joint conditions, and indeed they have been used in folk tradition for arthritic inflammation (Wood, 2008). A meta-analysis of the available studies using rose hip powder from R. canina to reduce osteoarthritic pain concluded that although clinical data was scanty, rosehip powder does have analgesic effects; the authors recommended large-scale trials to independently replicate these results and to further determine safety and efficacy (Christensen et al., 2008). Some clinical studies have followed suit on such topics; for instance, one randomized controlled trial concluded that rose hips could reduce physical symptom scores for patients with rheumatoid arthritis (Willich et al., 2010). More recently, a placebo-controlled, double-blind study employed rose hips from R. canina in an herbal compound formula for gonarthritis and noted its success over placebos (Moré et al., 2017).

Preliminary research on rose has indicated potential antimutagenic activity, specifically when using R. centifolia petal (Kumar et al., 2013) and anticancer effects, specifically pertaining to Rosa damascena essential oil (Rezaie-Tavirani et al., 2013). Salmón (2020) notes that the phenolics, tannins, and flavonoids within R. arkansana, R. stellata, and R. californica, among other rose species, display anticarcinogenic activity.

Meditating on the rose, we can clearly see its healthy boundaries: a soft but strong heart that is open yet protected. Rose’s connection with grief may be traced back to ancient days wherein they were symbolically used in funerals and honoring the dead (D’Andrea, 1982). Today roses are often used to soothe and open the energetic heart chakra and for a lack of self-love, vulnerability, broken hearts, and grief (Hardin, n.d.), as well as for emotional shock and depression (Holmes, 2007). 

Rose petals can be incorporated into a variety of preparations, such as creams, lotions, oils, ointments, teas, elixirs, vinegars, glycerites, conserves, and tinctures, while rose hips lend themselves well to powders, teas, jellies, jams, and syrups. Note that the length of time spent preparing an infusion may matter in terms of chemical extraction: herbalist Peter Holmes (2007) has written that when prepared as a long infusion (steeped for up to 8 hours), rose eye washes and mouthwashes/gargles are markedly antiseptic, soothing, and anti-inflammatory. A sprinkling of rose petals makes a great addition to an uplifting tea—not only for the energetic effects, but also to add color and beauty to the tea blend.

Adult Dose

Infusion: 1-10 g dried petal/day (Pole, 2012); 5-45 g dried hip/day (de la Forêt, n.d.).

Tincture: 5-15 mL fresh petal (1:2, 50%) per day (Pole, 2012); 5 drops-2 mL dried petal (1:5, 40%) 3x/day (Easley & Horne, 2016).

Safety

Rose is generally deemed very safe. Due to their tannin content, rose petals may reduce the intestinal absorption of iron and alkaloids; caution is advised in cases of iron deficiency and for those taking pharmaceuticals containing alkaloids (Brinker, 2010). 

There is some confusion regarding the toxicity of rose hips. Some herbalists assert they are harmless while others, such as Matthew Wood (2008), warn that the seed within the hip contains a toxic glucoside that can result in vertigo, headaches, and torpor, and thus should not be used in herbal preparations. Many plants in the Rosaceae family do contain cyanide in their seeds. You can find the hips sold in herbal commerce with the interior parts removed. There are also minute hairs inside the hips that may irritate the mouth and digestive system (Lyle, 2006); indeed, rose hip powder can cause itching (Hoeger et al., 2020), hence the French call the hips of R. canina gratte-culs, which translates as “scratch-butt.” Turner et al. (1990) notes that among the Thompson Indigenous people, rose hips were thought to be poisonous and plague those who consumed them with an itchy bottom. In the same text, however, it is also noted that rose hip infusion was taken for sore, irritated throats and given to babies for coughs (Turner et al., 1990). Interestingly, rose hip is listed in The Nursing Mother’s Herbal with a safety rating of ‘A’–indicating that it is generally considered safe when used appropriately (Humphrey, 2003).
