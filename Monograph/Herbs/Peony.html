Peony

Paeonia lactiflora

Native To

Siberia, Tibet, Mongolia, and Northern China (Missouri Botanical Garden, n.d.).

Geographic Distribution

White peony’s range in the wild includes China, Korea, Eastern Mongolia, Far East Russia and Southeastern Siberia (American Peony Society, 2019). It is the progenitor of over 7,000 peony cultivars and is now cultivated throughout Asia, the Middle East and North America (American Peony Society, 2019).

Botanical Description

Paeonia lactiflora is a shrub-like herbaceous perennial that is erect and clump-forming; it grows to a height of 2.5-3 feet tall on stems that are often tinged with red (Missouri Botanical Garden, n.d.). The 8-12” long leaves are dark green and compound with nine elliptical leaflets with irregular margins (Missouri Botanical Garden, n.d.). The foliage turns a rusty red in late summer, and leaves and stems die back to the ground after frost. Peony’s showy, bowl-shaped flowers bloom mid to late spring and come in a range of colors from white and light pink to dark crimson (Missouri Botanical Garden, n.d.). The flower heads in bloom range from 4-8” across. The plants have thick, tuberous roots. Peony prefers fertile well-drained soil and full sun and is hardy to zone 3 (Boeckmann, 2019).

Key Constituents

Monoterpenoid glycosides (paeoniflorin, albiflorin), triterpenoids, acetophenones (paeonol, etc.), tannins (gallotannins), volatile oils, phytosterols (5-sterols, sitosterol, etc), carbohydrates (starch, sucrose, water-soluble polysaccharides: paeonans) (Skenderi, 2003; Tierra, 2003).

Sustainability

None known.

Harvesting Guidelines

The roots of the peony are dug in the spring or fall when the plant is 3-5 years old; Traditional Chinese Medicine favors fall-harvested roots. Bai shao (white peony) and Chi shao (red peony) are from the same plants but are processed differently after harvest. For Bai shao (white peony), the roots are harvested, washed clean, stripped of the rhizome and fibrous rootlets. The outer root bark is scraped off, the peeled root is boiled for a short time, and is then dried in the sun (Dharmananda, 2002). For Chi shao (red peony), the roots are cleaned and the rhizome and fibrous rootlets are removed and then the root is simply dried in the sun (Dharmananda, 2002). The peony plant can be divided up when it is harvested, and the pieces of root with eyes can be replanted to eventually grow into new plants.

Uses

The peony is a long-lived, hardy perennial with a long, rich history of use in herbal traditions. Paeonia is also a prized plant to many a gardener, with its luscious and fragrant flowers blooming in a myriad of whites and pinks year after year. The genus Paeonia is derived from the name of the most ancient Greek god of healing, Paeon. He was known as the physician of the gods, and was said to have been the first to discover the peony plant and use it for healing purposes. The peony is mentioned in the most ancient Chinese texts, where it was originally used as a flavoring for food (Dharmananda, 2002). Dioscorides, the Greek physician and pharmacologist (born circa 40 CE) who wrote the greatly influential De Materia Medica, described two forms of peony he used in medicine. From his descriptions these have been identified as Paeonia mascula and Paeonia officinalis. These two species of peony are native to Europe but are considered close relatives of Paeonia lactiflora, which is native to Asia (Tobyn et al., 2016).

Distinguishing which Paeonia species is being referred to can get quite confusing because various species have been used historically and because Paeonia lactiflora is used in both its red and white forms in Chinese medicine. Paeonia officinalis and P. mascula have both been used in Western herbal traditions, and we will explore those uses further in the following paragraphs. Paeonia suffruticosa is another important Paeonia species used in Chinese medicine and Kanpo, traditional Japanese medicine. This species is known as tree peony and is also called moutan peony (Tobyn et al., 2016).

The most commonly used peony in Chinese medicine and Western herbalism today is derived from Paeonia lactiflora, which will continue to be the main focus of this monograph. Here’s where the name gets even more confusing, though! Chinese medicine differentiates between white and red peony based on different post-harvest processing methods and gives them two different names: Bai shao and Chi shao. The white and red peony are considered to have slightly different qualities and are used for different purposes. But white and red peony are the same species (Paeonia lactiflora), and the difference between the two depends on the preparation of the root. As mentioned previously, white peony root has been peeled and boiled, while red peony root has not (Dharmananda, 2002).

Paeonia officinalis and P. mascula were both recorded as being widely used in the historical Western herbal tradition, and interestingly the whole plant was used, not just the roots. Dioscorides recommended decocting the root of peony in wine for belly aches, kidney disease, and bladder issues. The seed and occasionally the flower petals were commonly used along with the root (Tobyn et al., 2016).

Historically, two of the conditions most commonly said to be helped by peony were nightmares and convulsions. Pliny, who was born 23 CE in Como Italy and educated in Rome, was the celebrated author of the influential book Natural History, an extensive encyclopedia of the natural world. Dioscorides and Pliny both referred to using peony seeds for people who were plagued with terrible nightmares. Culpeper, an influential physician in London during the mid-1600s whose writings built upon the basis of Galenic medicine, advised the use of peony for melancholic people who are prone to nightmares (Tobyn et al., 2016). Hildegard von Bingen, a German mystic and physician in the 11th century, used peony to purge the head and chest of phlegm and give a person good-smelling breath. She wrote:

If a person goes out of his or her mind as if they know nothing and are lying deranged in ecstasy, dip peony seed in honey and place it under the tongue. The powers of the peony will rise up in the person’s brain and excite it so that the person will return to his or her right mind and the understanding will return (Hozeski, 2001, p. 120).

The root steeped in wine and taken internally was also recommended for epilepsy, as was the use of peony root or seed tied around the neck as an amulet (Tobyn et al., 2016). Galen suggested that some quality in the root flowed through inspiration to the affected places in a person (Tobyn et al., 2016). Hildegard von Bingen described a preparation of peony seed for epilepsy that included dipping the seed in the blood of a swallow and immediately rolling it in flour. She wrote, “When a person falls with epilepsy, put this in the mouth while the person is still lying there. Do this every time they fall with the disease, and they will finally be cured” (Hozeski, 2001, p. 121).

John Hall was the son-in-law of William Shakespeare and a practicing physician in the early 17th century. Some of his case records survived and he described practices based on the Galenic system of medicine including using extracts of peony to treat patients with epilepsy (Betts & Betts, 1998). The use of peony in Europe seems to have dropped off in the 19th century, so there has been almost no analysis of the constituents of either Paeonia mascula or Paeonia officinalis (Tobyn et al., 2016).

While there have not been studies on the European species of peony to determine the validity of their historic use as anticonvulsants, there is ongoing interest in the possible use of white peony as an adjunct herbal support in addressing epilepsy (Tobyn et al., 2016). Modern herbalist Leslie Tierra writes that she has successfully used a combination of white peony and black cohosh (Actaea racemosa) to address epilepsy (Tierra, 2003).

The Shennong Bencao Jing, (circa 100 CE), a book on Chinese agriculture and herbalism that is thought to be a compilation of oral traditions, describes peony as being bitter and “treating evil qi.” Evil qi is an ancient concept in Chinese medicine. This term refers to an energetic imbalance within the body that can happen through exposure to conditions in the outside environment (Dharmananda, 2010). The text also lists peony as being used for abdominal pain, eliminating blood stasis, breaking hard gatherings, promoting urination, and boosting the qi (Shouzhong, 1998). While it is not known exactly when preparations of white and red peony began to be distinguished from one another, we know that this was already in practice by the 16th century when the text on traditional Chinese medicine and ophthalmology, the Yinhai Jingwei, was produced. This text describes the differing qualities of the two peonies as follows:

Bai shao (white peony): Bitter, salty, cold. It enters the liver conduit. It can supplement the spleen and diminish the liver qi. It can nourish the liver blood and drain liver fire. When the liver is depleted, and the fire weak, it cannot be used.

Chi shao (red peony): Bitter, acrid, cold. It enters the liver conduit. It can disperse the blood, and it can make the blood flow. It removes redness of the skin, and stops pain (as cited in Dharmananda, 2002, para. 6).

This early description of the difference between white and red peony is consistent with the way in which the uses of the peony roots are distinguished today. White peony is generally considered to be cool and more building and nourishing, while red peony is slightly colder than white and is more moving and dispersive (Tierra, 2003). Red peony is used to invigorate the blood and to bring movement where there is a pattern of stagnation, and is also considered to be helpful in clearing liver heat and supporting abdominal pain, traumatic injury, and resolving hard masses (Tierra, 2003).

While there is a very long history of distinguishing between the qualities and uses of red and white peony, there still remains some confusion and even contradictory information. One report looking at herbs on the Chinese market at the end of the 19th century indicated that only peony root with the bark on (red peony) was being sold (Dharmananda, 2002). This would indicate that distinguishing between the different root preparations has had varying levels of importance throughout history. Subhuti Dharmananda (2002), the director of the Institute for Traditional Medicine, concludes that the differences between red and white peony may not be very significant after all.

According to Ayurvedic medicine, white peony root is bitter and sour in flavor and cool in quality, and it reduces vata and pitta dosha while enhancing vitality and nourishing the blood (Caldecott, 2012). Peony is recommended for reducing spasms and pain, and is considered especially useful for premenstrual syndrome (PMS), menopause, coughs, asthma, and liver issues (Caldecott, 2012). Peony is combined with other building and nourishing herbs to support convalescence and infertility.

White peony is often combined with other herbs in Chinese herbal formulas. Peony and licorice (Glycyrrhiza glabra) are combined to make a popular formula, Shaoyao Gancao Tang, which is used for a variety of muscle spasms and muscular pain, and is also a popular base formula that other herbs are added to for more specific complaints. Many of the formulas containing peony are used for some form of spasm, as well as stomach pain and intestinal disorders. Peony also appears in a number of formulas indicated for deficient blood and gynecological and menstrual issues, as well as pregnancy formulas to avoid miscarriage (Dharmananda, 2002). Other indications in Chinese medicine for formulas containing peony include skin eruptions, cold hands and feet due to stagnation, infections, and pain and heat with constipation (Dharmananda, 2002).

White peony is considered to be building to the blood and yin and to be helpful in regulating the menstrual cycle. The root is currently used by Chinese and Western herbalists as a tonic to support a variety of gynecological issues and the balance of related hormones. These include menstrual dysregulation, vaginal discharge, dysmenorrhea, amenorrhea, abnormal uterine bleeding, anemia, and polycystic ovarian syndrome (PCOS) (Tierra, 2003).

In Traditional Chinese Medicine, white peony root is regarded as especially useful in formulas for menstrual disorders because of its building and moving qualities (Tierra, 2003). Many issues with menstruation, including PMS, dysmenorrhea (pain), and irregular cycles, are considered to arise from stagnant qi. Stagnant qi is connected to depressed Liver qi and stagnant and deficient blood, and all of these interrelated issues of stagnation and deficiency contribute to menstrual and hormonal dysregulation (Tierra, 2003). These patterns of stagnation that present as menstrual disorders are addressed by moving the blood and qi in the body, while also nourishing and building the blood with the use of white peony (Tierra, 2003).

Western herbalists rely on the antispasmodic, anti-inflammatory, analgesic, and hormone-modulating actions of white peony to support dysmenorrhea and other menstrual disorders (Tierra, 2003). Herbalist Mathew Wood connects the Western concept of excess estrogen with the Eastern concept of excess heat, and uses peony to address this pattern of excess that shows up in a variety of gynecological conditions including endometriosis, irregular periods, PCOS, and menopausal symptoms (Wood, 2008). White peony is specifically considered by modern herbalists to be a uterine smooth muscle relaxant (Romm, 2018).

Interestingly, the European species of peony (Paeonia officinalis and P. mascula) were also historically used for many gynecological issues. Dioscorides and Culpeper both recommended the root be taken when a woman was not sufficiently “cleansed after childbirth” (Tobyn et al., 2016, p. 235). In the Salernitan Herbal (a work originating from a center of medical teaching and practice in medieval Europe in the 11-13th centuries), the advice for cleansing the womb is to use fumigation which suggests that some part of the peony was burned and the smoke was used. In the same Salernitan text it is also recommended that during childbirth 15 black peony seeds be given for “deliverance of the bed of the child in her womb” (Tobyn et al., 2016, p. 235). This quote along with the other references to cleansing of the womb suggest that peony was used to aid in the expulsion of the afterbirth. Dioscorides and Galen also suggested a decoction of the root powder the size of an almond be taken to induce menstruation, while powdered root and seeds could also be taken when menstruation was excessive (Tobyn et al., 2016).

Polycystic ovary syndrome is a complex hormonal dysregulation characterized by irregular menstruation, hyperandrogenism, and polycystic ovaries (Arentz et al., 2017). There is some evidence that in vitro, the paeoniflorin constituent in white peony root binds to androgen receptors, suggesting that peony root is a useful herb in addressing PCOS by reducing ovarian hyperandrogenism (Ong et al., 2018). Other studies indicate that white peony root in herbal formulas may be helpful not just in balancing hormonal dysregulation in PCOS, but also in reducing other conditions associated with PCOS, including weight gain and type 2 diabetes (Arentz et al,. 2017; Liao et al., 2019). Wood (2008) recommends using peony to reduce acneiform sores on the chin, which are another symptom of PCOS.

White peony is also used by modern herbalists to ease symptoms of menopause, especially when the person is energetically hot and congested  (Tobyn et al., 2016; Easley & Horne, 2016). Because white peony is considered building and nourishing, as well as cooling, moving and relaxing, it is energetically a good fit for addressing hot flashes, night sweats, mood lability, and anger associated with menopause (Tobyn et al., 2016).

White peony is an important herb for easing stagnant Liver qi and is used for associated symptoms including headaches and stomach pain associated with stagnation and digestive issues (Tierra, 2003). While the cooling, moving, and building qualities of peony are part of what make it so commonly used to support menopausal symptoms, it is believed that peony’s ability to reduce Liver qi stagnation also makes it particularly helpful in reducing menopausal symptoms, since these can be exacerbated by stagnant Liver qi (Tobyn et al., 2016). Not only is peony supportive in reducing Liver qi stagnation, it has also been shown to reduce liver damage from certain medications used for rheumatoid arthritis. Rheumatoid arthritis is an autoimmune condition characterized by inflammation and pain in the joints, though many different parts of the body can be affected. Methotrexate and leflunomide are two medications that are commonly used to treat rheumatoid arthritis that have been found to be hepatotoxic. A recent meta-analysis confirmed that total glucosides of peony (TGP) have a hepatoprotective action when taken in conjunction with methotrexate and leflunomide as well as a possible lipid-regulating effect (Huang et al., 2019). The “total glucosides of peony” referred to in studies as TGP is a water/ethanol extract of specific constituents including several glycosides that are isolated from the root of white peony (Tu et al., 2019). Paeoniflorin is considered to be the major active component of TGP (He, 2011).

Paeoniflorin, one of the most abundant and active constituents found in white peony, is thought to significantly contribute to the anti-inflammatory and immunomodulating actions of peony (He, 2011; Tu et al., 2019). Herbalists currently employ white peony for these actions in a number of conditions including: autoimmune conditions such as rheumatoid arthritis and systemic lupus erythematosus, ulcerative colitis, headaches, cerebrovascular disease, impaired mental function, vertigo, hepatitis, psoriasis, herpes, and externally for skin inflammations such as eczema (Skenderi, 2003).

In 2011, a clinical study administered the total glucosides of peony (TGP) extracted from Paeonia lactiflora in patients with the autoimmune condition systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) with beneficial results;the average daily dose of prednisone was able to be reduced, and the recurrent cases and episodes of infection were reduced (Zhang et al., 2011). A randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled clinical trial investigated the use of TGP with patients with primary Sjögren’s syndrome, an autoimmune disorder characterized by dryness in eyes, mouth, and other mucous membranes, which often accompanies other autoimmune disorders such as rheumatoid arthritis and lupus. This clinical trial found that TGP decreased symptoms, and the authors concluded that TGPseemed to be a safe and effective treatment for Sjögren’s syndrome (Liu et al., 2019).

One recent study found that the active constituent paeoniflorin had a protective effect on endothelial cells, thus reducing endothelial injury, suggesting that white peony is an effective and supportive herb to use for conditions such as inflammatory bowel disease (Wu et al., 2019).

Adult Dose

Decoction (dry or powdered root): 3-10 g per day (Junying, 1997)

Tincture (1:5, 60% alcohol) 1 mL up to 4x per day (Easley & Horne, 2016)

Safety

Peony has no known contraindications, including during pregnancy and lactation (Gardner & McGuffin, 2013). White peony is one of the ingredients in several formulas used in Chinese medicine during pregnancy to prevent miscariage and to provide support during the postpartum period (Dharmananda, 2002). Some herbalists, however, caution against taking peony during pregnancy due to the blood-moving qualities of the herb (Tobyn et al., 2016). In the Eclectic tradition of Western herbalism, the European peony species Paeonia mascula and Paeonia officinalis have been used as an emmenagogue (Tobyn et al., 2016). According to Traditional Chinese Medicine Bai shao should be avoided when there is diarrhea from coldness and weakness, while Chi shao should be avoided in the case of deficient blood (Tierra, 2003).
