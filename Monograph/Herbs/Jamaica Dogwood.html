Jamaica Dogwood

Piscidia piscipula (syn. P. erythrina)

Print← Irish mossJapanese knotweed →

Cooling

Taste

AstringentBitterSweet

Actions

AnalgesicAnti-inflammatoryAntispasmodicHypnoticParasympathomimeticSedative

Ways to use

DecoctionFomentationPoulticeTincture
Common name
Jamaica dogwood, Jamaican dogwood, jabín, bois ivrant, crete de coq, corniro, mulungu, koraalstruik, Jamaice dogwood, coralshrub, fischrinde, corniola, Jamaica fishfuddle, fish poison tree, iguano blossom, palo de gusano
Family
Fabaceae
Parts used
Root bark or branch bark (7Song, 2014; Hamilton, 1832).

Native To

Locales surrounding the Gulf of Mexico and the Caribbean Sea

Geographic Distribution

Caribbean islands, southern Florida, southeast Texas, southern Mexico, Guatemala, Belize, Honduras, northern South America

Botanical Description

Jamaica dogwood is a large deciduous tree growing up to 15 meters high found in full sun in forests or thickets below 300 meters (7Song, 2014; Standley & Steyermark, 1946). The trunk is a grayish-olive green that can become bespotted with lichen patches as it ages (7Song, 2014). Branches are dotted with lenticels, and the young twigs are lighter in color due to the fine hairs covering their surfaces; as new twig and leaf growth emerges, they are curled up and covered in hairs as well (7Song, 2014). Upon the branches grow pinnately compound leaves consisting of 7-13 opposite leaflets (Standley & Steyermark, 1946). Their upper sides are a rich yellowish-green while their strongly contrasting undersides are almost white due to a thick layer of tiny hairs (7Song, 2014). The beautiful pea-like, red to white, spring-blooming flowers (about 2 cm long) occur on tiny pedicels in dense panicle clusters 8-20 cm long (Standley & Steyermark, 1946). These mature into 1-6 seeded legumes 2-8 cm long and 2-4 cm wide (Standley & Steyermark, 1946).

Key Constituents

Isoflavones (3′,5′-diprenylgenistein, dehydromillettone, ichthynone, isomillettone, jamaicin, lisetine, millettone, munetone, piscerythrone, piscidone, rotenone, sumatrol), and other compounds including 3-o-methyl-fukiic-acid, citric acid, malic-acid, piscidic-acid, piscidic-acid-methyl-ester, piscidia-saponin, and tannins (Duke, 2018; Hoffmann, 1998).

Sustainability

This plant is locally abundant but only grows within a limited region. Use care to harvest only from areas where the tree is common.

Harvesting Guidelines

Using standard pruning techniques to support the overall health of the tree, harvest smaller branches using a pruning saw, then peel the bark using a good knife and dry thoroughly (7Song, 2014). A historical account from 1832 indicated that harvesting the root bark during the full moon of April yielded the most potent medicine (Hamilton, 1832).

Uses

The name Piscidia derives from pisces, “a fish,” and caedo, “kill,” referring to its use as a fish poison (Harper, 2019). The epithet piscipula also refers to fish, while the former epithet erythrina derives from the Greek word for “red,” referring to the vibrant flower color (Harper 2019).

Jamaica dogwood has been an important tree to people living throughout its range. The strong, durable wood has been employed for construction of both buildings and boats (Standley & Steyermark, 1946). It is valued as a source of firewood for cooking among Maya in the Yucatan, where it was also a sacred symbol of rain and growth in rain-bringing ceremonies due to appearance of its flowers and fresh foliage at the times the rains were to begin (Anderson, 2011).

Jamaica dogwood is well known for its use as a fish poison in the Caribbean region. The bark and leaves were crushed and thrown into water in sacks or baskets to stupefy fish in Guatemala and other Caribbean locales (Balick & Arvigo, 2015; Duke, 2018; Hoffmann, 1998). One ethnographic account detailed that the root bark was harvested for this purpose only during the full moon of April, when the “juices of this plant were in the highest state of activity” (Hamilton, 1832, p. 178). The bark was then macerated with the “lees of the stillhouse” along with quicklime, then placed in a basket which was dropped into the water and agitated until “the water has become impregnated with the intoxicating preparation” (p. 179). The stupefied fish were then harvested by hand without resistance. If these fish were then returned to fresh water, it was observed they would recover. (Hamilton, 1832). After such use, the meat or fish wounded by these methods were still considered safe to eat (Hamilton, 1832; Mulheron, 1885).

Ethnobotanic study in Belize reveals the use of a decoction of the bark as a general astringent for concerns such as diarrhea, dysentery, or heavy menstruation, and topically for wounds, rashes, skin sores, and leishmaniasis and as a mouthwash for bleeding gums (Balick & Arvigo, 2015). The Yucatan Maya employed the juice from the bark to treat wounds and toothache (Balick & Arvigo, 2015). The leaves were also used, boiled in water and sugar to treat coughs and colds, or a decoction added to the bath for fevers in infants (Balick & Arvigo, 2015). It was used similarly by indigenous people in other parts of its range (Duke, 2018). In Jamaica, a root extract was used to cure mange in dogs (Balick & Arvigo, 2015).

Practitioners of the late 1800s and early 1900s, including Harvey Felter, John Uri Lloyd, Finley Ellingwood, and Samuel Potter, upon “discovering” it as a “new remedy” of the Americas, found it to be a potent and reliable alternative, albeit weaker in strength, to opiates for relieving pain of various sorts (Ellingwood, 1919; Felter & Lloyd, 1898; Felter, 1922; Mulheron, 1885; Potter 1902). Unlike opiates such as morphine or opium, Jamaica dogwood did not cause dryness of throat or mouth or cause constipation, nor did it decrease appetite or digestion (Ellingwood, 1908).

The Eclectics wrote extensively of Jamaica dogwood’s efficacy as a sedative, analgesic, anti-inflammatory, and antispasmodic (Ellingwood, 1908; Ellingwood, 1919; Felter & Lloyd, 1898; Mulheron, 1885). It was considered a potent anti-inflammatory for general rheumatism or for pain caused by the adjustment of fractured bones and dislocations, as well as for inflammatory pain of the eye and ear (Ellingwood, 1908; Felter & Lloyd, 1898). Jamaica dogwood was also known to relieve headaches of various types (Ellingwood, 1908).

Jamaica dogwood was applied both internally and topically for easing toothache (Ellingwood, 1908; Felter & Lloyd, 1898; Hamilton, 1832). Other topical applications included the treatment of burns, scalds, and hemorrhoids, likely in part due to its strong astringency (Felter & Lloyd, 1898). A number of practitioners highlighted its efficacy for trigeminal neuralgia (known at that time as tic-douloureux) and other facial and eye neuralgia, with one doctor noting that a patient with tic-douloureux was able to discontinue hypodermic morphine use upon administration of Jamaica dogwood (Ellingwood, 1908; Felter & Lloyd, 1898; Mulheron, 1885).

Jamaica dogwood was noted as more effective than codeine as an antispasmodic for violent or persistent coughs, even in the case of tuberculosis (Ellingwood, 1908). It was also found beneficial for intestinal colic, gallstones, and renal colic (Ellingwood, 1908).

Ellingwood (1919) claimed “excellent results in the treatment of pelvic pain,” (para. 7) and also recommended Jamaica dogwood for dysmenorrhea with ovarian congestion or uterine displacements. Felter & Lloyd (1898) lauded its capacity to calm false labor pains and threatened miscarriage when combined with Viburnum, and Ellingwood (1919) noted its ability to potentiate the actions of Viburnum, Cimicifuga, Pulsatilla, and Dioscorea for uterine complaints. He also referenced a case of a woman with extremely painful periods who had been habituated to morphine use; she was able to gain consistent relief from Jamaica dogwood when consuming 10 drops per hour (presumably of fluid extract) and could discontinue morphine (Ellingwood, 1919).

Jamaica dogwood was considered a potent remedy for insomnia due to nervous excitement, mental worry, anxiety, or pain (Ellingwood, 1908). It was administered to patients diagnosed with delirium or hysteria, and it was recommended in the treatment of patients deemed insane (Ellingwood, 1908; Mulheron, 1885).

Regarding the safety of Jamaica dogwood, a number of these practitioners and their contemporaries of the late 19th and early 20th century conducted experiments on themselves and on animals to evaluate its safety and efficacy (Hamilton, 1832). Some, using large doses of concentrated solid extracts, found that in great excess Jamaica dogwood could cause narcotic effects that could paralyze respiratory reflexes to the point of death (Mulheron, 1885).

Despite many reports of use in literature, Felter writes of Jamaica dogwood in 1922, “It has never been used to a great extent by Eclectic physicians; and in children and the feeble it should be employed with caution on account of its tendency to produce convulsions, even though it may satisfactorily relieve pain,” (para. 8). Ellingwood writes in The American Materia Medica (1919) the following account of the toxic effects possible from excess dosage:

In toxic doses it destroys sensation, paralyzes the respiratory centers, reduces the heart’s action; first increases, then diminishes arterial tension and decreases the pulse rate. It first contracts, then dilates the pupils in full overdoses. It causes dyspnea, spinal convulsions, general paralysis and death. It reduces reflex action, including tetanic spasm, by excessive stimulation of the spinal cord (Ellingwood, 1919, para. 1).

Ellingwood (1919) goes on to cite other practitioners, who state its ability to cause heart failure or arrest of respiratory action. Despite these cautions, he assures his readers that “It does not produce toxic or undesirable effects in medicinal doses” (para. 8) which he states is a half to two drams (approximately 1.85- 7.4 mL of the fluid extract, a preparation significantly more concentrated than a standard tincture). A similar assessment was made previously by Mulheron in The Medical Age (1885), who upon reviewing case reports from at least a dozen publications, declared it a “pretty reliable, and in so far as we have yet learned, harmless narcotic, which is well tolerated by the stomach” (p. 468) and a valued substitute to morphine for long term use. However, he went on in his next article to describe the efficacy and harmlessness of the use of cocaine for the vomiting of pregnancy and in seasickness, so his safety evaluation may not be entirely reliable.

Modern uses are strongly aligned with those of the Eclectics, and were likely originally based in their reports. Jamaica dogwood is widely applied as an analgesic, anti-inflammatory, and antispasmodic for both smooth and skeletal muscles (7Song, 2014; Easley & Horne, 2016; Grieve, 1931; Mcdonald, 2011; Moore, 1991; Sinadinos, 2008; Willard & Caldecott, 2011). Described as having ibuprofen-like action, pain relief seems to be best achieved by administering the extract every couple of hours until pain subsides (Sinadinos, 2008). It is a prime choice for first aid kits, as it is considered reliable, is generally well tolerated, and has a rapid effect (7Song, 2014). For acute pain, it is commonly combined with herbs such as Eschscholzia, Corydalis, Viburnum, Paeonia, Pedicularis, Passiflora, Humulus, Piper methysticum, Lactuca, Valeriana, or Scutellaria (7Song 2014; Bergner, 2012).

A variety of headaches are known to be eased with this herb, including tension, occipital, temporomandibular joint (TMJ), and migraine headaches (Sinadinos, 2008); Eric Yarnell describes Jamaica dogwood as one of the strongest North American herbs effective in preventing or stopping headaches, and suggests dosages as high as 2-3 mL every 2-3 hours to quell a cluster headache or migraine (Yarnell, 2007). For migraines, Jamaica dogwood is often combined with Tanacetum parthenium, which induces vasodilation as well as provides further anti-inflammatory action (Sinadinos, 2008).

In combination with herbs such as Corydalis yanhusuo, Scutellaria lateriflora, Pedicularis, Actaea racemosa, and/or Salix, Jamaica dogwood may address skeletal muscle pain, facilitating relaxation and reduction of inflammation, and useful for back, neck and shoulder tension or pain, sciatica related spasms, and general muscle spasms (7Song, 2014; Easley & Horne, 2016; Sinadinos, 2008).

Nerve pain may also be soothed by Jamaica dogwood (Sinadinos 2008), including even that of a “violent toothache” (Grieve, 1931, para. 3). When combined with Hypericum, Verbascum root, and/or Scutellaria, one may find even greater relief from nerve related inflammation, sciatica, cervical neuralgia, trigeminal neuralgia, Bell’s palsy, or temporomandibular joint pain (Grieve, 1931; Moore, 1991; Winston, 2001).

Sub-acute and acute flare-ups of inflammation of joints, connective tissue, or chronic inflammatory conditions may be addressed with Jamaica dogwood, including carpal tunnel syndrome, rheumatoid arthritis, chronic fatigue syndrome, fibromyalgia, or gout (Cabrera, 1994; Sinadinos, 2008; Willard & Caldecott, 2011). It combines well with other tannin rich anti-inflammatory herbs, such as Filipendula, Salix, or as mentioned previously, Hypericum (Sinadinos, 2008).

The smooth muscle antispasmodic activity of Jamaica dogwood has been applied to the respiratory spasms of whooping cough or asthma (Grieve, 1931; Sinadinos, 2008). Gastrointestinal cramping may also benefit from this plant (Sinadinos, 2008). Jamaica dogwood has been applied for the referred pain of kidney stones, gallstones, or cholangitis (Willard & Caldecot, 2011; Moore, 1997).

The plant is used in combination with herbs such as Viburnum, Valeriana, Matricara, and/or Zingiber for menstrual spasms, cramps, or other ovarian or uterine pain (Cabrera, 2001; Hoffmann, 1998). The pain of endometriosis has also been successfully addressed with Jamaica dogwood (Devi, 1998). Yarnell states that Piscidia can be used for threatened miscarriage as a spasmolytic in cases of inappropriate uterine irritability, though because it is difficult to even discern the root problem in threatened miscarriages, this has not always been effective (Yarnell, 1997). Jamaica dogwood has also been safely administered for back labor during childbirth, without interfering with contractions (Sinadinos, 2008).

This plant is a potent sedative, and is often administered when experiencing insomnia due to pain (Hoffmann, 1998). It combines well with Scutellaria, Valeriana, Lactuca, and/or Humulus for this application (7Song, 2014). Moore highlights the use of Jamaica dogwood for insomnia “with spasms, pain, nervous irritability, tachycardia” (1991, p. 12). Combined with Pulsatilla, Valeriana, or Humulus, one may experience relief from panic attacks (Ody, 1993). Rasmussin writes that Jamaica dogwood may benefit those experiencing opiate withdrawal, to relieve insomnia and nervous tension (Webb, 1998).

Adult Dose

Tincture: 10 drops to 4 mL, 3-4 times daily, of dried bark tincture (1:5, 60-80% alcohol). * ** *** Begin with low doses and only increase as needed.
Decoction: 1-2 teaspoons bark in a cup of water, simmer 10-15 mins, as needed. * This is considered an inferior preparation method due to difficulty in controlling dosage.
Topical: Apply poultice or fomentation to affected areas.
Dosage information from *The Herbal Handbook by David Hoffmann, **The Modern Herbal Dispensatory by Thomas Easley and Steven Horne, and ***The Professional Herbalist Training Course lectures by Christa Sinadinos.

Safety

While this plant is generally well tolerated and it is rare to encounter side effects, the following may guide the safe administration of this plant. Avoid use during pregnancy, except during labor under the guidance of an experienced herbalist-midwife (Sinadinos, 2008). Use caution and limit use to low-doses while breastfeeding, in elderly persons, or in those with hypotension or bradycardia (Easley & Horne, 2016; Moore, 1996; Sinadinos, 2008). Avoid use in children under 10 years of age (Sinadinos, 2008). Avoid combining with pharmaceutical or over-the-counter pain relievers or anti-inflammatories unless directed by a qualified practitioner, as it may potentate sedative medications (Easley & Horne, 2016; Sinadinos, 2008). Potential side effects one may experience in the recommended dose range may include gastric distress or nausea (Grieve, 1931), though many practitioners state it does not cause any gastric distress. This herb is commonly noted as one to approach with caution, as doses in excess of recommended doses may slow breathing excessively, and individual responses may vary (7Song, 2014; Easley & Horne, 2016; Moore, 1996; Sinadinos, 2008).
