Hawthorn

Crataegus spp.

Hawthorn, thornapple, mayblossom, mayhaw, may tree, whitehorn, hawberry, hagthorn, ladies’ meat, gazels, halves, bread and cheese tree
Family
Rosaceae
Ayurvedic name
Ban-sangli
Chinese medicine name
Shānzā (C. pinnatifida)
Parts used
Fresh or dried berries (technically pomes), flowering tops, leaves

Native To

Europe, Asia, North America, North Africa

Geographic Distribution

Temperate regions of Europe, North America, Asia, and North Africa

Botanical Description

Depending on species, hawthorn trees range in height from 3 feet to 49 feet. Its branches are thorned. The white flowers are grouped in inflorescences of 5-30 flowers (depending on species) and appear in the spring. The berry-like pomes appear in the fall and are usually bright red. The leaves are toothed and can be elliptic or oval shaped, and some are deeply lobed (Chevallier, 2000; Phipps et al., 2003).

Key Constituents

Bioflavonoids (vitexin, rutin, quercetin, and hyperoside), triterpenoids, oligomeric proanthocyanins (epicatechin and procyanidins), phenolic acids, polyphenols, tannins (Chevallier, 2000; American College of Healthcare Sciences, 2008).

Sustainability

None known.

Harvesting Guidelines

The flowers and leaves are harvested in the spring, and the ripe berries may be harvested in the fall. Be sure to mind the large thorns found along the branches!

Uses

Sitting at the bookends of the time of growth, she guards the seasonal shifts – but rooted at the edge of the field, she also guards the border between the wild and the hearth. She loves humans, and thrives with our touch, but she remains forever untamed…   — Guido Masé, 2014

The beautiful, gnarly hawthorn tree shares certain characteristics of her rose family (Rosaceae) relatives, including bearing thorns like roses and producing edible fruits like apple, pear, apricot, and cherry trees. There are nearly 300 species of hawthorn, which takes its name from the Old English term haw, which means “hedge,” alluding to the popularity of using the common hawthorn (Crataegus monogyna) tree as a hedgerow shrub in England and Ireland.

In England, the lovely blossoms (sometimes stinky, depending on species) appear in May, thus inspiring its common English names of mayblossom and maythorn. In days past, a hawthorn tree was tied with ribbons during Beltane (May Day) to make wishes and ask for blessings from the supernatural.

Crataegus is derived from the Greek word for hard, kratos (Murray, 1995). The species most known used as food and herbal benefits are C. monogyna, the one-seed hedgerow tree common in England; C. pinnatifida, Chinese hawthorn; and C. laevigata, native to the midlands of Great Britain, central Europe, and North Africa. C. oxyacantha is an older name commonly used by herbalists for what is now called C. laevigata, but oxyacantha is no longer officially recognized by botanists (Foster, 2009).

The leaves, flowers, and fruit of C. monogyna and C. laevigata are used as herbal support and food in Western herbalism, and the Chinese used C. pinnatifida fruit to make traditional desserts, jams, and jellies as well as a general digestive aid, and as a remedy for diarrhea, dysentery, and heart pains (Dharmananda, 2004; Foster, 2009).

The leaves, flowers, and fruit of C. monogyna and C. laevigata are used as food and in Western herbalism, and in China the fruit of C. pinnatifida is used to make traditional desserts, jams, and jellies as well as a digestive aid, for diarrhea and dysentery, and heart pains (Dharmananda, 2004; Foster, 2009). 

Cherokee peoples have a long-standing tradition of working with hawthorn as a food, beneficial herb, and even with the tough wood and thorns as a tool. The berries are consumed in Cherokee culture as an appetite stimulant, while an infusion of the inner bark is used to soothe spasms and promote strong circulation, a use which has carried into the present (Hamel & Chiltoskey, 1975). Hawthorn’s reputation as a tree of protection is evidenced by the bark tea being drunk or bathed in by Cherokee sports players before games to “ward off tacklers” (Hamel & Chiltoskey, 1975, p. 37). In The Cherokee Herbal, hawthorn is best known for its calming and relaxing properties and its use in blends to settle the heart (Garrett, 2003). 

The Indigenous Israeli hawthorn has a history of use in traditional Arab medicine for heart disease, cancer, sexual weakness, and diabetes (Ljubuncic et al., 2005).

Hawthorn’s current use as an herbal cardiac tonic was encouraged in England in the late 1800s, after an Irish physician named Greene became well-known for success among his heart patients with a hawthorn berry extract (Foster, 2009). Ellingwood reports that in 1896, an American physician in Chicago, Dr. Jennings, published a letter in the New York Medical Journal reporting success in giving his own heart patients hawthorn, proclaiming:

Crataegus oxyacantha is superior to any other of the well known and tried remedies at present in use in the treatment of heart disease… [and] …it may be regarded as specific, or the nearest approach to a specific, in the following cardiac diseases: Angina pectoris, valvular deficiency, with or without enlargement, endo-myo and pericarditis, tachycardia, rheumatism (co-called) of the heart, cardiac neuralgias, from whatever cause, palpitation, vertigo, apoplexy, dropsy, and functional derangements (Ellingwood, 1919).

American Eclectic physician Finley Ellingwood (1919) writes less enthusiastically about hawthorn for the heart, but found it useful in cases of heart weakness with murmurs and difficulty breathing as well as in nervous tension and weakness.

The German Commission E has approved only the use of hawthorn leaf with flower for decreased cardiac output as defined by the New York Heart Association (NYHA)’s definition of Class (or Stage) II heart disease:

Patients with cardiac disease resulting in slight limitation of physical activity. They are comfortable at rest. Ordinary physical activity results in fatigue, palpitation, dyspnea, or anginal pain (American Heart Association).

Today, herbalists use hawthorn extract (from berry, leaf, and/or flower) as a general cardiac tonic, a cardiac trophorestorative, and for cases of angina, high blood pressure, early stages of congestive heart failure, and atherosclerosis (Murray, 1995). It is the go-to herb for many heart-related conditions, including support after a heart attack. The flavonoids (proanthocyanidins and anthocyanidins) in hawthorn extract are thought to promote these actions, but according to Verma et al. (2007), “no single agent has proven to be the primary agent for the effect observed.” Hawthorn berries, leaves, and flowers are also used for their nervine and anti-inflammatory actions.

While the berry is popular among herbalists, the hawthorn extracts most commonly studied scientifically are standardized, commercial extracts of leaf and flower: Crataegutt WS® 1442, which is 45% ethanol dry extract of flowers and leaves standardized to contain 18.75% of oligomeric procyanidins; and Faros® LI 132, which is a 70% methanol dry extract of leaf and flower, standardized to 2.25% flavonoids (Verma et al, 2007; Engels & Brinckmann, 2012). The berries contain higher amounts of procyanidins, but the leaves and flowers have a higher total content of phenolic compounds than the berries (Yang and Liu, 2012). The hawthorn berry as a single preparation as well as the single use of leaf and flower are not approved by the German Commission E.

Hawthorn extract is believed to increase the strength of heart muscle contractions, as well as helping to modestly reduce serum cholesterol levels and plaque deposits in the arteries (Foster, 2009; Murray, 1995), although the latter assertion needs more research on the clinical level. Hawthorn appears to improve the mechanics of the heart and its metabolic processes, dilate coronary arteries, and inhibit enzymes that cause vasoconstriction (Murray, 1995; Verma et al., 2007). These actions make it useful in early congestive heart failure. In 2001, a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled study found that Stage II heart failure patients taking standardized hawthorn extract for three months showed borderline statistically significant improvements in exercise tolerance and decreased blood pressure. Researchers concluded that the extract was clinically effective and was safe and well-tolerated (Zapfe, 2001).

A 2003 randomized, double-blind, and placebo-controlled study by Degenring et al. found that Stage II heart failure patients taking a standardized fresh berry extract for 2 months experienced significantly increased exercise tolerance as well as lowered blood pressure.

A Cochrane Review (Pittler et al., 2008) examined 14 qualifying clinical trials of hawthorn used by chronic heart failure patients using hawthorn leaf and flower extract, mostly as an adjuvant. The review found:

  • Hawthorn was more beneficial than placebo in the physiologic outcome of maximal workload.
  • Hawthorn extract resulted in significantly increased exercise tolerance.
  • A beneficial decrease in the pressure-heart rate product, an index of cardiac oxygen consumption, in those taking hawthorn.
  • A significant improvement in issues such as shortness of breath and fatigue by those taking hawthorn.
  • Mild and infrequent adverse events, which included nausea, dizziness, cardiac, and gastrointestinal complaints.

In 2008, results were published in the European Journal of Heart Failure from a 2-year, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, multicenter German study. Over 2 years, half of a group of 2,681 patients with Class II or Class III heart failure and reduced left ventricular ejection fraction took a placebo, and the other half took 900 mg a day of a proprietary commercial extract of C. monogyna leaf and flower (WS® 1442). Researchers found that those taking the hawthorn reduced the risk of dying in the short term (40% fewer deaths in the first 6 months, and 20% fewer deaths in the first 18 months), but after 2 years the same percentage of deaths occurred in both groups. However, those in the hawthorn group experienced less side effects and chest pain than the placebo group. The researchers concluded that the extract was safe in patients taking cardiac drugs and “in addition, the data may indicate that WS® 1442 can potentially reduce the incidence of sudden cardiac death, at least in patients with less compromised left ventricular function” (Holubarsch et al., 2008).

Naturopathic physician Michael T. Murray (1995) explains that hawthorn’s flavonoids help protect and stabilize the collagen matrix within the artery by cross-linking fibers, scavenging free radicals, quenching inflammation, and inhibiting the release of inflammatory agents. Maintaining sound arterial integrity and protecting the arterial walls from lesions discourage cholesterol deposits. Hawthorn also inhibits LDL oxidation (Quettier-Deleu et al., 2003).

Some preclinical studies verify hawthorn’s artery-protecting actions, but there has been only one clinical study and the results were mixed. In 2011, a placebo-controlled, double-blind trial included 49 diabetic patients with coronary heart disease who were given either a placebo or 400 mg of C. laevigata leaf and flower extract three times a day. After taking baseline measurements, the patients’ blood cell count, lipid profile, C-reactive protein, neutrophil elastase (NE), and malondialdehyde were analyzed at 1 month and 6 months. The NE decreased in the C. laevigata group and the LDL cholesterol levels decreased when used in combination with statin drugs. There were no significant changes otherwise after 6 months (Dalli et al., 2011).  Another study in 2014 used a crataegus-based multiherb formula (C. pinnatifida; Alisma orientalis, Stigma maydis, Ganoderma lucidum, Polygonum multiflorum, and Morus alba). After 12 weeks, the patients on the formula experienced mild beneficial effects on LDL levels without adverse events (Hu et al., 2014).

The part of the plant and the time of harvest should be considered when developing and using hawthorn preparations. According to Liu et al. (2011), who analyzed C. grayana leaves and fruit during ripening, “[t]otal phenolic contents reached highest levels by the end of August in fruits and by the end of September in leaves.” There has been disagreement among herbalists about whether the seeds of hawthorn fruit are toxic. Herbalist Guido Masé (2010) believes it’s only the skin of the seed that contains trace amounts of cyanogenic glycosides, which are also found in apple seeds, but that the compound disperses quickly without building up in the body.

Extracts from the peel of the hawthorn fruit have been found to induce cell death in human breast cancer cells (Li et al., 2013), and extracts from the seeds of C. pinnatifida have shown cytotoxic activity against human cancer cell lines (Huang et al., 2013). Extracts from the bark of C. oxyacantha have shown “antioxidant/detoxifying effects in hepatocytes, and this may explain the hepatoprotective and chemopreventive properties of these phytochemicals” (Krajka-Kuźniak et al., 2014).

Hawthorn is also a calming nervine and is used much like rose, and often with rose, to soothe, open, and protect the energetic heart. Hawthorn is herbalist Rosemary Gladstar’s (2010) favorite herb for sadness, grief, and loss or when the heart needs a bit of extra protection. Herbalist David Winston also suggests it for people with “disturbed shen, especially attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD)” (Winston & Maimes, 2007. p. 210). It is also helpful in cases of anxiety. A 2004 double-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled study to evaluate the efficacy and safety of a fixed combination of hawthorn flowers (C. oxyacantha), California poppy, and magnesium in mild-to-moderate anxiety disorders. Researchers concluded that the formula was safe and more effective than placebo in addressing mild-to-moderate anxiety disorders (Hanus et al., 2004).

Known as the Lady of May, hawthorn carries a rich magical tradition, and is associated with the sacred Christian crown of thorns, Celtic Beltane rites, a Welsh goddess who scattered a trail of hawthorn petals that became the Milky Way, and with Joseph of Arimathea, who is said to have brought the hawthorn tree to Britain along with the Holy Grail. Superstitions about the tree still abound in many places where it is considered bad luck to cut down a hawthorn, which is both celebrated and respected, but often feared. This archetype persists today in modern fairy tales like Harry Potter, where hawthorn wood is used to make wands, that are “as full of paradoxes as the tree that gave it birth, whose leaves and blossoms heal, and yet whose cut branches smell of death” (Pottermore.wikia.com).

Many herbalists plant hawthorn in their herb gardens to offer protection, or along the edges of their property to guard the land. When visiting with a hawthorn tree, you might also sense its protective but fierce, ever-wild energy in its sharp thorns, delicate blossoms, rich red berries, and rugged wood.

Adult Dose

Infusion: 0.75-6 g dried aerial parts/day divided into 1-3 doses (Mills & Bone, 2005)

Berry tincture: 2.5 mL (1:5, 40%) 3x/day, 2.5 mL morning and evening as maintenance, up to 5 mL 3x/day for acute conditions (Hoffmann, 2003).



Safety

It was originally thought that hawthorn may potentiate cardiac drugs and may lower the required dosage (Hoffmann, 2003; Blumenthal, 2003), and therefore be unsafe for those taking heart medications. A 2008 two-year study of 900 cardiac patients on standard drug therapy for heart disease including ACE inhibitors and beta-blockers, as well as hawthorn leaf/flower extract, found that hawthorn could be safely taken with other heart medications (Holubarsch et al., 2008). In addition, Walker et al. (2006) report that hawthorn does not contain cardioactive glycosides as once thought by some. However, all heart patients should speak with their physician before taking hawthorn extracts.

Some say the seeds may be toxic because of trace amounts of cyanogenic glycosides (also found in apple seeds), but others disagree (Masé, 2010). Care should be taken not to be pricked by the thorns found along hawthorn branches. They may cause infection or septic arthritis (Francis et al., 2011).
