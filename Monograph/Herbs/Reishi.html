Native To

China and Europe (G. lucidum), East Asia (G. lingzhi) (Loyd et al., 2018), North America (G. sessile, G. curtisii, G. tsugae) (Lloyd & Blanchette, 2019).

Geographic Distribution

Worldwide in both tropical and temperate climates. In North America, reishi is most frequently found on the East Coast, particularly in the Gulf Coast and Southwestern regions (Hobbs, 1986). 

Botanical Description

Reishi is a shelf polypore fungus with a shiny surface that gives it a varnished appearance. It has a distinctive red color and can also tend toward reddish-brown or reddish black. The semicircular or fan-shaped fungus has concentric zones, with the outer margin changing from white to yellowish as it matures until it becomes the same reddish color (Marley, 2009). The underside is white and has tiny pores that become more brown with age. Reishi’s cap is typically up to 30 cm wide and 30 mm thick, while the stem which may or may not be present is around 3-14 cm tall and 0.5-3 cm thick (Arora, 1986). Reishi is usually found growing on hardwood stumps and logs, most commonly maple, oak, and willow, and sometimes conifers (typically hemlock), depending on the species. 

The fruiting bodies have a woody texture (Wachtel-Galor et al., 2004) and generally possess a distinct stalk that connects to the cap from the side, although instances of stalkless specimens have been documented (Hobbs, 1986).

Key Constituents

Polysaccharides (beta-D-glucans), triterpenes (ganoderic acids A, C, D), lipids, lectins, fatty acids (oleic acid), peptidoglycans, proteins, glycoproteins, steroids, nucleotide ( adenosine), alkaloids, vitamins, minerals (magnesium, calcium, zinc, iron, copper, cyclooctasulfur) (Boh et al., 2007; Hobbs, 1986). 

Sustainability

In the wild, reishi is quite rare, existing on about 0.03% of trees that could harbor it, including oaks and plum trees (Babu & Subhasree, 2008). For instance, in Japan 99% of wild reishi are found on plum trees, yet only a few reishi mushrooms are discovered per 100,000 plum trees (Matsumoto, 1979). However, this mushroom is primarily cultivated in modern times.

Harvesting Guidelines

Accurate identification of wild mushrooms is essential. Due to its distinctive varnished appearance, it’s difficult to confuse any other mushroom for reishi. While artist’s conk (Ganoderma applanatum) shares some similarities, it stands apart as a perennial polypore characterized by its matte brown cap and the red-belted polypore (Fomitopsis pinicola) has cream to light-brown flesh and prominently exhibits a red band along its upper margin. Harvest relatively mature (but not old or boggy or disintegrating) reishi that has a more or less white-to-beige underside, but that has lost its white growing tip around the outer edge. Chop or slice while still soft and fresh before thoroughly drying. A meat slicer works well for particularly thick reishi. 

Uses

Various species of reishi are explored throughout this monograph, although much of the literature focuses on G. lucidum specifically. There is some lack of clarity regarding the exact species that make up the “reishi” group of mushrooms. The binomial “Ganoderma lucidum” is commonly assigned to many “functional/beneficial” reishi items in commerce, and it is thought that this species is used traditionally in China and Japan. However, a number of other reishi species have been reported, including at least 10 North American species documented (Lloyd & Blanchette, 2019). These include G. sessile and G. curtisii, both of which grow on hardwood trees, and G. tsugae, also called hemlock varnish shelf, which grows on hemlock trees. Often, these are used interchangeably and their useful qualities are believed to be equivalent to those traditionally used in Asia. 

While a significant portion of the literature focuses on G. lucidum, various species are explored in this monograph. Reishi is a beautiful polypore fungus with a long and venerable history of use in Chinese Medicine and other Asian traditions. Ganoderma derives from a Greek word meaning “sheen” and lucidum is Latin for “brightness,” so named because of reishi’s richly colored and shining lacquered appearance when wet. Regional names for reishi provide a hint to its universal use as a life extending herb: reishi means “divine” in the language of Japan, where it is also called mannentake or “10,000-year mushroom;” in China it is called ling zhi or “tree of life mushroom;” and in Vietnam, it is called linh chi, “supernatural mushroom.” Reishi is also nicknamed the “mushroom of immortality” (Hobbs, 1986). 

In traditional Japanese Medicine, G. lucidum was believed to exist as six different types, categorized by color, each with specific uses and strengths: blue reishi was traditionally used to improve eyesight and liver function and promote a calming effect; red reishi was indicated for supporting internal organs, enhancing memory, and increasing vitality; yellow reishi was associated with strengthening spleen function and providing a sense of calm (shen); white reishi was known for its ability to strengthen lung function; black reishi was valued for its kidney-protective properties; and purple reishi was used to improve ear function and support joints and muscles (Hobbs, 1986). The specific indications for each type of reishi originate from the writings of Japanese alchemist Ko Hung; it’s worth noting that today, black and purple reishi types are identified as a different species called Ganoderma sinense, while red reishi, the most potent of the varieties, may still be referred to as G. lucidum or G. capense (Hobbs, 1986). 

It’s interesting to note that taxonomy within the Ganoderma genus is complex; historically, most laccate (shiny) reishi species that grow on hardwood trees have been commonly referred to as Ganoderma lucidum (Loyd et al., 2018). DNA analysis now tells us that Ganoderma lucidum is native to Europe and certain regions in China, whereas Ganoderma lingzhi, the most commonly grown and researched variety in herbalism, is Indigenous to East Asia; with more advances in molecular studies Ganoderma lucidum has been further subdivided into distinct species (Loyd et al., 2018). 

Another species of reishi (G. tsugae) grows on dead or dying hemlock trees in the eastern U.S. and can be used almost interchangeably with the Chinese species, although herbalist David Winston claims it does not offer the “profound effects” that G. lucidum does, a notion debated among herbalists (Winston & Maimes, 2007). Other regional species like G. curtisii and G. oregonense are likewise utilized, though whether they are identical in terms of benefit is debated. While there are over 300 species of Ganoderma (Petruzzello, 2022), all have differing degrees of herbal benefit, use, and interchangeability.

At one time, reishi was used exclusively by royalty as a “superior herb” in ancient China (Winston & Maimes, 2007). Historically, reishi mushrooms were found only in limited amounts in their natural habitat, leading to their high cost, though the successful cultivation of reishi has made it more accessible to people; Shigeaki Mori, a Japanese pioneer, dedicated 15 years to developing an intricate way of inoculating wild reishi spores in the carefully prepared sawdust of old plum trees (Hobbs, 1986).

Today reishi is used primarily as an immunomodulant, adaptogen, anti-tumor, anti-inflammatory, hypocholesterolemic, liver protective, nervine, and antiviral herb (Winston & Maimes, 2007). Reishi is high in polysaccharide content with at least 36 different compounds identified. It also produces a group of triterpenes, called ganoderic acids, which have a molecular structure similar to steroid hormones. Reishi also contains lipids, lectins, fatty acids, peptidoglycans, proteins, adenosine, vitamins, and essential minerals (Boh et al., 2007). The stalk and cap contain minerals such as magnesium, calcium, zinc, and iron, with the stalk also containing copper (Hobbs, 1986). With its wide range of benefits, no wonder it was considered life extending! 

With a bitter, earthy taste, reishi is warming and fairly drying, and care should be used in those with dry constitutions. When preparing reishi decoction, boil the dried mushroom in water for longer than you would a root decoction (reishi should be boiled for at least one to preferably 3-5+ hours). A slice or two of reishi can also be added to the cooking water for rice or included in long-simmering broths, soups, and stews. The mushroom slice can be frozen or re-dehydrated after its first use and used again. 

Reishi has a longstanding tradition in Chinese medicine as a tonic for promoting longevity. In Chinese medicine, reishi is regarded for enhancing one’s emotional well-being, fortifying the heart, boosting vital energy (qi), and nourishing the blood (Powell, 2014); these qualities are often associated by many Western herbalists with mood enhancement, increased energy levels, and improved immune system function. Furthermore, these properties align with how reishi is often used in Western herbalism as an adaptogen. 

An herb that helps the body adapt to stress, reishi boosts general immune system strength through its action as an immunomodulator and immunostimulant (Easley & Horne, 2016). As an immune system modulator, it can both strengthen the body’s natural defenses or “down-regulate” an overactive response in people with autoimmune disorders (Winston & Maimes, 2007). Reishi is believed to boost white blood cell production and increase the activity of macrophages, which scavenge the immune system for potential threats (Green, 2000). 

As a nervine, in Chinese medicine reishi helps calm the shen, which translates as spirit (but not in the soul-sense) or consciousness and emotional balance (Winston & Maimes, 2007). Reishi can soothe disturbed shen that manifests as insomnia, nightmares, sleeplessness, and other signs of anxiety. Herbalist and mushroom expert, Christopher Hobbs (1986), specifically indicates reishi as a calming herb for individuals experiencing nervousness, poor sleep, or tension that presents with adrenal fatigue, exhaustion, or depletion. Herbalists Thomas Easley and Steven Horne (2016) share that an alcohol extraction of reishi is ideal when seeking the calming and anxiolytic properties of the mushroom as it primarily extracts the triterpenes, while decoction is the best method for immune activity as it primarily extracts the polysaccharides; though dual extractions are an option, the authors find they aren’t as potent as using alcohol or water extracts independently, a viewpoint debated among herbalists. 

Reishi contains compounds that fortify both the structure and capability of the lungs, resulting in improved utilization of oxygen, making this functional mushroom particularly beneficial for individuals susceptible to conditions such as bronchitis, pneumonia, asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and other lung related imbalances; consistent use is suggested to help proactively ward off recurring infections, enhance oxygen use, ease fatigue, boost red blood cell functionality, and maintain a balanced immune response (Groves, 2016). Additionally, reishi is regarded for enhancing blood oxygenation, and herbalist Ed Smith (2011) also suggests reishi to help stave off or lessen high-altitude sickness. Reishi mushrooms are known to possess anti-allergic properties attributed to their terpene content; ganoderic acid C stands out as the most active anti-allergic component, trailed by ganoderic acids A and D in terms of potency (Hobbs, 1986). Additionally, oleic acid and cyclooctasulfur are regarded for inhibiting the release of histamine, which helps to inhibit allergic reactions and reduce inflammation (Hobbs, 1986).  

Reishi may also have antiviral effects, and as part of a mixture of other herbs (Wisteria floribunda, Trapa natans, Terminalia chebulae, Coicis lachryma-jobi, and Elfuinga applanata), has been shown to shorten the duration of illness in those with the herpes simplex virus (Hijikata et al., 2007). One in vitro study in which a plaque reduction assay was used (a common laboratory technique in virology to test the antiviral activity of substances) identified two protein-bound polysaccharides, a neutral one (NPBP) and an acidic one (APBP), extracted from water-soluble components of reishi, which demonstrated effectiveness against both HSV-1 and HSV-2 (Eo et al., 2000). These findings indicate that the antiherpetic (anti-herpes virus) activity of reishi’s polysaccharides appears to be associated with its ability to bind to HSV-specific glycoproteins; furthermore, APBP disrupts the intricate relationship between the viruses and cell membranes (Eo et al., 2000). In terms of other antiviral actions, reishi also induces interferon production, a key aspect of the body’s immune response to viral infections (Smith, 2011). 

One mechanism of action for reishi and other functional mushrooms involves complex polysaccharides, particularly glucans, which prompt an immune response wherein the body mistakenly identifies them as potential threats, which is not surprising considering some mushrooms are indeed poisonous. In essence, these glucans provide a type of schooling regimen for the immune system, bettering the performance of various immune cells and pathways while diverting focus away from allergies and autoimmune conditions; this results in immune cells becoming more robust and skilled in both combating common illnesses like colds and eradicating abnormal cells that contribute to cancer (Groves, 2016). Reishi is widely regarded for improving resilience towards both physical and mental stress. Reishi is believed to optimize adrenal gland function and is indicated in cases of adrenal deficiency, particularly as a revitalizing tonic following an extended or exhausting illness (Smith, 2011). 

According to herbalist Ed Smith (2011), reishi is known for its ability to promote liver detoxification and enhance overall liver function, making it a valuable supportive option for liver-related conditions, including degenerative liver states and hepatitis. Reishi is believed to be a hepatoprotective, possibly due to mushroom’s phenolics, triterpenes, polysaccharides, and peptides (Soares et al., 2013), although there have actually been a few human cases (at least one fatal) of liver toxicity after ingestion of powdered reishi. These individuals had taken traditionally prepared reishi for some time without incident before switching to powder, which may have been contaminated with adulterants or not prepared properly (Yuen et al., 2004; Wanmuang et al., 2007). However, a 2004 controlled study on human biomarker responses to a commercially prepared reishi supplement showed no evidence of damage to the liver, kidneys, or DNA (Wachtel-Galor et al., 2004). And in 2007, a small (16 individuals), double-blind, randomized, and placebo-controlled study on the safety and tolerability of reishi discovered no adverse effects (Wicks et al., 2007).

Reishi is included in a Chinese Medicine approach to cancer; numerous Chinese and Japanese legends recount individuals with cancer and various chronic illnesses embarking on extensive journeys in search of this remarkable mushroom (Hobbs, 1986). Reishi’s extensive study of its potential in cancer treatment is partly attributed to its long history of use. Apart from bolstering the body’s natural immune response, reishi contains compounds that may directly induce the death of cancer cells and inhibit the proliferation and spread of tumors; isolated polysaccharides from reishi have shown to enhance immune responses in advanced-stage cancer patients, as supported by both preclinical and clinical research (Gao et al., 2013). While the mushroom has shown some in vivo and in vitro activity against cancer cells (Gao et al., 2013; Martínez-Montemayor et al., 2011; Jiang et al., 2004; Silva, 2002), additional and better-designed studies are needed. One study (Oka et al., 2010) used a water-soluble extract from cultured reishi in those with colorectal adenomas, which are benign tumors that often transform into malignant cancers; another group were not given the reishi as a control; all individuals were examined after one year, and those taking the reishi extract showed a decrease in size of adenomas, while the control group’s adenomas increase in total size. A review of clinical trials examining reishi’s potential in cancer treatment reveals limited evidence supporting its use as a first-line protocol and uncertainty regarding its impact on prolonging survival, however it suggests that reishi “could be administered as an alternative adjunct to conventional treatment in consideration of its potential of enhancing tumor response and stimulating host immunity” (Jin et al., 2012).  One in vitro and in vivo study investigated the effects of G. lucidum spore oil on breast cancer cells and found that it inhibited breast cancer cell growth through promoting apoptosis, specifically of MDA-MB-231 breast cancer cells (Jiao et al., 2020). Furthermore, a comprehensive review of reishi use in breast cancer found that “therapeutic approaches that incorporate Ganoderma lucidum are 1.25 times more likely to yield better tumor responses than those that do not” (Rossi et al., 2018, p. 24851). As an adjunct support in cases of cancer, reishi can help guard against damage from ionizing radiation and side effects of chemotherapy (Smith, 2011). There has been ex vivo research using human blood cells (leukocytes and human peripheral blood lymphocytes) which shows that the beta-glucan found in a water extract of reishi has a protective effect against damage caused by gamma-ray radiation; additionally, in vitro studies of plasmid show this beta-glucan can lower adverse effects of radiation (González et al., 2020).

A later review by Boh (2013) reports that reishi’s anti-cancer activity may be attributed to at least five groups of mechanisms:

(1) activation/modulation of the immune response of the host
(2) direct cytotoxicity to cancer cells
(3) inhibition of tumor-induced angiogenesis
(4) inhibition of cancer cells proliferation and invasive metastasis behavior, and
(5) carcinogens deactivation with protection of cells (Boh, 2013).

Traditionally, reishi has been used to strengthen the Heart in Chinese medicine, not to be confused with the physiological heart. According to Chinese medicine, shen or spirit, resides within the Heart and embodies the complete expression of a person’s life force, including emotional vitality, mental clarity, and physical health; shen also encompasses higher moral and spiritual faculties, including a conscious connection with nature and humanity, which accompanied by the compassion that naturally arises from these sentiments, emanates from the Heart (Beinfield & Korngold, 1991). 

Reishi also supports the physical heart. Historically, it was believed that the red color of reishi was responsible for its health-promoting properties; in the Ming Dynasty, reishi was known as qi zhi which translates to “red fungus” (Hobbs, 1986). Reishi is indicated for cardiac and circulatory imbalances, including atrial fibrillation, atherosclerosis, angina, elevated blood pressure or cholesterol levels, and stroke (Chan et al., 2021; Smith, 2011). Moreover, reishi contributes to healthy platelet function and promotes blood clotting when appropriate, a vital aspect of cardiovascular health (Green, 2000). In fact, reishi’s applications extend to addressing various conditions associated with aging, including immune system dysfunction, coronary heart disease, elevated cholesterol levels, and arthritis, while also bolstering energy levels and promoting overall wellbeing (Gladstar, 2017). 

In a randomized, double-blind, cross-over study (Chu et al., 2012) with placebo-controlled run-in and cross-over periods, reishi was administered along with a placebo to those with elevated blood pressure and cholesterol; while reishi was well tolerated, data showed no changes in BMI or blood pressure when supported with either reishi or placebo. However, plasma insulin and homeostasis model assessment-insulin resistance were lower after a protocol with reishi than after placebo, and HDL (“good cholesterol”) increased with reishi but not with placebo in the first period; according to Chu et al. (2012), “Results indicate that Lingzhi might have mild antidiabetic effects and potentially improve the dyslipidaemia of diabetes, as shown previously in some animal studies.” While there is not any safety data on reishi in pregnancy, herbal expert Dr. Aviva Romm notes that it may be beneficial for its antihypertensive effects; she considers it safe, yet doesn’t suggest regular consumption during pregnancy (Romm, 2018), though this is debated by some who utilize reishi throughout pregnancy, referring to newborns born after this practice as “reishi babies.” 

While in vitro studies have supported the positive effects of reishi as a cardiovascular support, clinical trials are limited; moreover, the use of different reishi products in these studies makes it challenging to compare results. Among the cardioprotective benefits, reishi’s hypoglycemic actions are well-established in laboratory settings, yet lack clinical trial support; some compounds in reishi, namely polysaccharide peptides, show promise for lowering lipids and blood pressure, yet it is important to note that uncertainties exist regarding their bioavailability in various reishi formulations (Chan et al., 2021). The terpenoid compounds in reishi may be behind its potential mechanism of action as a hypotensive; these terpenoid compounds are thought to inhibit angiotensin enzyme (ACE), which contributes to lowering blood pressure (Powell, 2014). Ultimately, to better understand reishi’s role in cardiovascular support there needs to be further research into active ingredients and their bioavailability, with this knowledge applied to clinical trials. 

Adult Dose

Decoction: 1-2 ounces dried and cut reishi in 32 fluid ounces water, decoct for 1-3 hours until volume reduced by half; drink 3-4 cups/day (Winston & Maimes, 2007).

Tincture: 2-8 mL (1:5, 75%), 3x/day (Easley & Horne, 2016). A double extraction in both water and alcohol is ideal to maximize the amount of both water- and alcohol-soluble constituents.

The conscious consumer will take care to avoid commercial reishi products that are simply the indigestible powdered mushroom, as the mushroom must first be extracted before powdering.

Safety

Reishi should be used with caution in individuals taking immunosuppressant, anticoagulant, or antiplatelet medications (Gardner & McGuffin, 2013). Be mindful that there have been cases in which people exhibited allergic reactions to the fruiting body, spores, or both (Hobbs, 1986).
