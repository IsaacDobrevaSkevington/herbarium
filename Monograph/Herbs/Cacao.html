Cacao

Native To

Mexico, Central America, northern South America (Colombia, Ecuador, Venezuela, Brazil, Guyana, Suriname, and French Guiana) (Royal Botanic Gardens of Kew [RBGK], n.d.).

Geographic Distribution

Tropical West Africa, New Guinea, Madagascar, Sri Lanka, Ghana. (de Souza et al., 2018). Cacao will only flower between 20º north and south of the equator (Pendell, 2002).

Botanical Description

A spindly evergreen tree, cacao grows from 5-8 meters in height and finds its niche in the understory of tropical forests. Due to the competitive nature of the tropical understory, cacao can occasionally grow upwards of 20 m in height in competition for sunlight (de Souza et al., 2018). The root system is pivoting, and while some consider it a taproot (RBGK, n.d.) others have noted that the secondary roots that reside within 30 cm of topsoil are responsible for gathering the nutrients the plant needs (de Souza et al., 2018). The leaves of the cacao are large, leathery, and elliptical, with a prominent rib down the middle. They vary in color and may or may not possess hair depending on the cultivar (RBGK, n.d.). When new, the leaves are supple and range from light green to violet, depending on levels of anthocyanins present, and will age to become stiff and green (de Souza et al., 2018). The stem begins as smooth, but evolves to become rough and bumpy after the production of flower clusters (de Souza et al., 2018).

Cacao flowers are small, yellowish white to pale pink, and grouped together in clusters that arise directly from the trunk, a process that is called cauliflory development (RBGK, n.d.). These hermaphroditic flowers appear most often in the armpits of old leaves. The composition of the flower is based on the number five: five stamens, five sepals, five petals, and one pistol that has five ovules (de Souza et al., 2018). Because of its highly specific and small structure, the cacao flower is almost exclusively pollinated by midges, a species of insect closely related to mosquitoes (Pendell, 2002). A cacao plant can produce up to 100,000 flowers per season, but only about 0.1% of these flowers will turn into fruit, with unpollinated flowers dropping off of the plant within 48 hours of blooming; fertilized flowers will remain fixed on the plant, developing into the fruit (de Souza et al., 2018).

It is of note that well-manicured plantations often have notoriously low yields, which may have to do with the fact that the natural understory environment in which cacao evolved was rich in decaying plant matter, providing a breeding ground for midges (Pendell, 2002). The fruit of the cacao plant is a fleshy pod that is green when unripe, turning varying shades of yellow, orange-red, or purple when ripened. The ripening process can take between 140-205 days, with an average of 167 days (de Souza et al., 2018). Once ripe, the pods contain 30-40 seeds that are covered in a bittersweet white pulp (RBGK, n.d.). The seeds are dried and fermented to produce the cacao that is used for the production of chocolate.

While a number of different cultivars of cacao are grown, three in particular stand out. The Forastero variety is the most common and is responsible for about 80-90% of cacao cultivation worldwide; this cultivar possesses rounded, almost smooth fruits (RBGK, n.d.). The Criollo variety is often considered to produce the best of all chocolate, but its importance is on the wane due to hybridization techniques (RBGK, n.d.). A hybrid of Criollo and Forestero was created and is known as Trinitario; while a good quality hybrid, Trinitario produces a less intense flavor than Criollo (RBGK, n.d.).

 

Key Constituents

Polyphenols, flavanols, proanthocyanidins, catechins, xanthines, caffeine, theobromine, lipids (oleum theobromatis), carbohydrates, and minerals (magnesium, zinc, iron, copper, phosphorus) (Felter & Lloyd, 1898; Harvard, 2020; Pendell, 2002).

 

Sustainability

In recent years, several sustainability issues regarding cacao have surfaced. Deforestation, climate change, increased production, and child labor all play a part in complicating the issue of sustainable cacao (World Wildlife Foundation [WWF], 2017). Globally, 70% of the world’s cacao beans are grown in West Africa; climate change, prolonged droughts, and increased average temperatures have made growing cacao increasingly difficult, leading to lower yields in these delicate plants (WWF, 2017).

With slow maturity rates and increased demand, Theobroma cacao has been straddling a dangerous line. One study done in 2011 by Peter Blommer of the Blommer Chocolate Company, the largest cacao processor in North America, indicated that a 3% increase in chocolate consumption would require the increase of nearly 1.8 million metric tons of cacao by 2025. This would require roughly a 50% increase in production to meet projected need (Blommer, 2011). Poor crop yield is one of the major problems the industry faces, and in many of the areas where cacao is grown, additional farm land is unavailable and expansion would have undesirable environmental impacts (Blommer, 2011). Thus, much of the focus of sustainability efforts have been focused on efficiency in hybrid creation and genetic technologies rather than expansion (Blommer, 2011).

Cacao farming has in recent history become a highly invasive process responsible for massive deforestation in the areas of its production. It is projected that in Cote d’Ivoire alone, 70% of illegal deforestation is due to the farming of cacao (WWF, 2017). Furthermore, the cacao harvest is fraught with ethical dilemmas. Child labor is frequently used to help with growing, harvesting, and transporting cacao beans in Western Africa; in the 2013-2014 growing season, about 2 million children were recorded as involved in cacao production (WWF, 2017). Chocolate companies worldwide have repeatedly missed their marks when it comes to removing children from their cacao supply chain (Drew & Boal, 2019). Ethical consumption of chocolate is possible, however, and research should be done into the sourcing and guidelines used in both harvesting and production.

Harvesting Guidelines

Once the cacao pods are ripe, which in most species is indicated by a brightly colored pod, a clean cut through the peduncle (or stem) is made. Care is taken not to damage the stem or other flowers on the branch, which may yet be fertilized. The pods are then split, revealing the white pulp encasing the cacao seeds (sometimes referred to as “beans”). The pulp is separated from the cacao seeds, and the juice released from the pulp in this process may be drunk or eaten (Greene, 2018). It was this sweet white pulp that likely generated the first interest in this plant, and some believe the pulp was the main harvest for the Amazonians who first cultivated this plant (Pendell, 2002).

It was the Olmec civilization (1600-350 BCE) of Mesoamerica that developed the use of the seeds and fermentation process that makes cacao both desirable and edible (Pendell, 2002). Once harvested, the cacao seeds are cured and fermented for a number of days. For Criollo varieties, this process takes 1-3 days, while for Forastero varieties 3-5 days are needed (Pendell, 2002). In this process, heat produced by the aerobic fermentation of the juices in the pulp encourages germination, which is essential to the development of the cocoa flavor, after which a second anaerobic fermentation takes place (Pendell, 2002). Once fermented, the cacao seeds are dried and then roasted. During these processes, enzymatic actions are instrumental in further developing the chocolate flavor for which cacao is known (Pendell, 2002). Following this roasting, the cacao seeds are winnowed, loosened husks are separated from the kernels, and the seeds are ready for further processing. In 3000 years, very little has been modified in the processing of cacao, save for the defatting process developed by Dutch chemist Coenraad Johannes Van Houten (Blumberg & Myhrvold, 2020; Pendell, 2002).

Uses

Since the dawn of ancient civilizations in the Americas, cacao has dazzled the human palate and ensorcelled the spirit as both a sacred plant and a functional food. The Latin name Theobroma translates to “food of the gods,” and in both the old world and the new, it has embodied this name (Baharum et al., 2016). The history of the cacao bean is long and complex, spanning hundreds of years and winding through a history of conquest and genocide. It is theorized that the Olmecs, the native civilization of the Gulf of Mexico from 1600-350 BCE, were the original cultivators of cacao, despite the many sources that claim both the Aztecs and Mayans to be the first (Pendell, 2002). As the Olmec civilization predates Aztec and Mayan civilizations and did not have a written language, it’s possible that many refer not to the archeological evidence found in Olmec digs, but rather reference the textual evidence found in Mayan and Aztec cultures (Pendell, 2002). It was in these cultures that cacao as we know it rose to prominence, and we find the first written records of its herbal and ritual use.

Before its climb to popularity in European cultures, cacao was considered a sacred plant gifted to the people by the gods. “Cacao” derives from the Olmec, and later Mayan, word kakaw. With the rise of the Aztec empire, kakaw would evolve into cacahuatl, the etymological root of our current word chocolate (Dillinger et al., 2000). According to Mayan mythology, after humans were created from maize by the goddess Xmucane, they were given the sacred plant cacao from a mythical mountain garden lush with other agricultural plants (Dillinger et al., 2000). Cacao had a similar origin story in Aztec mythology; it was discovered by the god Quetzalcoatl in a mountain full of plant foods (Dillinger et al., 2000). Because of its divine origin, cacao would come to be used in various aspects of Olmec, Mayan, and Aztec culture throughout their history, almost always retaining this tinge of divinity.

According to Dillinger et al. (2000), cacao was regularly used in rituals honoring various Aztec gods where it was anointed with the blood of the attending priests to make it sacred and fit to be offered to the gods. When not in religious service, cacao was served only to the most important members of Aztec society. Cacahuatl was reserved for priests, warriors, and the upper levels of aristocracy and kept away from women and children due to its reputation as both a divine food and an intoxicant (Dillinger et al., 2000). Due to the importance of sacrifice in Aztec culture, cacao was also occasionally served to those intended for human sacrifice to help calm them in a drink called itxpacalatl, which literally translates as “water from the washing of obsidian blades,” which no doubt contained remnants of blood from previous sacrifices (Pendell, 2002). In his exploration on cacao in Pharmako Dynamis, author Dale Pendell (2002) notes that the associations between the heart, blood, and cacao are integral to the story of cacao, often showing mystical attributes as a fortifier of both the soul and body of the consumer. In ancient Nahuatl art, chocolate is poetically referred to as yollotl-eztli, a compound word for “heart-blood.” Pendell (2002) notes that the cacao pod is a metaphor for the heart torn out in sacrifice, an association that would prove to be curiously suggestive of its herbal actions related to the fortification of the cardiovascular system.

Cacao was originally served scented with a myriad of herbs and spices ranging from chili and vanilla to magnolia and masa (corn processed with lye), and occasionally sweetened with honey (Pendell, 2002). It was traditionally prepared by grinding the seeds into a paste, adding water, and foaming the beverage by pouring the resulting liquid between two pots (Pendell, 2002). Cacao was not only limited to ritual use, however. Its worth to the indigenous peoples of Central and South America was so great that it was in fact used as a form of currency by the Aztec peoples across the empire for hundreds of years (Dillinger et al., 2000). It is in this capacity that Europeans first encountered cacao seeds in 1502 when Columbus mistook them for dark almonds in one of his historical accounts (Dillinger et al., 2000).

Early accounts of cacao’s herbal use are quite varied. With applications ranging from encouraging weight gain and clearing infection to strengthening the heart, cacao accumulated a myriad of uses that makes one think early users considered it almost a panacea. Many early Aztec preparations including cacao were lost in the conquest of the Americas, and much of what we do know has been corroborated only by European priests and doctors. One early preparation includes a mixture of cacao and cotton silk tree (Castilla elastica) bark used for infections, while another uses cacao for diarrhea or blood in the urine (Dillinger et al., 2000). In one account from conquistadors in 1552, the flowers of the cacao tree were used to ease fatigue, and another account in 1590 notes a preparation of cacao seeds, maize, and Calliandra anomala (a relative of mimosa) “to alleviate fever and panting of breath and to treat the faint of heart” (as cited in Dillinger et al., 2000, Abstract).

In a study by Dillinger et al. (2000) of manuscripts spanning from the 16th-20th centuries, over 100 uses were recorded for Theobroma cacao, a testament to its import and popularity in herbalism. Of these over 100 uses, there were consistencies to be found in the use of cacao: weight gain, nervous system support, and digestive support specifically in instances of weak stomach and kidneys (Dillinger et al., 2000). The Eclectics felt that cacao was indeed nutritive and diuretic, supporting its earliest uses for weight gain and kidney support (Felter & Lloyd, 1898). They also noted the stimulating effect cacao has on the body, a use that stems back to Aztec use in fortifying warriors before battle (Felter & Lloyd, 1898; Pendell, 2002).

The ancient association between cacao, the heart, and blood shows continued relevance in modern use and medical studies. Modern herbalists like Guido Masé (2018) encourage regular cacao consumption for its many benefits for the cardiovascular system. The plant’s antioxidant qualities have long been touted as one of the major health benefits of cacao, specifically in relation to skin and heart health. Natural cacao is markedly high in antioxidants and flavanols, and also contains minerals used in a number of essential processes in the body (Scapagnini, 2014). Copper and magnesium are present in relatively high amounts compared to other foods, with dark chocolate containing 36 mg magnesium per a 100-kcal serving (Katz et al., 2011). Both of these minerals are necessary cofactors for protein synthesis, energy production, and glucose metabolism, among other functions (Scapagnini et al., 2014). Potassium and iron are also present in chocolate, both being found in higher amounts in dark chocolate than its sweeter milk chocolate counterpart (Katz et al., 2011).

The flavanol content is of particular importance when examining the herbal applications of Theobroma cacao. Dark chocolate contains significantly higher concentrations of flavanols, in some cases 2-3 times the content of its milky counterpart (Katz et al., 2011). It is thought that many of the flavonoids present in red wine are found in cacao at a more concentrated rate, with one cup of hot chocolate containing as many flavonoids as three-fourths of a glass of wine (Pendell, 2002). Much of the current medical research regarding cacao centers around its potent cardiovascular action. Flavanols in cacao have proven to help in the production of nitric oxide in the inner lining of blood vessels, helping to relax the endothelium and support the lowering of blood pressure (Fisher et al., 2003). In a study by Flammer et al. (2012) on patients with congestive heart failure, samples of flavanol-rich chocolate were given and vasodilatation measured. Flavanol-rich chocolate was shown to improve flow-mediated vasodilation, and have a positive effect overall on vascular function with congestive heart failure. Platelet activity was also found to be affected by the consumption of chocolate, with platelet adhesion significantly decreased 2 hours after intake of flavanol-rich chocolate (Flammer et al., 2012).

Research by Hollenberg et al. (2009) on the relatively isolated Kuna people indigenous to the Carribean coast of Panama further corroborated these findings, comparing the cardiovascular health of insular Kuna people to their metropolitan relatives. Those who lived in their isolated home island regularly consumed significantly higher amounts (almost 5 cups daily) of a beverage made with locally grown cacao and presented extremely low incidences of hypertension. When compared to their metropolitan relatives who consumed less cacao, the isolated Kuna individuals were shown to have statistically lower incidences of death due to heart disease, cancer, and diabetes (Hollenberg et al., 2009).

In a study by McCullough et al. (2006), urine samples from island-dwelling Kuna showed strikingly increased flavanol content, likely from this cacao-rich diet, linking their cacao consumption to a healthier lifestyle. The evidence for the positive effect of flavonoids on hypertension is furthered by the fact that the island-dwelling Kuna eat liberal amounts of salt, a practice traditionally associated with hypertension, yet still maintained lower blood pressure than their metropolitan counterparts (McCullough et al., 2006). While a number of other lifestyle variables may be factors, these findings have led researchers to suggest that a high intake of dietary flavanols may have important benefits for cardiovascular health (Hollenberg et al., 2009; McCullough et al., 2006).

The flavanol content in cacao has also been researched for its potential in supporting treatment of type 2 diabetes. According to a review of the most relevant research of the past decade by Ramos et al. (2017), cacao flavanols have been shown to improve glucose homeostasis by decreasing the rate of carbohydrate digestion in the stomach and inhibiting other digestive enzymes to slow the rate of decomposition of carbohydrate-rich molecules. Flavanols, including catechin-rich cacao compounds, may also indirectly affect glucose regulation through these enzymes by affecting lipid digestion (Ramos et al., 2017). These same antioxidant compounds have been shown to protect -pancreatic cells, which are of great import in diabetic patients, thereby helping the body’s natural insulin response by supporting glucose-triggered insulin secretion. Cacao flavanols have also been shown to help with overall inflammation and oxidative stress, which are often exacerbated in diabetes (Ramos et al., 2017).

Theobromine and caffeine, both methylxanthines, are two other molecules that play an important part in the consumption and desirability of chocolate. The stimulating effects of caffeine are well documented, and are likely one of the main reasons cacao has made such an impact on humans throughout history. Psychoactive plants hold a special place in the human heart and have been highly sought after throughout history (Pendell, 2002).

In addition to its stimulating effects, of note are the associations between caffeine intake and a reduction of neurodegeneration, often linked to a reduced risk of Alzheimer’s disease (Martínez-Pinilla et al., 2015). Cacao’s neuroprotective effects are also attributed to its flavanol content, specifically epicatechin (Nehlig, 2013). Theobromine, while molecularly similar to caffeine, produces less of a stimulating effect but comes with its own range of beneficial actions. Recent studies link consumption of theobromine with anti-inflammatory, antitumoral, and beneficial cardiovascular effects, all without the negative side effects associated with overconsumption of caffeine (Martínez-Pinilla et al., 2015). The mechanism of action is likely due to the inhibition of phosphodiesterase and blockade of adenosine receptors, which are both responsible for signaling inflammation response (Martínez-Pinilla et al., 2015). There is some evidence to suggest that theobromine might also make an effective antitussive, but the concentration at which this molecule would need to be taken falls far beyond the applicable intake of cacao (Martínez-Pinilla et al., 2015).

Cacao flavonoids and polyphenols have also been shown to present anti-inflammatory, immunoregulatory, and antioxidant effects on skin cells. Both in vitro and in vivo studies have provided evidence for cacao’s photoprotective effects in response to ultraviolet (UV) radiation (Katz et al., 2011). These antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties have been further studied for their potential to prevent photodamage and wrinkle formation, specifically focusing on the flavonol content and its relation to helping prevent UV damage (Katz et al., 2011). One study found that ingestion of flavanol-rich cacao helped epidermal response to UV light, with negative response to UV radiation decreased by 15% after 6 weeks of controlled testing, and 25% reduction in negative response after 12 weeks of ingestion (Heinrich et al., 2006).

The polyphenol and flavanol content present in cacao also acts as a strong antioxidant that acts on epidermal cells in a number of ways. The ingestion of chocolate has been shown to support the inhibition of lipid peroxidation and help support the skin’s response to oxidative stress by neutralizing free radicals and chelating both Fe2+ and Cu+ metals (Heinrich et al., 2006). Additionally, cacao flavanols have the ability to increase antioxidant action by inducing the action of genomic response to cellular stress including that of the nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2) sequence, which is considered to be play an important role in protecting many tissues against cellular death (Scapagnini et al., 2014).

While often touted as an aphrodisiac, there is little pharmacological evidence to suggest a chemical basis for any direct physiological change in the body due to constituents present in cacao. Most aphrodisiac responses related to chocolate have been linked to dopamine release as a biological reward response (Leonti & Casu, 2018). Dale Pendell (2002) notes that many of the aphrodisiac qualities originally associated with chocolate likely stem from the other intoxicating plants hidden in original recipes for kakaw.

That is not to say that chocolate does not possess other properties that should stop one from serving a pot au chocolat to a lover come Valentine’s Day. Herbalist Guido Masé (2018) notes that from a holistic point of view, cacao’s effect on the cardiovascular system may indirectly support proper sexual function. Consistent use of chocolate helps promote a healthy heart and circulation, which can support microcirculation and increased sensation in erogenous areas of the body (Masé, 2018). Masé (2018) also considers cacao to be an opener of the energetic and emotional heart, which is helpful for promoting intimacy. If we require a scientific response as to why we serve chocolate to our lovers, we can simply say we are protecting our loved ones’ hearts with flavanols, and leave the rest of the mystery to Eros.

When consuming cacao for its health benefits, it is important to consider if your cacao is natural or Dutch processed. Dutch processing is a technique invented in 1828 by Coenraad van Houten to remove a large portion of fat from the bean, making it easier to mix with water and more available to the public; through the process of alkalization both excess fat and bitterness are removed (History Network, 2017). Unfortunately, Dutch-processed cacao contains a heavily reduced flavanol and antioxidant content. In a study done on cacao treated with different alkali levels, the flavanol content of natural, unprocessed cacao was recorded as 34.6 +/- 6.8 mg/g, whereas for the lightly processed cocoas it was 13.8 +/- 7.3 mg/g (Miller et al., 2008). In the medium-processed cacao there was a strikingly low flavanol content of 7.8 +/- 4.0 mg/g, over four times less than the natural cacao (Miller et al., 2008).

Cacao butter, the lipid portion of the cacao plant, will be familiar to many practicing herbalists as an ingredient common to many ungents and ointments. The saturated fat content in cacao butter mostly consists of palmitic acid and stearic acid; the latter exhibits qualities unusual for a saturated fat in that it is not associated with the rise in low-density lipoprotein (LDL) levels in the human body (Katz et al., 2011).

In today’s age, chocolate can be found in myriad forms ranging from heavily processed chocolate bars to raw cacao nibs. Should one wish to try cacao as it was originally consumed, cacao powder might be enjoyed as a hot beverage frothed up with a touch of chili pepper and a hint of vanilla. Go for dark chocolate, as it will be more authentic and healthful, and make sure you source it from a reputable seller.

Adult Dose

There is no established standard dose for cacao (Hoffelt, 2017). Guido Masé (2013) suggests 1-2 tablespoons raw cacao powder prepared as hot chocolate. Cacao is most often consumed as a food stuff, in the form of processed chocolate, and as a functional food, normal guidelines regarding sweets are recommended.

Safety

While generally considered safe for those without a chocolate allergy, it is important to remember that many of the beneficial properties of cacao are directly related to flavanol-rich dark chocolate. Excessive consumption of overly processed chocolate rich in sugars and fat is still contraindicated by most medical experts (Katz et al., 2011). Cacao contains the stimulating compounds caffeine and theobromine and may cause overexcitability and sleep disruption in children and sensitive individuals (“Cocoa,” 2007; Johnson et al., 2012). Large doses of cacao may trigger migraines or cause gastrointestinal disturbances (Chevallier, 2007). Consult with your healthcare provider before consuming cacao during pregnancy. While moderate amounts may be beneficial especially during the first two trimesters (Frost, 2020), evidence suggests potential negative effects on the fetus when consumed during the third trimester (Zielinksy et al., 2014).
