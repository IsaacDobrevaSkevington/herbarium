Horseradish

Native To

Eastern Europe (Willard, 1993)

Geographic Distribution

Horseradish is naturalized in many parts of the world (Willard, 1993).

Botanical Description

Horseradish is a hardy herbaceous perennial with cylindrical taproots that have a brown skin and white flesh and become woody with age (Flora of China, 2008). The plant’s stems commonly reach up to 120 cm tall (Flora of China, 2008). They bear broadly oblong to ovate leaves, typically 20-45 cm long by 5-12 cm wide with wavy margins. The basal leaves have petioles up to 60 cm in length, while the cauline leaves have diminished or lacking petioles as well as lobed leaves that become smaller as they climb the stem (Flora of China, 2008). The plant produces panicles of small white flowers with four egg-shaped petals and six stamens; the flowers almost never produce seeds (Flora of China, 2008; Sampliner & Miller, 2009). Horseradish is a domesticated species believed to be found only in cultivation (where it is tenacious and easily becomes invasive) and is likely derived from either Armoracia macrocarpa or A. sisymbrioides (Sampliner & Miller, 2009).

 

Key Constituents

Glucosinolates (isothiocyanates), myrosinase, flavonoids, vitamins A and C, iron, calcium, magnesium, and potassium (Gafrikova et al., 2014; McBride, 2010; Nguyen et al., 2013).

Sustainability

No known sustainability issues. It is widely cultivated and commonly becomes invasive.

Harvesting Guidelines

Horseradish root is most commonly harvested in the fall, when the pungent and acrid qualities are most potent (Felter & Lloyd, 1898a).

Uses

Horseradish has long been appreciated for culinary and herbal applications, and with its spread over many regions, it has acquired many names. The Ancient Greeks and Romans included it in the genera Thlaspi, Persicon, or Persicum, while Renaissance herbalists considered it a type of Raphanus along with true radish (Courter & Rhodes, 1969). Carl Linnaeus, who established the scientific naming system we still use today to classify plants, put it in the genus Cochlearia, while some of his contemporaries placed it with Nasturtium (Courter & Rhodes, 1969). Horseradish is currently named Armoracia rusticana, with Armoracia deriving from the Celtic ar (“near”), mor (“the sea”), and rich (“against”), meaning “a plant growing near the sea” (Courter & Rhodes, 1969, p. 157).

The common name horseradish may originate from a mistranslation of the German name meerrettich (“sea radish”), as the word meer (“sea”) was likely confused with the similar word mähre (“old horse”) (Courter & Rhodes, 1969).

Horseradish root has been cultivated for over 2000 years, and modern commercial cultivation takes place primarily in Europe and California, Illinois, and Wisconsin as well as Russia, Caucasus, and Asia, exclusively from cuttings (Sampliner & Miller, 2009). Seed production is very rare (Sampliner & Miller, 2009). While horseradish has never been known to occur in the wild, it is most likely native to Eastern Europe (Sampliner & Miller, 2009). The plant favors moist areas such as those that may be found in fields, farms, gardens, roadsides, ditches, and disturbed areas (Sampliner & Miller, 2009).

Horseradish may be most commonly known for its culinary uses. In the Middle Ages, it was only commonly used in German and Danish cooking, because those in other European countries considered it too strong for most people’s constitutions (Kowalchik & Hylton, 1998). By the 1600s it became more commonly used as a condiment in England by country people and laborers (Kowalchik & Hylton, 1998).

Horseradish is a common component in Russian and Eastern European cuisines (Kowalchik & Hylton, 1998). Ethnobotanical studies in these areas have described many uses for the root, including grating it and including it in salads, condiment pastes or sauces, ketchup, or mayonnaise (Kowalchik & Hylton, 1998; Sampliner & Miller, 2009). It is frequently used as a condiment for cooked meats, mixed with vinegar, salt, oil, cream, or yogurt (Sampliner & Miller, 2009). Grated horseradish is mixed with grated red beets, added to potatoes or polenta, or combined with grated apples, salt, sugar, and vinegar; small pieces are also added to soups (Sampliner & Miller, 2009). Horseradish leaves are sometimes stuffed with meat, rice, and vegetables (Sampliner & Miller, 2009). The roots and leaves are also used as an additive for the pickling of various vegetables, which purportedly keeps the vegetables crisp and the juice clear (Sampliner & Miller, 2009). The root is also used as an antiseptic and fermenting agent in the preparation of alcohols, such as tuica (Sampliner & Miller, 2009).

Horseradish is an important plant in the traditions of Judaisim as it is included as one of the five bitter herbs (maror) eaten during modern observances of Passover Seder, though it was likely not one of the plants originally listed in the Mishnah (Pesachim 2:6; 39a) because it is not known to have grown in Israel at that time; rather, it possibly became a replacement for wild lettuce or the other bitter herbs after Jewish people immigrated to colder climates (Zivotofsky, 2006).

The Eclectics and Physiomedicalists, professional herbal practitioners of the late 1800s and early 1900s, described how to obtain the most potent effects from horseradish. The constituents responsible for the acrid and pungent qualities of horseradish are activated by crushing its tissues, and are dissipated by drying and extended heat, such as boiling (Cook, 1869; Remington & Wood, 1918). The active constituents can be extracted by water or alcohol (Remington & Wood, 1918).

Expert pharmacists and editors of one of the last editions of the United States Pharmacopeia to include extensive information on herbal pharmacy wrote, “It is said that if to the powder of the dried root which has become apparently inert, the emulsion of white mustard seed containing myrosin be added, it reacquires its original irritant properties…Hence the powdered root may be added with advantage to mustard in preparing cataplasms, pediluvia [foot baths], etc.” (Remington & Wood, 1918, p. 189). In general, fresh roots were known to induce inflammation or blistering of the skin, while dry roots were not considered to be acrid but were known to have a sharp glandular stimulation effect (Cook, 1869). Potential side effects noted by Eclectic pharmacists Felter & Lloyd (1898a) included faucial and nasal irritation followed by tears, as well as burning epigastric pain, nausea, and vomiting from larger doses.

Horseradish was especially lauded for its ability to promote secretions, particularly in the urinary system (Cook, 1869; Felter & Lloyd, 1898a). Practitioners of that time described their experience of using horseradish to promote diuresis and diaphoresis and to increase fullness and firmness of the pulse for very sluggish conditions, such as in dropsy (edema) (Cook, 1869; Felter & Lloyd, 1898a). It was used only for atonic conditions, such as debility of the body, paralysis, rheumatism, and scurvy (Felter & Lloyd, 1898a). Horseradish was to be avoided in cases of local or arterial excitement and in sensitive people (Cook, 1869).

Compound Spirit of Horseradish, a preparation containing scraped horseradish root, dried bitter orange (Citrus × aurantium) peel, nutmeg (Myristica fragrans), alcohol, and water, was a recipe noted in multiple materia medica as a potent diuretic agent for dropsy attended with debility, “especially in the case of drunkards” (Remington & Wood, 1918, p. 1054). Another formula, called Compound Tincture of Horseradish, contained mustard (Brassica spp.) seeds, juniper (Juniperus spp.) berry, barberry (Berberis vulgaris) bark, orange (Citrus × sinensis) peel, cider, and diluted alcohol and was suggested for dropsy with biliousness (Cook, 1869).

Physiomedicalist William Cook valued horseradish for its ability to increase gastric secretions in cases of atonic, mucoid, or semi-paralyzed conditions of the stomach (Cook, 1869). These indications were affirmed by Felter & Lloyd (1898a), who also suggested its use as a sialogogue.

Compound Syrup of Horseradish, described by Felter & Lloyd (1898b), included horseradish, boneset (Eupatorium perfoliatum), Canada snake root (Asarum canadense), diluted acetic acid, and sugar, and was suggested for “obstinate colds, catarrhs, hoarseness, and chronic irritation of the throat and fauces” (para. 2).

Modern ethnobotanical studies of Eastern Europe and Russia also describe some of the folk uses of horseradish, including making a poultice of the grated root for rheumatism pain (Sampliner & Miller, 2009). In these communities, roots are also added to wine or used in a cloth poultice applied to the head for headaches, or mixed with vinegar, salt, and sugar for lowering blood pressure (Sampliner & Miller, 2009). Other preparations include a paste applied to the throat to aid breathing, and the grated root mixed with honey for coughs/bronchitis or consumed as a morning stimulant to promote health (Sampliner & Miller, 2009).

Horseradish is a key component of fire cider, the widely known apple cider vinegar tonic created and named by herbalist Rosemary Gladstar in the early 1980s (Gladstar, n.d.). Fire cider was subject to a tempestuous legal battle regarding an attempt to trademark the product and name, spurring the Free Fire Cider movement, which has inspired other traditional herbalists to protect the free use of other heritage formulas and their names (Gladstar, n.d.). Fire cider is valued for its warming, aromatic, and antimicrobial qualities, which are employed for stimulating circulation, warming the digestion, breaking fevers, aiding expectoration, clearing the sinuses, and supporting the immune system against colds, the flu, or other respiratory infections (Sinadinos, 2008).

Like their predecessors, modern herbalists also recognize that fresh preparations of horseradish are the most potent (Willard, 1993) and it loses flavor when cooked (McBride, 2010). This is due to the presence of glucosinolates, compounds that are widely found across the cruciferous vegetables. When the tissue of horseradish root is crushed or cut, the glucosinolates are hydrozyled by the enzyme myrosinase into isothiocyanates, which are the compounds responsible for the pungency and many activities of horseradish (Soundararajan & Kim, 2018). However, myrosinase may be degraded by heat or drying, preventing it from converting the glucosinolates into their active forms.

Horseradish is high in vitamins A and C, iron, calcium, magnesium, and potassium and also has antioxidant activity (McBride, 2010).

Contemporary herbalists appreciate horseradish as a stimulant that increases metabolism, circulation, and heat in the body (McBride, 2010). This is related to its diaphoretic action, and it is commonly used in influenza and colds to peak fevers in a manner similar to cayenne (Capsicum annuum) (Hoffmann, 1998).

Horseradish promotes stomach secretions (Hoffmann, 1998; Willard, 1993) and can be used to support digestion of protein (Easley & Horne, 2016). The root has carminative action that can soothe gas and griping pain (Hoffmann, 1998). The stimulating effect on the digestion can act as an aperient for those with constipation due to sluggishness of the digestive organs, such as those with a kapha constitution (Grieve, 1931).

The roots are commonly employed for their anticatarrhal activity, which helps to thin and drain mucus from the sinuses and keeps infection from growing in clogged sinuses (McBride, 2010). The anticatarral action is officially recognized by the German Commission E, a scientific advisory board of the Federal Institute for Drugs and Medical Devices in Germany (American Botanical Council, 1988). Horseradish also provides antibacterial support for established infections of the respiratory tract, possibly due to the glucosinolate sinigrin, which breaks down into the antimicrobial allyl isothiocyanate (Stansbury, 2018b). An in vitro study showed horseradish inhibited Pseudomonas aeruginosa, which causes pneumonia among other human infections, with the isothiocyanate iberin responsible for inhibiting quorum-sensing activity in the bacteria, a common drug-resistance mechanism (Jakobsen et al., 2012).

These actions may benefit sinusitis resulting from hay fever and respiratory allergies as well as colds, for which herbalists suggest using horseradish at the initial onset of symptoms (Stansbury, 2018b). Horseradish is commonly extracted in apple cider vinegar for such uses (Willard, 1993).

Horseradish also addresses mucus in the throat and lungs (Stansbury, 2018b). The counterirritant quality stimulates the lining of the lungs; combining horseradish with nourishing base herbs can work synergistically to thin and expel mucus, which can benefit long-term smokers and those with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) or cystic fibrosis (Stansbury, 2018b).

A syrup made with grated horseradish, honey, and water is traditionally relied upon for hoarseness (Kowalchik & Hylton, 1998). Herbalist and naturopathic physician Dr. Jill Stansbury (2018b) suggests a horseradish cough syrup with ginger (Zingiber officinale), coltsfoot (Tussilago farfara), elderberry (Sambucus canadensis or S. nigra), fennel (Foeniculum vulgare), pleurisy root (Asclepias tuberosa), and lomatium (Lomatium dissectum). She also combines horseradish with garlic (Allium sativum), butterbur (Petasites hybridus), yerba santa (Eriodictyon californicum), lobelia (Lobelia spp.), and grindelia (Grindelia squarrosa) to assist with improving expectoration in cases of cystic fibrosis (Stansbury, 2018b). For those with emphysema, she suggests a combination of horseradish, grindelia (Grindelia squarrosa), lobelia (Lobelia spp.), blue flag (Iris versicolor), and cayenne (Capsicum annuum) to aid expectoration (Stansbury, 2018b).

For respiratory applications, horseradish can also be used externally. Herbalist David Hoffmann (1998) suggests a poultice laid on the chest for bronchitis (with care not to blister the skin).

Herbalists have also included horseradish in formulas as a diuretic for water retention due to renal insufficiency (Stansbury, 2018a). The American physician Dr. Albert Isaiah Coffin, who made popular in Britain the traditions of Thomsonianism and Physiomedicalism, created a famous, simple formula for dropsy which is still used by contemporary herbalists; it includes one ounce of horseradish and a half ounce of crushed black mustard (Brassica nigra) seed. This was steeped in one pint of boiled water for 4 hours, and then 3 tablespoons were consumed, three times per day (Kowalchik & Hylton, 1998; Willard, 1993).

Horseradish may also address urinary pathogens in cystitis, pyelonephritis, and chronic urinary tract infections (UTIs) (Stansbury, 2018a). The Commission E acknowledges horseradish as supportive for UTIs (American Botanical Council, 1988), while studies have shown it to be effective for inhibiting common urinary tract pathogens in vitro (Mickymaray & Al Aboody, 2019). Clinical studies have found  a combination of horseradish and nasturtium (Tropaeolum majus) to be effective for reducing recurrence of chronic UTIs (Albrecht et al., 2007) and catheter-related UTIs (Lau et al., 2018) in humans; this combination was also found to inhibit multi-drug-resistant E. coli in vitro (Mutters et al., 2018). Horseradish may also ease the passage of urinary stones (Stansbury, 2018a). The connective tissue in leaky bladders and weak urinary epithelial lining can be strengthened due to the sulfur content of horseradish (Stansbury, 2018a).

Along with other members of Brassicaceae, horseradish has been shown to have chemopreventive and anticancer effects in vitro due to the isothiocyanates (Stansbury, 2020) and other compounds including monogalactosyl diacylglycerides (Weil et al., 2005). Sulforaphane, benzyl isothiocyanate, and phenethyl isothiocyanate could stimulate apoptosis in human colon carcinoma LS-174 and Caco-2 cells (Bonnesen et al., 2001). A methanolic extract of horseradish root modulated the genotoxic effect of the radiomimetic antibiotic zeocin in human lymphocytes (Hudecova et al., 2012). Another study found that pretreatment with an aqueous extract of horseradish could prevent damage to human DNA in lymphocytes challenged by hydrogen peroxide due to its antioxidant properties; as the researchers explain, the prevention of genotoxicity is considered an important action in cancer prevention (Gafrikova et al., 2014).

Topically, horseradish may be employed to clear blemishes due to its sulfur and calcium content; Terry Willard (1993) suggests combining 4 ounces freshly ground root with 1 quart buttermilk and 4 ounces glycerin, which is rubbed into the skin and then wiped off.

The grated root is also mixed with water and applied topically as a rubefacient compress to ease pain, an action which may be partially due to the high sulfur content. This preparation is used to soothe neuralgia, stiffness, neck pain (Kowalchik & Hylton, 1998), minor muscle aches (American Botanical Council, 1988), and rheumatism (Hoffmann, 1998).

Isothiocyanates in horseradish have demonstrated antimicrobial activity in vitro against oral microorganisms (six strains of facultative anaerobic bacteria: Streptococcus mutans, Streptococcus sobrinus, Lactobacillus casei, Staphylococcus aureus, Enterococcus faecalis, and Aggregatibacter actinomycetemcomitans; one strain of yeast: Candida albicans; and three strains of anaerobic bacteria: Fusobacterium nucleatum, Prevotella nigrescens, and Clostridium perfringens) (Park et al., 2013).

Adult Dose

Tincture:  1-3 mL fresh root (1:2, 95%) 2-4x/day (Easley & Horne, 2016).

Glycerite: 2.5-10 mL fresh root (1:4, 100% glycerin) 2-4x/day (Easley & Horne, 2016).

Topical: Finely grind fresh root and mix with water to form a paste and place on the affected area.

Safety

Avoid consumption of horseradish beyond limited culinary use during pregnancy (Willard, 1993). Excess intake can cause gastrointestinal upset or nausea (Easley & Horne, 2016). Avoid intake if one suffers from stomach ulcers, intestinal ulcers, stomach inflammation, or acid reflux (American Botanical Council, 1988; Brinker, 2010). Horseradish may antagonize antacids (Brinker, 2010). Avoid use with kidney inflammation (Brinker, 2010). Avoid large doses internally in children under age 4 (American Botanical Council, 1988; Brinker, 2010). It may be prudent to avoid use with levothyroxine or in those with thyroid insufficiency due to suppressant effects of isothiocyanates on thyroid function (Brinker, 2010).
