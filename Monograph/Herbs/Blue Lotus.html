Blue Lotus

Nymphaea nouchali var. caerulea (synonyms Nymphaea caerulea, Nymphaea capensis, Nymphaea stellata)

Native To

Eastern and southern Africa, Israel (the northern limit of its range) (Israel Nature and Parks Authority, n.d.; Royal Botanic Gardens Kew [RBGK], n.d).

Geographic Distribution

Blue lotus is naturalized in many places of the world, including parts of eastern South America, Costa Rica, Bangladesh, eastern Australia, Fiji, and New Zealand (RBGK, 2023).

Botanical Description

Blue lotus is an herbaceous perennial water lily that grows from a thick, fleshy, submerged stem (rhizome) that is anchored in the mud or soil at the bottom of the water. The plant stands 9-12 in (23-30 cm) in height and spreads 6-8 ft (1.8-2.4 m) wide, blooming in full sun to part shade from July to September (Missouri Botanical Garden, n.d.). It has day-blooming, star-shaped (stellate) flowers (to 4-6 in (10-15 cm) across), positioned up to 8 in (20 cm) above the water (World Flora Online [WFO], 2025) each with upright, pointed, petals spread flat on the water surface. The fragrant, whitish flowers have 14-20 petals which are white with pale blue tips and acute to obtuse in shape (International Waterlily and Water Gardening Society [IWGS] Plant Database, n.d). The sepals are smooth and green, often featuring a longitudinal blackened blotch and furrow, and their venation is impressed (sunken) (WFO, 2025). 

The leaves of blue lotus (commonly called lily pads), arise on long, flexible stalks (petioles) directly from the rhizome. A characteristic feature of most Nymphaea species is the deep, V-shaped notch or sinus where the petiole attaches to the blade, giving the leaf a heart-shaped (cordate) base. Mature leaves have smooth, entire margins, are ovate to orbicular in shape, and float directly on the water surface. The leaves are covered in a waxy, waterproof cuticle, which helps them float and keeps the stomata (pores for gas exchange), which are primarily located on the upper surface, dry. The top surface of the leaf is green, while the bottom surface is green with red margin and some dark blotches [IWGS], Plant Database, n.d). Blue lotus seeds are ellipsoid and smooth.

Key Constituents

Sterols, saponins, alkaloids, flavonoids, and polyphenols

Flower: Flavonoids, phenolic acids/esters, sterols (including anthocyanins, flavanols, flavones, rhamnoside, galactopyranoside, myricetin, kaempferol, quercetin, naringenin and their derivatives), beta sitosterol, gallic acid (Selvakumari, 2016), nuciferine (Pohl, 2025), apomorphine (purported to be present, however, this is not supported by the scientific literature and will be discussed below).

Leaf: Gallic acid, caffeic acid, kaempferol, catechin, luteolin, epigallocatechin, and epicatechin gallate (Kiranmai et al., 2023).

Rhizome: Polyphenols, flavonoids, rutin, myricetin, gallic acid, catechin, quercetin, ellagic acid, vanillic acid, rosmarinic acid, p-coumaric acid, and sterols (Kiranmai et al., 2023).

Stem: Sodium, calcium, potassium, phosphorus, magnesium, and trace elements like zinc, iron, and copper (Kiranmai et al., 2023).

Sustainability

VULNERABLE PLANT. SEEK SUSTAINABLY WILDCRAFTED/CULTIVATED SOURCES OR ALTERNATIVE PLANTS.

Nymphaea nouchali has been listed in the category of least concern on the International Union for Conservation of Nature and Natural Resources (IUCN) (2025) Red List of Threatened Species. However, the caerulea variety has not been evaluated, although true Nymphaea nouchali var. caerulea is endangered in its native range and wild harvest should be avoided (Cape Town Green Map, 2024; Israel Nature and Parks Authority, n.d.; Pohl, 2025).

Harvesting Guidelines

Harvest the flowers of cultivated blue lotus carefully by hand during the short window of their peak bloom. Take care to cut the flowers just beneath the surface of the water either in the early morning or at night before they have begun to wither. Dry the whole flower in a single layer to preserve its color and appearance.

Uses

Nymphaea is a diverse genus in the water lily family, comprising 50 species worldwide (Qian et al., 2022). There is great variation in the chemical constituents and genetic makeup of the Nymphaea genus (Qian et al., 2022) and species of Nymphaea are known to hybridize (Yakandawala et al., 2017a).

Blue lotus (Nymphaea nouchali var. caerulea) flower is an example of an ancient herb that experienced a surge in popularity in the quest for unregulated psychoactive plants. Blue lotus dried flowers, tea blends, tinctures, powdered extracts, gummies, tablets, essential oils, absolute, smoking mixtures, and vape products are all commercially available, and the internet has seen a profusion of these in recent years. However, the botanical identity of these products is highly suspect, given the complex history and nomenclature of this plant. 

Blue lotus holds profound symbolic importance in ancient Egyptian culture, representing life, eternal existence, and rebirth. This reverence is clearly reflected in ancient texts, for example, in an inscription discovered in the tomb of Pharaoh Ahmose I (who ruled between 1539–1514 BCE) instructing the living to provide offerings. It stated they should bring “lotus flowers, plants, and lotus buds,” along with “every bloom and every herb of sweet odor at its season; cool water and incense,” so that the deceased’s ka (vital essence) would be eternally content (American Botanical Council, 2020).

The spiritual significance of blue lotus stems from its unique natural cycle: the flower breaks the surface of the muddy water in which it grows once annually, blooming for a mere 3 days. This pattern is strongly associated with the Egyptian solar deity Ra, symbolizing the dawn, enlightenment, and the source of wisdom. The flower was often used to guide the departed into the afterlife. Furthermore, it was linked to Nefertem, the Egyptian god of perfume, who is often depicted wearing a blue lotus blossom upon his head.

Other often-cited evidence for the use of blue lotus as a euphoric, psychoactive herb by the ancient Egyptians includes the classic Egyptian work the Ebers Papyrus. The American Botanical Council (2020) cites the following: “The blue lotus is said to contain narcotic alkaloids in both the roots and the flowers, and these narcotic properties were noted as far back as the medicinal Ebers papyrus (1550 BCE) which recounted the herbal knowledge of that time.”

However, based on the text available from the Cyril P. Bryan (1930) English translation of the Ebers Papyrus, the term “lotus” refers to a species of jujube plant (Zizyphus lotus) which was used in medical preparations, not N. nouchali var. caerulea. There is no other mention in this translation of the Ebers Papyrus of any other lotus species. It should be noted that in early Greek and Latin literature, the name “lotus” did not refer to one plant but to a whole group of plants with different uses, locations, and customs (Prigioniero et al., 2020). 

The lotus-eaters story in The Odyssey (Homer, ca. 8th century BCE/1919) is widely used in commercial marketing to suggest the blissful forgetfulness and deep relaxation produced by ingesting or vaping blue lotus. However, the legend of the lotus eaters (lotophages) detailed in Homer’s The Odyssey most likely also refers to Zizyphus lotus (Prigioniero et al., 2020) or a related herb, jujube (Zizyphyus jujuba) seed, widely used in Chinese medicine as a sedative and sleep aid. This conflation lends further support to the idea that the lotus of the “lotus eaters” was in fact not a water lily. 

There is little scientific research to verify that blue lotus is or ever was used as a narcotic plant, and there remains much confusion regarding the correct nomenclature, identification, and phytochemistry of this plant.

Blue lotus has a complicated history, and there are many areas of misinformation and confusion. First, the blue lotus is not a true lotus, but a water lily. The sacred pink lotus that is revered as a symbol of the essential teachings of Buddhism and the national flower of India is Nelumbo nucifera (though formerly Nymphaea nelumbo), not a Nymphaea species, and is a member of the Nelumbonaceae family, not Nymphaeaceae. In addition, blue lotus has had a recent name change. Nymphaea caerulea was first described by Marie Jules Cesar Savigny in 1821, in Description de l’Égypte, a comprehensive study commissioned by Napoleon Bonaparte (Savigny, 1981). The blue lotus was then re-classified as N. nouchali var. caerulea by Bernard Vercourt in 1989 (RBGK, n.d.). There are still differing opinions amongst botanists regarding this new classification, and so the older name Nymphaea caerulea is still widely used, especially in herbalism.

William A. Emboden, an ethnobotanist at California State University, was the first researcher to hypothesize that the blue lotus might have narcotic or psychoactive properties, in part explaining its frequent use as a motif in Egyptian funerary and religious arts (Emboden, 1978, 1981). The work of Emboden suggests that the blue lotus was used by ancient Egyptians in shamanic healing and in magical rites involving various purportedly narcotic or psychoactive plants. However, further review of Emboden’s original texts reveals that the use of blue lotus by the Egyptians for its psychoactive qualities is simply conjecture that has since been repeated in contemporary herbal circles, where blue lotus is used as an entheogen, a calming nervine, to promote lucid dreaming, and even as an aphrodisiac. Emboden (1978, 1981) gives “evidence” in support of his hypothesis, which became the foundation upon which modern herbal use of blue lotus rests, however as discussed below that “evidence” is disproven upon further examination:

  1. Nymphaea caerulea was frequently found in association with the toad Bufo marinus, whose skin contains bufotenine, a potent hallucinogen in Mayan motifs, leading to the theory that perhaps the toad skin, not the blue lotus itself, was the psychoactive element.
  2. Nymphaea ampla rhizomes are used in Mexico as a recreational hallucinogenic drug. This is a distinct species of Nymphaea, not the common blue lotus (Nymphaea caerulea).
  3. J.L. Diaz, an ethnobotanist cited in Emboden’s work, isolated the alkaloid group aporphine, and alkaloids nuciferine and nornuciferine from Nymphaea ampla rhizomes. According to Emboden, aporphine is similar (but not identical) to the synthetic drug apomorphine. Note that aporphine refers to a group of alkaloids, not a specific compound. The Nymphaea genus contains numerous aporphines, including nuciferine, nornuciferine, pronuciferine, and many others (Sun et al., 2024). Emboden likens the aporphine in N. caerulea to apomorphine, but gives no evidence to support this claim. Again, this research applies to a distinct species of Nymphaea, not blue lotus.
  4. Nymphaea caerulea flower motifs are often associated in Egyptian art with mandrake (Mandragora officinarum) fruits. Mandrake contains the psychoactive tropane alkaloids hyoscyamine, atropine, and scopolamine (Duke, n.d.) and possesses narcotic and sleep-inducing qualities which may have been conflated with blue lotus.
  5. In 1910, researchers Goris and Creten isolated nupharine alkaloids, which have sedative effects, from yellow water lily (Nymphaea lutea, syn. Nuphar luteum), not from N. caerulea. 
  6. In published animal studies, Nymphaea alba, not N. caerulea, was the source of powdered rhizomes extracted in alcohol that were used as a sedative. 
  7. In 1912, the explorer Pobeguin states in an obscure French text that a decoction of Nymphaea caerulea and Nymphaea stellata flowers is narcotic (Emboden, 1981). While this statement has never been corroborated, Emboden relies on it to support his hypothesis.
  8. Finally, Emboden (1981) himself states: “Of course, there is no direct evidence that the (blue) water lily was actually made into a narcotic preparation and regarding its ritual use: while such a ceremony has not been found in association with the Egyptian Nymphaea caerulea, we have every reason to believe it existed” (p. 68-69).

Clearly, Emboden laid out a very seductive hypothesis regarding the mysterious and beautiful blue lotus flower, but it remains a hypothesis, nonetheless. 

To complicate matters further, the species of water lily currently sold in commerce as blue lotus flower or Nymphaea caerulea and products said to contain it are probably a hybrid, and not true N. caerulea, the native blue lotus of Egyptian fame. One study found that commercially grown blue lotus sourced from Asian countries such as China, Vietnam, Sri Lanka, and Thailand is, in fact, a hybrid. Evidence for hybridization between the native blue lotus, Nymphaea nouchali var. caerulea and the hybrid Nymphaea x erangae has been verified in Sri Lankan wetlands (Yakandawala et al., 2017a). Nymphaea × erangae, considered a silent invasive species in Sri Lanka, is an exotic violet-colored water lily that has characteristics of both N. nouchali var. caerulea and N. micrantha, a West African species of water lily introduced to Asia. There is concern about the loss of genetic biodiversity in populations of the native Egyptian blue lotus due to this natural hybridization (Yakandawala et al., 2017b).

Apomorphine (also known as apomorphia and sulfomorphide), chemically synthesized by heating morphine with sulfuric acid (Auffret et al., 2018), acts as a psychoactive alkaloid that is often cited as a key constituent of blue lotus. Apomorphine has emetic, sedative, and dopaminergic actions, is one of the oldest anti-Parkinsonian drugs, and is prescribed for motor disorders still today (Auffret et al., 2018). Along with the alkaloid nuciferine, apomorphine is the sought-after euphoric and sedative agent in blue lotus, though new research questions whether blue lotus contains, or has ever contained, apomorphine.

Liam McEvoy, an Egyptology researcher who studies N.nouchali var. caerulea in collaboration with the University of California Berkeley Center for the Science of Psychedelics, has uncovered some intriguing information about the blue lotus products that are commercially available (Pohl, 2025). His lab confirmed that the primary psychoactive compound in the blue lotus flower is in fact the alkaloid nuciferine, not apomorphine. Nuciferine binds to both dopamine and serotonin receptors, exerting depressive activity on the central nervous system in a way that is similar to antipsychotic drugs, as demonstrated in rodent studies (U.S. National Library of Medicine, n.d.). Using mass spectrometry, McEvoy compared his botanically verified flower samples (procured from a confirmed living blue lotus at the UC Berkeley Botanical Garden) to samples of the herb sold online. The results showed that most of the samples of the herb that he obtained via online marketplaces like Etsy did not contain any nuciferine (an important psychoactive constituent marker in blue lotus) and could not possibly be N. nouchali var. caerulea (Pohl, 2025). Analysis showed that the verified N. caerulea sample he procured from the botanical garden contains significantly higher levels of nuciferine than the dried flower petals sold as blue lotus, suggesting that most commercially available products are a different, non-psychoactive species of water lily.

Furthermore, Liam McEvoy challenged the long-held belief that ancient Egyptians simply steeped the blue lotus flower in wine to unlock its psychedelic properties. Because nuciferine is fat-soluble and the flower has a waxy exterior, he hypothesizes that the Egyptians first created an infused oil with the flower (using olive oil or animal fat) to dissolve the alkaloid, then added the infused oil to wine to create a ceremonial beverage (Pohl, 2025). Emboden (1978) also presented this idea in his seminal paper, as blue lotus imagery is often found as a motif on unguent (salve) jars and ancient Egyptians are often depicted with cones of fragrant herbal oils or unguents on their heads, and the blue lotus motif suggests inhalation, as the flowers are seen being held under the nose.

One study that is often cited as evidence of blue lotus’s psychoactivity showed that blue lotus fragrance compounds (absolutes and concretes) from genuine extracts contained a complex mix of compounds, including aliphatic hydrocarbons, aromatic alcohols, fatty acids, phenyl derivatives, diterpenoids, phytosterols, and stigmastanes (Dosoky et al., 2023). However when compared, none of the commercial blue lotus products for topical use analyzed matched the aroma or chemical composition of the authentic Nymphaea caerulea extracts. Importantly, the compounds nuciferine and apomorphine were either absent or present only in trace amounts in the authentic extracts.

Krishna et al. (2025) performed liquid chromatography analysis on locally available (sourced from China) blue lotus (Nymphaea caerulea) flower powder and commercial extracts sold online. Significant variation was found in commercially available blue lotus products regarding the amount of apomorphine present, leading the authors to conclude that commercially available preparations of blue lotus may be spiked with pharmaceutical apomorphine.

In another study, confiscated blue lotus (Nymphaea caerulea) “resin,” together with four commercially available blue lotus products, were analyzed for their chemical content (Poklis et al., 2017). Apomorphine was detected in only two out of five samples, while nuciferine was detected in all five samples. The confiscated resin was determined to contain no apomorphine, but it did contain 4300 ng/g of nuciferine (Poklis et al., 2017), again demonstrating the inconsistency of “blue lotus” products.

In a series of five case studies on military personnel where emergency medical services were sought for altered mental state and behavior, four out of five of the patients had vaped with “blue lotus,” and one had consumed “blue lotus”-infused wine of unknown origin (Schimpf et al., 2023). The authors concluded that “These could all have been intentionally contaminated with apomorphine or other drugs.” Synthetic cannabinoids, including 5F-MDMB-PICA, ADB-BUTINACA, and MDMB-4en-PINACA were found in 90% of samples of “blue lotus” vaping products tested at the United States Army Criminal Investigation Laboratory, Drug Chemistry Division (USACIL) (Seither et al., 2024).

Most of the blue lotus flower that is available commercially is probably a hybrid species. How exactly blue lotus was prepared and used by the ancient Egyptians is still a mystery, but recent research suggests that blue lotus was likely used in an oil-based form, and its active constituent was probably nuciferine. 

Lesser known actions of blue lotus that have been overshadowed by its reputation as a purported narcotic include its cooling capability to quell heat, fever, and discomfort, especially spasm; this, coupled with its analgesic and sedative effects, indicates blue lotus has some use for supporting states of inflammation and pain (Lall et al., 2020; Raja et al., 2010). Blue lotus contains antioxidants and is known for its antimicrobial compounds (Agnihotri et al., 2008; Mukherjee et al., 2023). It has some effect on blood sugar and may be useful in cases of diabetes (Raja et al., 2010). Limited research has also been conducted on its anticancer and antiulcer properties (Chatterjee et al., 2024; Sun et al., 2024). 

While this monograph has focused on the blue lotus flower, the seed, as well as its bitter, acrid rhizome were also used as survival foods. The rhizome is not edible until after leaching, roasting, and boiling (Emboden, 1978).



Adult Dose

Because of the challenges inherent in sourcing the authentic plant, the dosage is uncertain.

Safety

In sourcing blue lotus, caution should be used to find a reputable supplier as this plant is widely adulterated with the synthetic drug apomorphine, although there is no concrete evidence that this plant itself contains any apomorphine (Pohl, 2025; Poklis et al., 2017).

While blue lotus is legal for purchase in the U.S., and is not a controlled substance, it does not have Generally Recognized as Safe (GRAS) status, and is not approved for human consumption as a food, dietary supplement, or a drug by the FDA. It is illegal for members of the military to use (Blue lotus: Prohibited for use, n.d.). It is currently illegal to cultivate, sell, or purchase blue lotus in the state of Louisiana (Louisiana State Legislature, n.d.).

According to Dosoky et al. (2023) regarding topical use of concrete and absolute extracts, the risk of psychoactive effects from a small dose is minimal and safety literature indicates no major safety concerns apart from minor amounts of potentially active compounds.
