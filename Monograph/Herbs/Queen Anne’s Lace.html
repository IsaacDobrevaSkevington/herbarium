Queen Anne’s Lace

Daucus carota L. (sometimes referred to as subspecies carota)

Parts used
The seeds are most often used in herbalism; the flowers, leaves, and roots are sometimes used as food (Shebaby et al., 2015)

Native To

Europe to Northwest Africa and southwestern Asia (Lim 2015; Royal Botanic Gardens Kew [RBGK], n.d.)

Geographic Distribution

Queen Anne’s lace is naturalized in many parts of the world’s temperate biome and is widely distributed in waste areas, roadsides, and disturbed soils throughout North America and Eurasia (Royal Botanic Gardens Kew [RBGK], n.d.).  

Botanical Description

Queen Anne’s lace is a biennial herb from 1-3 feet in height with a hairy stem and finely divided, fern-like foliage that is strongly aromatic, reminiscent of parsley when crushed. The leaves are distributed alternately, with sheaths at the base of the petiole where it meets the hollow stem, and the taproot has finely attenuated lateral rootlets. Flowering occurs from June to August, with a prominent umbel inflorescence composed of many diminutive white florets in a fractal-like pattern, the flower umbel often has a single dark red or purplish center. The inflorescence’s form is dynamic, alternating between concavity when immature, then flattening out and later becoming convex when in full bloom, and again collapsing back to a concave cup shape once the seeds have ripened. 

Key Constituents

Phenylpropanoids, monoterpenes, sesquiterpenes, phenols (including apigenin and quercetin), polyphenols (including flavonoids) (Shebaby et al., 2013; Shebaby et al., 2015)

Sustainability

Queen Anne’s lace is classified as a noxious weed in some states (Iowa, Michigan, Ohio, Washington) (USDA NRCS, 2015)

Harvesting Guidelines

 It is absolutely critical to become expert at distinguishing Queen Anne’s lace from its fatally poisonous lookalike poison hemlock (Conium maculatum) prior to harvesting. Poison hemlock’s leaves and stems are generally smooth, while the leaves and stems of Queen Anne’s lace are typically hairy and slightly rough. Herbalist Linda Patterson suggests one way to remember the distinction, “the queen has hairy legs” (Ellis, 2015), but several other methods of confirming a correct identity should be used in addition to the visual appearance of hair on the stem. See the table in this post with some additional characteristics of Queen Anne’s lace and poison hemlock: https://theherbalacademy.com/blog/yarrow-materia-medica/. 

As Queen Anne’s lace is often found on roadsides and waste areas, be sure to harvest from areas that are uncontaminated. The seeds are ready to harvest in the late summer or early fall after the flowering cycle has completed (usually after August or so). An easy way to harvest is to just snip off the whole umbels and then hang upside down in a paper or mesh bag (to keep the seeds from falling off as they dry) in a dry and shady place. Once they are dried they may be garbled to remove seeds from stems. The roots may be harvested in fall.

Uses

Queen Anne’s lace is often known as wild carrot. It is in fact the same genus and species as the domestic carrot (Daucus carota), however the cultivar is a sub-species (the domestic carrot is subspecies sativa, while wild carrot is sometimes referred to as subspecies carota). It is said that the beneficial properties of both are similar, but that wild carrot is more potent (Healthy Ingredients, 2015). The domestication of wild carrots is thought to have occurred in Afghanistan before the 900s, but the orange color attributes of the common cultivar were not observed prior to the 15th and 16th century in Europe (Lim, 2015).  

A common name for wild carrot was “bird’s nest,” seen in early European herbals such as Grieve’s A Modern Herbal, originally published in 1931. This name could have come about due to the concave appearance of the flower umbel, as it is cup-shaped when immature and again once it has gone to seed, resembling a nest. However, wild carrot could also have earned the name “bird’s nest” because some species of birds actually use the plant to line their nests (Engel, 2003). Scientific investigation of European starlings found that chicks raised in nests lined with wild carrot had less risk of anemia and higher levels of blood hemoglobin, due to reduced infestation of hematophagous mites in nests with wild carrot (Clark & Mason, 1988). Wild carrot is also one of the main host species for the black swallowtail butterfly (Brooks et al., 2004).

Queen Anne’s lace has a history of use as both food and for its health-supporting benefits in many Native American cultures. An infusion of the fresh blossoms when in full bloom has been used in Indigenous practice of the Mohegan and Delaware Indian people to support those with diabetes (Tantaquidgeon, 2007). According to Cherokee Herbal author J.T. Garrett (2003), a member of the Eastern Band of Cherokee from North Carolina, wild carrot was used along with popular tonic ginseng (Panax ginseng) root to promote recovery and restoration post-illness, especially when the sickness was brought about by the weather. Supporting colds in both children in adults has been a common use of wild carrot in Cherokee tradition (Garrett, 2003).

A recent ethnobotanical study among Kashmiri communities in India, a culture with a long history of both Ayurvedic and Unani folk medicine, found that wild carrot was a highly cited plant used among traditional herbal practitioners for liver and circulatory disorders (Kumar et al., 2015) as it tends to be stimulating (Warmund, 2010) to the circulation and also to the kidneys. In Lebanon it is used as food, as well as for its herbal benefits. The leaves may be added to salads, the roots are eaten cooked, and the flower umbel may be fried and eaten (Shebaby et al., 2015). However, there is some evidence that the chemotype, or locally adapted variety, found in Lebanon may have a different phytochemistry from the varieties that grow elsewhere, even though they are classified as the same genus and species (Shebaby et al., 2013).

 Wild carrot seed is a warming carminative, and chewing the seeds can be used to reduce gas or bloating. Russian herbalists use wild carrot seeds to reduce flatulence and expel intestinal worms (Zevin, 1997); indeed, a Cherokee elder recalls wild carrot being part of a formula used as a vermifuge (Garrett,  2003). Wild carrot also has antispasmodic properties, which would also help with intestinal griping and colic (Hoffmann, 2003). Recent research in animal models has uncovered significant anti-inflammatory properties as well in the volatile oils of carrot seed extract, demonstrating significant COX-I and COX-II inhibition when compared to other anti-inflammatory drugs such as ibuprofen, aspirin, naproxen, and Celebrex (Lim, 2015). Wild carrot seed oil was also shown to possess significant hepatoprotective properties in animal trials (Shebaby et al., 2015).

The volatile oils of wild carrot seed are antiseptic and antibacterial, and that in addition to the diuretic properties of the plant make it useful for urinary conditions such as cystitis or prostatitis (Hoffmann, 2003). It is said to be antilithic and deobstruent, helping to dissolve stones or gravel in the kidneys, and was also used in the treatment of gout and rheumatism (Hoffmann, 2003; Zevin, 1997). 

Interestingly, like warming, stimulating fennel seed (another Apiaceae family member), wild carrot seed is also considered to be a galactagogue. Bennett (2014) observes its use in increasing milk production during lactation. 

Carrot seed oil is also used in skin care products for its emollient and antioxidant properties, to increase hydration and protect against free radical damage, and to help reduce scars, irregular skin tone, or wrinkles (Healthy Ingredients, 2015). In vitro studies of wild carrot seed extract have shown significant antioxidant and anticancer properties (Shebaby et al., 2013). Russian herbalists use a poultice of boiled, mashed wild carrot root for skin injuries such as burns and cuts (Zevin, 1997). In animal trials, topical application of a cream containing an extract of wild carrot root was found to significantly increase the speed of wound repair, with a decrease in scarring and no adverse reactions or skin irritation (Patil et al., 2012). Indigenous Cherokee use of Queen Anne’s lace includes using the infusion of the herb topically as a wash to reduce swelling of the skin (Hamel & Chiltoskey, 1975). 

Perhaps the most well-known use of wild carrot seed is as a contraceptive. A very old children’s rhyme may contain a veiled reference to the antifertility properties of Queen Anne’s lace (Stuart, 2004):  

“Queen Anne’s lace 

Saves women’s place.

I love that flower 

To keep my power”

Due to gender discrepancies between authors of antiquity, and the providence of contraception being largely the domain of women, it is likely that much of the knowledge of wild carrot seed as an antifertility agent would have been transmitted orally rather than through written records. However, there is historical evidence that wild carrot seed was utilized as a female contraceptive in Ancient Greece. In the 4th or 5th century B.C.E, Hippocrates made reference to the abortifacient properties of the plant, and Pliny the Elder described it as an emmenagogue (Riddle, 1997). There is also more recent evidence of its contraceptive use in folk tradition of Appalachian women (Riddle, 1997). In a grassroots, non-RCT human study, 30 women collaborated with an herbalist to study the antifertility effects of wild carrot seed for one year (Bennett & Schuler, 2011). The women used either a tincture (15 drops Queen Anne’s lace seed and 15 drops Queen Anne’s lace flower) or dried seed (one teaspoon of seeds, chewed directly), approximately 8 hours after intercourse, and repeated the dose up to three times, 8 hours apart, if they were known to be in their fertile phase. Bennett and Schuler concluded that Queen Anne’s lace seed was 94% effective at impeding conception when used in this way, but noted that if a woman had recently stopped using pharmaceutical birth control, she should allow at least three months for cycles to normalize. Bennett (2014) also notes that there are contraindications and efficacy considerations for its use as a contraceptive agent. In animal research, carrot seed extract was shown to impede implantation of a fertilized embryo or triggering fetus resorption, and demonstrating weak estrogenic activity (Bhatnagar, 1995).

Queen Anne’s lace is also used in North American flower essence therapy, much like a homeopathic preparation. Its flower essence is said to integrate the earthly and cosmic poles to facilitate grounded spiritual insight, “balancing the polar relationship between the physical senses and soul consciousness…for harmonious integration of soul consciousness and sensory awareness” (Kaminski & Katz, N.D.).

Adult Dose

Infusion: To make an infusion of aerial parts, use 1 cup of boiling water with 1 teaspoon of dried herb and infuse for 10 to 15 minutes, to be drunk three times a day. For an infusion of the seeds, use ¹/3 to 1 teaspoon per cup of boiling water, also to be drunk three times a day (Hoffmann, 2003).

Tincture:  1 to 2 ml (1:5 in 25%), three times a day (Hoffmann, 2003). 

Whole seeds: One heaping teaspoonful to be eaten once daily (Bennett, n.d.).

Topical: Carrot seed essential oil should be diluted prior to use.

Safety

Like a few other species in the Apiaceae family, skin contact with fresh Queen Anne’s lace could trigger photosensitivity or contact dermatitis in some individuals. As there are some species of Apiaceae that can be very toxic and even fatal, it’s important to be absolutely certain of plant identification when using species from this plant family. Not for use in pregnancy due to its emmenagogue action.
