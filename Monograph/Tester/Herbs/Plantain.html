Plantain

Plantago spp. (esp. P. lanceolata and P. major)

Common name

Chinese medicine name
Che qian zi (Plantago asiatica) (Scott, 2010)
Parts used
Leaves, roots, and seeds.

Native To

North and South America, Eurasia, Africa (except some areas of Western Africa), Australia

Geographic Distribution

Worldwide, except for the Antarctic continent.

Botanical Description

Plantain is a perennial herb with a basal rosette of 5-7 ovate to elliptical (P. major) or lance-shaped (P. lanceolata) ribbed leaves with distinctly fibrous parallel veins. When picking the leaves, you will see stretchy white threads as the leaf separates from the stem. The overall size of the plant is variable in accord with its growing conditions. A cylindrical spike of inconspicuous hermaphroditic flowers grows up to 15 inches in height, followed by seed capsules. Polysaccharides in the seed coat absorb moisture and become sticky, allowing the seeds to adhere to animals or humans for dispersal.

Key Constituents

Allantoin, mucilage (polysaccharides), iridoid glycosides (including aucubin and catalpol), flavonoids (apigenin, luteolin), alkaloids, terpenoids, phenolic acid derivatives, fatty acids, polysaccharides (Adom et al., 2017), minerals (including calcium,zinc, and potassium), tannins (Blumenthal et al., 2000), vitamins A, C, and K (Geniusz, 2015), and carotenoids (Samuelsen, 2000).

Sustainability

Plantain is a tenacious weedy plant that is in no danger of overharvest.

Harvesting Guidelines

Use care not to harvest in areas where the soil may be contaminated or the plant may have been exposed to herbicides or pesticides. The young leaves may be harvested in spring or early summer as food. The leaves are often harvested and used fresh as needed for first aid throughout the growing season. The leaves may also be harvested and preserved for later use by freezing or drying. To dry, spread the leaves on a screen or brown paper in a warm, dry, shaded place, turning daily until they are crisp. Discard any leaves if they become black or discolored. The immature flower spikes may be harvested early to mid-summer as food, and the mature, brown seeds may be harvested in late summer. To dry the mature seeds, spread the seed heads on a drying screen or brown paper and once they are completely dry, strip the seeds from the stem and store in an airtight container.

Uses

Romeo: Your plantain leaf is excellent for that.
Benvoleo: For what, I pray thee?
Romeo: For your broken shin.

– William Shakespeare, Romeo and Juliet, Act I, Scene II

This seemingly lowly little weed, omnipresent in disturbed and compacted soils around the world, actually has a surprisingly illustrious history, from Shakespeare (in both Romeo and Juliet and Love’s Labour’s Lost) to a place among the nine holy herbs of the Anglo-Saxons, rulers of the British Isles in the 5th century, who called it “the mother of worts (plants)” (Blair, 2014). In the 1st century AD, Greek Physician Dioscorides included plantain for its wound easing and anti-infective properties in his treatise De Materia Medica. Nicolas Culpeper (1652) said of plantain that it is in the command of Venus with antipathy to Mars. That is to say that plantain is cooling and demulcent, a yin tonic which soothes inflammation, infection, or injuries of the skin, lungs, urinary, and digestive systems.

Plantain has followed on the heels of colonization around the world, earning common names such as Englishman’s foot or white man’s footsteps, owing to its broad dispersal and naturalization concurrent with colonization, as well as its resilient ability to thrive despite being downtrodden. There are more than 250 species within the Plantago genus around the world, and all are said to be edible, or safe for consumption if not always palatable (Blair, 2014; Scott, 2010; Blumenthal et al., 2000). The common name “plantain” may refer to several species, particularly Plantain major and Plantain lanceolata, which are widely naturalized throughout North America. Many herbalists will use these two species interchangeably, for the most part, although some say P. major may be a stronger diuretic (Mars, 2014).

Plantain is also related to psyllium (Plantago ovata), which is a soluble fiber used in bowel-regulating products such as Metamucil. The seeds of plantain may be used similarly, as a bulk-forming, mucilaginous laxative, taken ground or whole in water. Be sure to drink plenty of additional water with this. The seeds can also be used as a thickener or binding agent in recipes, and could be substituted for eggs in baked goods (Blair, 2014). The demulcent and anti-inflammatory properties of both the seed and the leaves can help soothe digestive issues related to irritated mucous membranes in the gastrointestinal tract such as ulcerative colitis, irritable bowel syndrome, and constipation. Recent studies show that plantain may also have prebiotic benefits (Nemereshina et al., 2015).

Plantain has been referred to by a Cherokee Elder as “Indian Bandid,” because it is an effective herb for bites, stings, cuts, and scrapes, and it helps to draw out slivers, splinters, or stingers (Bruton-Seal, 2009; Garrett, 2003). Plantain may also be used to soothe and cool the pain, itching, or burning symptoms of poison ivy, nettles, or sunburn. All of these indications, combined with the herb’s overall safety and ease of identification, make plantain a great herb for children to learn about. In-the-field preparation of a “spit poultice” for direct topical application for any of the above indications is a plant preparation that is very accessible for children (or anyone), and the enzymes in one’s own saliva may also add to the beneficial effect (Blair, 2014). You can also use a leaf tucked into a sock for hotspots, blisters, or even plantar fasciitis. 

Plantain’s historical reputation for repairing wounds has recently been corroborated in laboratory and animal studies which have shown that wounds aided with plantain showed faster improvement times and greater wound tensile strength due to collagen growth (; Cardoso et al., 2021; Kováč et al, 2015; Zhakipbekov et al., 2023; Zubair et al., 2015). Recent lab studies have validated plantain’s anti-inflammatory actions, and also suggest that plantain may have the potential to attenuate hepatotoxicity (Hussan et al., 2015). Plantain’s anti-inflammatory and astringent properties are applicable to soothe and tone other chronic or acute skin conditions such as eczema, rosacea, shingles, varicose veins, and hemorrhoids (Bruton-Seal, 2009). In vitro studies have also shown plantain has antiviral, immunomodulatory, and anticarcinoma properties (Chiang et al., 2003). The root is sometimes used like comfrey (Symphytum officinale) for its bone-knitting properties (Blair, 2014).

Plantain has also been shown to possess antimicrobial properties that are applicable in helping abscesses, ulcers, dental issues, mastitis, and, historically, even blood poisoning (Bruton-Seal, 2009). One clinical trial found that plantain leaf gel used topically as a wound dressing accelerated the repair of diabetic foot and pressure ulcers (Ghanadian et al., 2024). Its antibacterial properties are destroyed by heat, however (Blumenthal et al., 2000). “[Plantain’s] medicine is like a lance that penetrates and opens the wound to draw out the poison” (Scott, 2010). Plantain is used in Ghana to maintain oral hygiene (Addo-Yobo, 1991), and its anticariogenic properties have been verified by lab study (Ferrazzano et al., 2015). It is also used to ease tooth pain and support healthy gums, and research has confirmed that plantain inhibits oral pathogens and plaque colonizers (Pasupuleti et al., 2023). Some herbalists suggest that plantain can be used as an emergency water purifier owing to its inhibition of E. coli, Streptococcus pneumonia, and Giardia, as well as its anthelmintic properties and soothing effect on the digestive tract (Blair, 2014). Plantain may also be used in cases of candida overgrowth such as thrush (Grieve, 1982). Research demonstrates plantain’s efficacy against Borrelia burgdorferi, which causes Lyme disease (Laanet et al., 2024).

Plantain is a powerful ally for lung health, helping to soothe inflamed and irritated airways. As an antitussive expectorant, plantain can be used as a tea, succus, or syrup for coughs, congestion, bronchitis, or laryngitis, and is useful when there is stagnant or stuck phlegm. Clinical studies have shown plantain’s ability to reduce cough and wheezing and signs of asthma and bronchitis, though further research is necessary (Boskabadi et al., 2025). Plantain may also be used as an aid to quit smoking–a tea or tincture made from the leaves curbs the desire to smoke and helps clean the lungs (Gray, 2011). Plantain is sometimes used to address allergies or hay fever due to its antihistamine effects (Bruton-Seal, 2009).

Plantain is also valued for its broadly useful effects on the urinary system. Its diuretic, anti-inflammatory, antimicrobial, and detoxifying properties may help chelate and eliminate toxins via the urinary system, and are also useful in cases of cystitis, prostate inflammation, or bladder infections. J.T. Garrett (2003) notes its contemporary use for urinary tract infections. The constituent aucubin increases uric acid excretion, and may therefore also be useful in addressing gout (Gray, 2011). Some suggest it may also help children overcome nocturesis or bedwetting. 

Plantain is an important ethnobotanical herb throughout most of the world where it is native–every continent except for Antarctica. Some Indigenous Cherokee uses are the poultice of the herb for headache, burn, wounds, stings, or ulcers or the juice for sore and inflamed eyes (Hamel & Chiltoskey, 1975). Hamel and Chiltoskey note that in Cherokee tradition, infusion of plantain leaf is given to strengthen a baby who is learning to walk, to aid gastrointestinal or urinary distress, and to help support the body after a snakebite. According to Delaware Indian practice, plantain is used to support the female reproductive system (Tantaquidgeon, 1942). Cherokee ethnobotanist J.T. Garrett (2003) notes that plantain is given to cleanse a baby’s bowels or act as a skin wash. A blood and spirit building tonic has traditionally been made combining plantain with rattlesnake plantain (Goodyera pubescens/repens), alder (Alnus spp.), wild cherry (Prunus serotina), wild ginger (Asarum canadense), and yellowroot (Xanthorrhiza simplicissima) (Garrett, 2003). Among its other uses, plantain is valued in Cherokee culture for addressing swelling, rheumatic pain, and cuts and wounds of all kinds; it is used in combination with horehound (Marrubium vulgare) to aid both dogs and humans who experience snakebite (Garrett, 2003).

13th century Danish botanical and medical writer Hapestreng wrote that plantain boiled with butter and consumed could fix every organ in the body, and used a mixture of plantain and honey to aid wounds (Samuelsen, 2000). In traditional Persian medicine, plantain enjoys a long history of use since being featured in pharmacopoeias such as The Canon of Medicine and Makhzan-Al-Advia from the 10th-18th centuries C.E., and is valued for its wound repair, antihemorrhagic, laxative, and antipyretic properties, among others (Najafian et al., 2018).

Various species of Plantago are used as food in traditional cultures around the world.  Plantain is a good source of vitamin C and carotenoids, as well as vitamin K (Samuelsen, 2000) and zinc. Fresh leaves were reported to contain 19 mg ascorbic acid and 6 mg beta-carotene per 100 grams, and dry leaves to contain 37 mg of phylloquinone (vitamin K1) per gram (Samuelson, 2000). The vitamin K content likely explains plantain’s topical use as a styptic or hemostatic agent. The young leaves may be added to salads or used as cooked green. The young flower stalks are also sometimes sautéed and eaten, and the seeds may be eaten from mature seed stalks as well, either raw, ground into flour and baked, or prepared as a beverage or porridge (Scott, 2010; Blair, 2014).

As an aid to fasting or internal cleanses, a chlorophyll-rich green juice made with plantain leaves can help to alkalize the body’s pH and eliminate toxins released during the fast, also helping to keep the bowels moving (Blair, 2014). Plantain similarly detoxifies or remediates poor soils. In material usage, plantain fibers can be worked for weaving or cordage (Blair, 2014). Flower essence preparations with plantain help to clear negative thought patterns, encouraging enjoyment of life and supporting strength in groundedness (Mars, 2014).



Adult Dose

Infusion: 5-10 g dried leaf/day divided in 1-3 doses (Tierra, 1998).

Tincture:  3-6 mL (1:2, 30%), 3x/day (Winston & Kuhn, 2008).

Topical: The fresh leaves may be macerated by chewing or crushing and applied directly to the skin. A cold infusion can be prepared to use as a rinse, gargle, compress, nasal spray, or wash (using distilled water if intended for the eye or nose area), preserving the heat-sensitive antimicrobial properties of plantain. Soak 1.4 g cut herb in 150 ml cold water for 1 to 2 hours, stirring often, and apply 3-4x/day (Blumenthal et al., 2000).

Succus: Juice the fresh leaves and blend with an equal amount of honey.  Store in a cool place and take 1 tsp as needed for coughs, or 1-3 tsp/day for stomach ulcers (Bruton-Seal, 2009).

Seeds: The seeds and husks can be used whole, or ground in a coffee grinder before use. Use 1 tsp, 1-3x/day (Bruton-Seal, 2009).

Safety

Plantain is generally recognized as safe, although caution may be advisable with diuretic medications. Due to its diuretic and chelating properties, when taken internally plantain could speed the elimination of some prescription medications, such as lithium (Scott, 2010). Plantain pollen can elicit an allergic response in sensitive individuals (such as those sensitive to psyllium), and in rare cases plantain could trigger contact dermatitis.

Humphrey (2003) gives plantain leaf a safety rating of ‘B’ in The Nursing Mother’s Herbal, noting its galactogogue properties and that it may cause adverse effects if misused, and may not be appropriate for self-use by some individuals or dyads.
