Teasel

Dipsacus spp.

Parts used
Root (Buhner, 2012) and dried flowering herb (Moore, 1993).

Native To

The genus is native to Europe, the Mediterranean area, tropical Africa (Encyclopaedia Britannica, 2019), and Asia (Wood, 2008).

Geographic Distribution

Dipsacus spp. has been spread globally by commerce, European colonization, and general interest (Rector et al., 2006). D. fullonum, native to North Africa, Europe, and West Asia, has been introduced to the United States, Canada, Bolivia, Argentina, Ecuador, Uruguay, New Zealand, and Australia (CABI, 2019a). D. laciniatus is native to Europe and Asia and has been dispersed across the United States and Canada (CABI, 2019b); it is classified as extinct in Palestine, however (Royal Botanic Gardens Kew [RBGK], n.d.a.). D. japonicus is native to China, Mongolia, Japan, and Korea (RBGK, n.d.b.) while D. asper’s native territory covers China, Bangladesh, Myanmar, and Tibet (RBGK, n.d.c.). 

D. strigosus has stayed closer to its Indigenous region within Europe (CABI, 2019c). Furthering its expansive geographical distribution, teasel can grow in a wide range of conditions from pristine habitats to heavily disturbed areas and from dry to moderately wet climates (Chicago Living Corridors [CLC], 2011).

Botanical Description

Dipsacus spp. are biennial plants, having a growth cycle that lasts 2 years. In the first year, a basal rosette is formed with most of the growth happening belowground in the root, which can grow up to 2 feet long and 1 inch wide at the crown (CLC, 2011). At this growth stage, it looks similar to broadleaf dock (Rumex obtusifolius), common burdock (Articum minus), and thistles (USDA Forest Service, 2017). The rosette leaves of D. fullonum are opposite, lanceolate in shape, and have a wrinkled appearance with entire or rounded irregularly toothed edges (USDA Forest Service, 2017). In D.laciniatus, these leaves are similar in shape but deeply lobed, making it easy to distinguish these 2 species from each other (Reeves, 2010). Both species present a prominent white vein in the center of each hairless leaf (USDA Forest Service, 2017) and have a fused leaf base that collects water with every rainfall. This cup-like base influenced the genus name Dipsacus which is derived from dipsa, the Greek word for thirst (Reeves, 2010).  

Once enough nutrients are gathered from the environment to support further growth (generally during summer months in the second year), a tall prickly stalk shoots up to 6 feet in the air and develops a characteristic cone-shaped (ovoid) flower head that can produce more than 2,000 seeds (CLC, 2011). These seeds are protected by the epicalyx, another distinctive botanical feature of this plant. The epicalyx is a modification of bracts that form an additional whorl around the calyx of a single flower (Carlson et al., 2009), often extending above the flower head (WSU, 2013). The flowers are small, tube-shaped, and densely packed, blooming in a unique circular pattern around the flower head (WSU, 2013). Each flower generally lives for 1 day (USDA Forest Service, 2017) and while in bloom, D. laciniatus has white flowers from July to September and D. fullonum has purple flowers from June to October (CLC, 2011).

Key Constituents

Root: Iridoids, polyphenols (Oszmiański et al., 2020), triterpenoid saponins (Li et al., 2017), bitters, glucosides, inulin, and mineral salts (Wood, 2008).

Aerial parts: Iridoids, polyphenols (Oszmiański et al., 2020), and volatile oils (Skala & Szopa, 2023).

Sustainability

Dipsacus spp. can be a weedy and invasive plant. In Illinois, all members of the Dipsacus genus are listed as exotic weeds (Illinois Exotic Weed Act, n.d.), and in Iowa this plant genus is considered a secondary noxious weed (The Iowa Legislature, 2019). In addition to these states, D. fullonum is considered a noxious weed in Colorado, Missouri, and New Mexico, and D. laciniatus is listed as such for most of the same states (Colorado and Missouri) plus Oregon (USDA Natural Resources Conservation Service, n.d.). D. spp. are reported by 11 other states as invasive, with each state having established its own requirements for regulating, controlling, and eliminating these plants; in total, various teasel species can be found growing in 43 states (Rector et al., 2006). Additionally, teasels have been documented as affecting natural areas in four United States national parks, leading to the start of biological control programs sponsored by the government to control the spread of these plants (Rector et al., 2006). For managing smaller growth patches in your local areas, removing the flowering heads before they go to seed and then placing them in a bag for disposal (the blooms can still release seed after they have been cut) or burning them will help prevent this plant from self-seeding (CLC, 2011).

Harvesting Guidelines

Teasel is a biennial, meaning it has a growth cycle of 2 years. The first year is spent developing its basal rosette (Washington State University [WSU], 2013) and accumulating nutrients and energy from the earth to establish its sizable taproot (CLC, 2011). In its second year, teasel puts more of its energy into developing a tall flowering stalk (WSU, 2013). Thus, the preferred time to harvest this plant’s root is during the first year throughout the autumn and winter months or in the spring of the second year, before the stalk has sprouted (Buhner, 2012).

Uses

The teasel subfamily (Caprifoliaceae) has inhabited areas across Europe, Asia, and Africa since before the European colonization of the Americas (Wood, 2008). The specific genus of Dipsacus comprises approximately 15 species (Bell & McClintock, 2012), although not all of these have a history of use in herbalism (Wood, 2008). 

Teasel flower heads were originally cultivated for use in textiles, specifically for pulling apart or “teasing” wool fibers, as far back as 12th century France and possibly even the Roman Empire (27 BCE – 476 CE). This ethnobotanical use led to the development of the common name teasel (Rector et al., 2006). One species, D. fullonum, has the common name of fuller’s teasel which signifies that it was used in fulling, a step in clothmaking that shrinks and thickens wool fibers for a smoother and tightly finished fabric (CABI, 2019a); D. sylvestris was used historically by the Navajo Indian people to card wool (Elmore, 1944).

Dipsacus spp. have also been used by gardeners for aesthetic purposes and to attract pollinators. These plants are popularly displayed in dried flower arrangements and their seeds have been used in bird seed mixtures. All of these utilizations have, in their own way, helped to contribute to the widespread growth of teasel across the globe and in establishing its invasive status in some regions (Rector et al., 2006).  

Despite its wide growth range, teasel isn’t a commonly used plant among Western herbalists, and there is little information regarding its use in this form of practice. However, in Chinese medicine traditions, Dipsacus japonicus (Asian teasel) has a long history of being used to support the kidney essence and liver blood, which are seen as the source of bones and source of tendons, respectively (Wood, 2008). The Chinese name, xu duan, literally translates to “restore what is broken” and testifies to this plant’s importance in strengthening the structure of the body in chronic conditions (Wood, 1998).

Based on this knowledge from Chinese medicine, practitioners in the West started to utilize local Dipsacus species for similar indications as the Asian species (Wood, 2008). While there were many to choose from, William Salmon, a 17th- and 18th-century English empiric doctor and a writer of medical texts, states that two species were primarily used (in addition to D. japonica): D. sativus (garden teasel) and D. sylvestris (wild teasel, now considered to be a synonym of D. fullonum) (as cited in Medicine Traditions, n.d.).

D. fullonum is widely naturalized in North and South America, and was introduced into practice in North America by William LeSassier, herbalist and acupuncturist, who used it in cases of extreme and drastic injuries to the tendons and joints. Herbalist Matthew Wood (2008) took this application a step further and applied it to Lyme disease, stating, “when I was searching for a remedy for Lyme disease I remembered that teasel was said to act on the kidney jing. Syphilis is viewed as an attack on the kidney essence in traditional medicine (East or West), and since Lyme disease is due to a syphilitic spirochete, I surmised that teasel would be a possible remedy for this dreadful disease” (p. 238).

The concept of kidney essence, also known as kidney jing, in Chinese medicine is explained by Wang et al. (2016) as the producer of kidney qi, and kidney qi then creates kidney yang and kidney yin. These two principles of yang and yin create foundational balance in the body. Kidney yang builds up and initiates growth, while kidney yin nourishes and hydrates the tissues and organs. When rich in kidney essence, bone marrow is nourished and bone structure is strengthened. If deficient in kidney essence, there can be an increased risk of bone fractures and osteoporosis (Wang et al., 2016). In a Taiwanese pharmaco-epidemiological study by Chen et al. (2014) assessing prevalence of herbs used in Chinese medicine for osteoarthritis, teasel root (xu duan) was included in the most commonly used formula, which combined it with gu sui pu (Drynaria fortunei, more commonly known as kunze), and an additional formula known as du huo ji sheng tang. Teasel was also the second most commonly used single herb (Chen et al., 2014). 

Additionally, an animal study showed that decoctions of teasel root improved trabecular bone formation by inhibiting receptor activator of nuclear factor-kappa B ligand, which plays a role in bone remodeling (Liu et al., 2012). Another study found teasel root extract counteracted the loss of bone mass, an indicator of its potential osteoprotective effects (Liu et al., 2009). This led researchers to conclude that teasel root may be useful for postmenopausal osteoporosis (Liu et al., 2009; Liu et al., 2012), although clinical studies are needed to confirm these effects in humans.

In the philosophy of Chinese medicine, the liver governs the tendons, which give the body the ability to move. Dysfunction or stagnation of liver blood results in impairment of body movements due to a deficiency in adequate nourishment and blood flow to the tendons, which connect the muscles to the bones (Ma et al., 2016). In Korean folk medicine, teasel is used to support both bones and joints, and is included in the Korean Herbal Pharmacopoeia and the Chinese Pharmacopoeia as such (Skala & Szopa, 2023). Addressing uterine bleeding and severe hematoma is another traditional use of teasel in Chinese medicine (Tao et al., 2020). 

Matthew Wood (1998) speaks of teasel’s use in promoting blood circulation and applies this herb as an analgesic internally or externally to remove discomfort and rigidity in areas that have experienced trauma, including bruises, and/or with malnourished muscles and tendons. He states that it “is excellent for chronic inflammation of the muscles, with limitation of movement and great pain” (Wood, 1998, pp. 234-235). Due to these specific affinities, he also indicates it for cases of excessive menstrual bleeding (menorrhagia) or abnormal uterine bleeding, and as a tonic that calms a restless fetus and forestalls miscarriage (Wood, 1998). In addition to acting as a circulatory stimulant, teasel is a diuretic and diaphoretic and aids the body in eliminating waste products (Chiej, 1984). 

Another animal study conducted by Li et al. (2017) focused on the saponins found in teasel root and showed that, in a concentration-dependent manner, they lessened the signalling in pathways of apoptosis in chondrocytes by increasing the cellular response to B-cell lymphoma 2 (Bcl-2), an apoptosis suppressor gene. Dipsacus saponins also decreased the cells’ responses to protease enzymes (caspase-3 and caspase-9) involved in programmed cell death and downregulated expression of Bcl-2-associated X-protein (BAX), which alters cellular mitochondria to induce cell death when activated. Chondrocytes are essential in helping to support and preserve joint function, thus inhibiting their cell death provides further support for teasel’s potential for osteoarthritis support (Li et al., 2017) and presents a mechanism of action that reinforces the traditional use of this herb.

Herbalist Stephen Buhner (2012) uses teasel root tincture in combination with extracts of echinacea (Echinacea angustifolia) root, sida (Sida acuta) leaf, isatis (Isatis tinctoria) root, ginger (Zingiber officinale) rhizome (as an extract and fresh juice tea), and red root (Ceanothus americanus) root for septic arthritis caused by a Haemophilus infection. Buhner also states that teasel is the most effective for helping with arthritic symptoms from Lyme disease, another bacterial-induced illness, suggesting that its actions extend further to not only strengthen bones and the connective tissue, but that it also possesses antibacterial properties (Buhner, 2011b).

Clinically, Lyme disease symptoms are due to inflammation presenting in various parts of the body including the skin, nervous system, joints, muscles, organs (Goc & Rath, 2016), tendons, connective tissue, and bones (Wood, 2008). D. fullonum influences and addresses these same areas which have been demonstrated both in Matthew Wood’s (2008) practice and in research, with the most specific study being conducted at the University of Leipzig, Germany. In this in vitro study, lipophilic extracts (using ethyl acetate and dichloromethane) of the fresh root of Dipsacus fullonum showed antibacterial activity against the growth of Borrelia burgdorferi over the course of an 8-day period at a concentration of 2 mg/mL (Liebold et al., 2008). 

Materializing as the most common vector-borne disease in the United States and Europe, with 500,000 reported cases per year in Europe and 300,000 in the United States, Lyme disease is a pertinent and important health concern (Goc & Rath, 2016). Conventionally, antibiotics are the first line of treatment that oftentimes result in persistence of the bacteria if not used early enough or have side effects that include joint pain, muscle aches, destruction of bacterial diversity in the gut, and/or fatigue. This approach results in more health concerns surrounding this disease and places more significance in researching antibacterial potential from natural sources like plants (Goc & Rath, 2016). 

D. fullonum is one of these plants, considered a “specific” that is distinctly indicated for Lyme disease (Wood, 2008) and is seen as being well tolerated overall (Goc & Rath, 2016). Yet, Matthew Wood suggests that the dosage for a tincture preparation of teasel be kept small, since in his clinical experience those with chronic Lyme can become irritated by it after a couple of weeks; he suggests 1-3 drops up to 3 times a day for no longer than 6 weeks (Wood, 2008). However, Stephen Buhner (2011a) prefers a dose of 1 teaspoon of teasel tincture 3 times per day. Buhner generally starts off at a lower 3-drop dosage, but if the client is not showing any improvement, he increases the dose for about a month. He states that while some people do respond to lower doses of teasel tincture, many people do not, and higher doses are oftentimes necessary to address their arthritic inflammation symptoms (Buhner, 2011a). Overall, he has a broad suggested range for this tincture, stating that an amount of 1-30 drops can be used up to 3 times a day depending on the dose strategy of the herbalist (Buhner, 2012). Easley and Horne (2016) note that while D. asper is very useful in managing the effects of Lyme disease, particularly the pain and discomfort, it does not eliminate Lyme completely. 

The root of D. fullonum can also be prepared as an infusion or decoction and serves as a bitter tonic. Sir John Hill, an 18th-century English herbalist and botanist, used this bitter concoction as a stomachic for stimulating one’s appetite and strengthening one’s stomach. He also used this preparation in cases of jaundice and liver blockages (as cited in Wood, 2008). Herbalist Michael Moore (1993) also used an infusion, using the flowering herb instead of the root. He utilized this part of the plant for indigestion especially when expressed by constipation and dry skin, suggesting that it be taken approximately 20-30 minutes before meals and before bed (Moore, 1993).

Galen, a physician, surgeon, and philosopher in the Roman Empire, stated that teasel roots are “dry in the second degree” (as cited in Culpeper, 1653/1995, p. 314) and Nicholas Culpeper (1653/1995), an English herbalist and physician, agreed with this and imagined that anyone who had used this plant found it energetically dry and cold. In keeping with these energetics along with its antibacterial and anti-inflammatory properties, this plant was used externally for different skin conditions. The Greek physician Dioscorides boiled the root of fuller’s teasel in wine until it thickened to be applied for intergluteal cleft irritations and to aid in taking away warts, boils, or other growths on the skin (as cited in Culpeper, 1653/1995). Culpeper (1653/1995) also stated that he used this plant in combination with lime (calcium oxide) powder and soap to create a topical preparation for cysts. 

Dipsacus has proven its abilities through historical use, research, and in clinical herbal practice. Despite it being found almost everywhere, teasel is often overlooked as a beneficial plant and is more widely known as being a bothersome and irritating weed; but in the words of Stephen Buhner (2012), “invasive plants [are] earth’s way of insisting we notice her medicine” (p. 89). Even the dew that collects in the leaves of teasel has been considered to have beneficial powers on the skin and for general wellbeing (Larkin, 2009). The prickly top of the teasel plant has been compared to a spiny hedgehog and according to the Doctrine of Signatures, its spiky character is symbolic of its fiercely effective defense against all manner of ills, from boils to bites (Larkin, 2009). 

 

Adult Dose

Tincture: 1-30 drops (1:3, 45%) 3x/day (Buhner, 2012); when addressing Lyme disease, higher doses of up to 1 tsp 3x/day may be necessary (Buhner, 2011a). Wood (2008) prefers smaller doses of 1-3 drops 1-3x/day.

Infusion: 2-4 fl oz (made with 1 oz dried herb in 32 fl oz water) up to 4x/day, 20-30 minutes before meals and before going to bed (Moore, 1993).

Decoction: 4-6 fl oz (made with 1 oz dried root in 32 fl oz water) up to 3x/day (Kloos, 2017).

Safety

Apart from some reports of allergic reactions, there are no known safety issues or precautions with this plant genus (Gardner & McGuffin, 2013).

Teasel use during pregnancy is debated. Generally speaking, consult a qualified healthcare practitioner before using herbal products, especially if you are pregnant, nursing, or on any medications. Large amounts of D. asper may be contraindicated during pregnancy due to adverse effects on the mother and fetus (Tao et al., 2020). However, in The Nursing Mother’s Herbal, Humphrey (2003) gives D. asper and D. japonicus root a safety rating of “A” for having no contraindications, side effects, or other concerns related to pregnancy.
