Botanical Description

Lavender (Lavandula spp.) is a familiar and beloved addition to many gardens, boasting a combination of shrubby and herbaceous features. This compact perennial plant features a spindly and delicate form, with a main stem that appears shrub-like, rigid, and woody. However, it divides out into numerous thin and dainty herbaceous branches that bolt upright, are typically covered in fine hairs, and showcase the characteristic square stem found in the Lamiaceae family (Cook, 1869). The woody main stem displays a light brown bark, while the herbaceous branches boast pale green leaves.

Lavender is a name that encompasses a fascinating array of species, each exhibiting distinctive flower and leaf shapes. Four of the most familiar lavenders are: Lavandula latifolia, a type found in the Mediterranean; Lavandula angustifolia, commonly known as English lavender; Lavandula stoechas, occasionally known as French lavender; and Lavandula x intermedia, which results from the sterile hybridization between L. latifolia and L. angustifolia; all in all, there are more than 30 distinct species, along with numerous hybrids and cultivated varieties (Koulivand et al., 2013). The overall size of the plant varies across species, with some reaching heights of up to 6 feet (Felter & Lloyd, 1898). The whole plant exudes a captivating fragrance, its summer blossoms becoming a delightful attraction for bees.

The gray-green leaves of this versatile plant display diverse forms, ranging from simple to pinnate, pinnate-toothed, multiple pinnate, and dissected, all adorned with fine hairs (NC State Extension, n.d.). Arranged opposite to each other and sessile, or stalkless, these leaves can reach lengths of up to 3 inches (Cook, 1869). 

However, the true allure of lavender lies in its strongly fragrant flowers, which mesmerize in shades ranging from blue to violet, while some species present pristine white or soft pink blooms (Foster, 1993). These captivating flowers appear elegantly clustered on lofty, terminal, cylindrical spikes; each floral whorl features two modest bracts at its base, and the individual blooms take on a tubular shape with a split structure (Felter & Lloyd, 1898). An enchanting characteristic of lavender flowers is their bilateral symmetry, featuring a large two-lobed upper lip and a three-lobed lower lip, a trait shared with flowers within the Lamiaceae family.

Key Constituents

Flower: volatile oils (especially linalyl acetate, linalool, d-limonene, eucalyptol, camphor, and cineole), flavonoids, tannins, coumarins, diterpenes, vitamin C, resin (Fahmy et al., 2022; Hoffmann, 2003).

Sustainability

None known.

Harvesting Guidelines

To attain superior quality lavender, precise timing during the harvest is crucial. The optimal period for collecting lavender flowers is when the buds are in the very early stages of opening; if harvesting is postponed until the buds have fully opened, it can result in reduced potency and a shorter duration of efficacy (Gladstar, 2012).

Ensure to harvest lavender on a dry day and promptly begin the drying process to minimize the risk of mold development. During the drying process, avoid subjecting it to heat in order to help preserve the plant’s volatile oils and delightful fragrance. Lavender flowers possess a unique and long-lasting aroma while retaining a robust, bitter, and aromatic flavor with subtle notes of camphor, even after they have been dried (Felter & Lloyd, 1898).

Uses

In the following discussion, we have included some uses of lavender essential oil, due to the wide use of this popular preparation. It is clearly noted below when information pertains to the essential oil instead of or in addition to other whole plant lavender bud/flower preparations. Note that essential oils should always be properly diluted for topical application and should not be used internally unless under the guidance of an experienced practitioner.

Fresh lavender flowers are collected shortly before reaching full bloom and used in the production of lavender essential oil or subjected to drying for teas and a wide array of health-related, cosmetic, and culinary applications. Lavender’s effectiveness is commonly associated with its volatile oils, which are responsible for its powerful fragrance, a scent that the late herbalist Michael Moore (1993) has described as unparalleled: “Nothing smells as fine as good lavender” (p. 17). 

Among all volatile oil-rich plants, lavender may be considered the most multifaceted, showcasing an exceptionally wide array of clinical and household uses. The volatile oils can introduce a bitter undertone to preparations, making it advisable to incorporate lavender flowers sparingly or as part of a harmonious blend. Both lavender itself and its essential oil are recognized for their energetically drying properties.

Edible fresh or dried lavender buds and blooms are commonly used for decorating desserts, adorning beverages, and garnishing pastries. The fresh blossoms are also incorporated into a diverse array of culinary creations, including ice cream and vinegar. Lavender oil, along with other lavender derivatives like lavender absolute and concrete, lavandin oil, and spike lavender (Lavandula latifolia) oil, contributes as a flavor component to various food products, including frozen dairy desserts, candies, baked goods, aromatic vinegars, puddings, gelatins, as well as alcoholic and nonalcoholic beverages (Khan & Abourashed, 2010).

Additionally, the soothing scent of lavender oil finds widespread use as a fragrance in a variety of cosmetic and home goods offerings, including perfumes, colognes, waters, lotions, creams, detergents, and soaps, with spike lavender (Lavandula latifolia) oil in particular holding a distinct presence in the production of soaps and detergents (Opdyke, 1976).

Many sources commonly assert that the genus and common names of lavender have their roots in the Latin word lavare, meaning “to wash.” Nevertheless, considering the absence of any reference to lavender flowers or oil in a comprehensive exploration of Roman bathing customs, it seems that this widely held belief may not be accurate. It is instead plausible that the genus and common names have their origins in the Latin word livere, denoting a livid or bluish coloration, likely referring to the flowers, which are labeled as blue in historical texts such as The Family Herbal by English herbalist Sir John Hill (Upson & Andrews, 2004; Hill, 1812). 

Despite the speculative etymology of Lavandula spp., traditional European herbalism primarily used the blooms of this plant in baths as well as in teas and pillows for its mild sedative effect (Wichtl, 2004), and in North Africa, lavender played a role in infusing the water of communal bathhouses with its fragrance (Barrett, 1996). Herbalist Rosemary Gladstar (2012) suggests that lavender is among the most effective herbs to incorporate into a bath routine for the purpose of easing tension, reducing stress, and addressing insomnia. Her advice for unwinding after a demanding day involves taking a soothing bath with a few drops of lavender essential oil or adding a bundle of lavender blossoms enclosed in a muslin bag to the bathwater (Gladstar, 2012).

Throughout history, lavender has maintained a longstanding reputation for its purifying and cleansing properties. It was frequently spread around homes to ward off plague and discreetly tucked into cupboards and drawers to repel insects (McIntyre, 1996). Historical accounts suggest that in ancient Greece, Persia, and Rome, various lavender species were utilized for disinfecting hospitals and spaces used for those who were sick (Blumenthal et al., 2000).

Lavender’s strong scent naturally repels flies, mosquitos, and moths, and is known for its ability to kill lice and parasites (Berger, 1998). Additionally, blending lavender essential oil with citronella (Cymbopogon nardus), cedarwood (Cedrus atlantica), and European pennyroyal (Mentha pulegium) essential oils creates a highly effective insect repellent (Moore, 1993). Spike lavender (Lavandula latifolia) is the species of choice for repelling insects and moths due to its higher levels of camphor and cineole (Tierra, 1998).

Furthermore, lavender’s volatile oils, found in the whole plant and harnessed in various herbal preparations, are known for their antimicrobial attributes, are utilized in combating infections caused by staph, enterococcus, streptococcus, and pneumonia (McIntyre, 1996; Cavanagh & Wilkinson, 2002). Lavender’s aromatic flowers even found their way into the ranks of the Roman army, prized for their disinfecting attributes (Barrett, 1996). Lavender essential oil, whether used independently or in combination with tea tree (Melaleuca alternifolia) oil, can be diluted and applied to the skin to combat fungal infections like ringworm and nail fungus (Gladstar, 2012). While some use lavender essential oil diluted in a coconut carrier oil in the case of vaginal yeast infections (Romm, 2018), this is best done under the guidance of an experienced practitioner as application of essential oils to delicate mucosal tissues can cause sensitivity and safety concerns. 

Herbalist David Winston (2014) highlights the antiviral qualities of Lavandula angustifolia, which can be beneficial for addressing intestinal viruses. While not explicitly mentioned by Winston, it’s likely that other lavender species may also possess similar antiviral properties. Furthermore, lavender flowers and essential oil have historical applications in addressing candida infections, laryngitis, and sinusitis (Keville & Green, 1995).

Lavender is also “legendary as an herbal antiseptic” (Gladstar, 2012, p. 151), which combined with its antimicrobial properties makes it useful for disinfecting cuts, wounds, and sores while it soothes pain and aids the affected area (Foster, 1993; McIntyre, 1996). It also soothes the itch and pain of insect bites. As a burn aid, lavender essential oil promotes wound repair and speeds the return of healthy tissue (Mori et al., 2016). Anecdotes commonly note that after frequent application of lavender essential oil to an oven burn, for example, the skin shows dramatic improvement within 24 hours. For this purpose, the essential oil can be mixed into a thick aloe (Aloe vera) leaf gel and applied after the burn has cooled. This could also be applied to sunburned skin, since antioxidants in lavender oil also reduce skin damage from sunburn while aloe cools and soothes pain.

Furthermore, the use of lavender essential or infused oil is beneficial for massaging the scalp before shampooing, helping to stimulate the scalp, encourage hair growth, and reduce dryness; for this purpose, a mixture can be prepared by combining lavender essential oil with a small amount of rosemary (Salvia rosmarinus) oil in a solution made from dried stinging nettle (Urtica dioica) seeds, which are moistened with a touch of alcohol and steeped in olive oil three times their volume for a week (Moore, 1993).

Lavender flower bud shines as a nervine tonic for addressing nervous system complaints such as anxiety, depression, insomnia, and headache. In an anxious or stressful situation, inhaling the scent of lavender (whole plant or essential oil) or drinking lavender flower bud tea can calm and relax the anxious mind, bringing perspective and enabling the clarity of thought necessary to ease the situation. Several scientific studies suggest lavender soothes anxiety (anxiolytic) and depression (Cavanagh & Wilkinson, 2002; Koulivand et al., 2013). Winston (2014) specifically uses Lavandula angustifola for “stagnant” depression in combination with rosemary (Salvia rosmarinus) leaf, tulsi (Ocimum tenuiflorum) aerial parts, and damiana (Turnera diffusa) aerial parts.

In a 17th-century Persian medical text titled Makhzan-El-Adwiya, lavender is referred to as “broom of the brain” because it was believed to have the ability to sweep away impurities of the mind (Nadkarni, 1976). Another medical work, authored by Chandranandana and titled Gyu-zhi, also referred to as the “Four Tantras,” stands as the earliest Indian text to be translated into Tibetan, dating back to the 8th century BCE, and serves as the foundational text of Tibetan medicine (Blumenthal et al., 2000). Within the Gyu-zhi, lavender is incorporated as an element in psychiatric formulas which are usually prepared as edible ointments or beneficial butters and are still applied in modern Tibetan Buddhist medicine for addressing conditions like insanity and psychosis (Clifford, 1984).

By relaxing tension, lavender also may bring ease to those suffering from headaches. Rubbing lavender essential oil mixed with a carrier oil on the temples often brings quick improvement. A clinical trial concluded that inhalation of lavender essential oil may be an useful and safe approach to migraine headaches (Koulivand et al., 2013; Sasannejad et al., 2012). Lavender essential oil can also help ease muscle tension and discomfort (Keville & Green, 1995), as could a carrier oil infused with fresh or freshly dried lavender flower buds.

Lavender’s beneficial effect on the nerves indicates its use as a hypotensive. A small investigation of the influence of lavender essential oil on the nervous system showed that its use in aromatherapy led to significantly lowered blood pressure, heart rate, and respiratory rate while also acting as a relaxant (Sayorwan et al., 2012).

Lavender flower bud is a gentle tonic for strengthening the nervous system in the case of nervous debility or exhaustion (Hoffmann, 2003). The sweet spirit of lavender uplifts and balances emotions, eases mild depression and restores strength and vitality, with Rosemary Gladstar (2012) stating that it’s “an herb used traditionally to imbue courage and strength” and “a favorite herb to strengthen the heart and mind in a stressful situation” (p.151). It’s like a mother’s hand on your back, gentle and loving yet strong and supportive. As such, it can also be an ally during grieving and strife. 

With its soothing properties, lavender can help address challenging thoughts and emotions during a crisis, ease feelings of heaviness, boost motivation, make waking up easier, and soothe sadness (7Song, 2020). Whether through aromatherapy, herbal teas, or baths, lavender provides a sense of tranquility and emotional balance in difficult times. Lavender essential oil is often carried in herbal first aid kits and used in crisis situations for this reason.

Lavender is used as an additional means of support during the childbirth experience. The application of one or two drops of lavender essential oil on the mother’s feet and/or back, or the use of a warm lavender flower poultice on the lower back, can offer a calming sense of relaxation (Gladstar, 2012). Lavender flowers are also among the traditional herbs used for bathing the newborn, offering a warm and gentle welcome to the world (Gladstar, 2012).

Utilizing lavender as a nervine and mood lifter aligns with the typical characteristic of plants boasting blue or purplish flowers, which often possess cooling and nervine attributes according to the Doctrine of Signatures (Tierra, 1998). Lavender is commonly included in recipe blends for teas and infusions to address emotional distress and nervous depression. 

Lavender offers a gentle solution for occasional insomnia by acting as a mild sedative. David Winston (2014) states that Lavandula angustifolia reduces the time it takes to fall asleep and enhances the overall quality of sleep. The effects of linalool in lavender essential oil are thought to operate through the serotonergic pathway and play a role in the brain’s reticular activating system, which influences the sleep-wake cycle (Malloggi et al., 2022). Several scientific studies support the idea that lavender reduces insomnia and improves sleep quality (Koulivand et al., 2013). Incorporating lavender into a nightly routine, whether through a calming cup of flower tea, a few drops of the essential oil on a pillow, or a sleep pillow filled with its fragrant flowers, are all lovely ways to ease into a restful sleep.

As a carminative and antispasmodic (Hoffmann, 2003), lavender relaxes spasms in the digestive tract associated with tension and anxiety, as well as eases gas (McIntyre, 1996; Berger, 1998). Both 14th-century abbess Hildegard of Bingen (Berger, 1998) and contemporary herbalist Kiva Rose (n.d.) suggest lavender as a tonic for the liver, particularly for conditions involving tension and heat due to its relaxing and cooling nature. Furthermore, David Winston (2014) emphasizes lavender’s effectiveness in addressing digestive issues such as gas, nausea, gut dysbiosis, and a state referred to as “damp spleen,” which can manifest as chronic diarrhea.

The Commission E has approved Lavandula angustifolia for various internal and external applications; internally, it is considered useful for addressing restlessness, insomnia, Roemheld syndrome (also known as gastrocardiac syndrome), meteorism (or gas accumulation), and nervous intestinal discomfort and stomach irritations (as a stomachic) (Blumenthal et al., 2000). Externally, lavender is approved for use in balneotherapy, a modality that utilizes bathing to address functional circulatory disorders (Blumenthal et al., 2000).

Furthermore, German doctor and author Gerhard Madaus (1938) noted an additional external application as a rubefacient, stating that lavender is found in numerous folk preparations, with the decoction of the flower being used as a poultice for swelling and discoloration caused by external dislocations and injuries (p. 1724).

Finally, lavender essential oil contains a class of compounds called monoterpenes including an isolate called perillyl alcohol, the intranasal use of which has been evaluated as a well tolerated and effective anticancer agent for those with malignant brain tumors (Chen et al., 2015), though further research is needed on this application method.

Some of the volatile oils present in lavender also show promise as potential anticancer agents. D-limonene has proven to possess immunomodulatory effects, including antitumor properties (Chaudhary et al., 2012), and displays antioxidant and anti-inflammatory attributes (Yu et al., 2017). Additionally, eucalyptol (also known as 1,8-cineole), found in lavender, has demonstrated antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects and may have potential as an anticancer agent due to its anti-proliferative and anti-metastatic properties (Rodenak-Kladniewa et al., 2020). These discoveries indicate that the volatile oils found in lavender could have a valuable role in the treatment of cancer, and a study by Fahmy et al. (2022) “concluded that the antioxidant properties of lavender essential oil may account for its DNA-protective role and anticancer activities.”

This multifaceted herb offers a wide range of solutions for common health issues, making it a versatile and valuable addition to holistic wellness practices. In the words of Rosemary Gladstar (2012), “what would the world be without lavender?” (p. 149).

Adult Dose

Infusion: 1 teaspoon dried lavender buds in 8 fluid ounces of water steeped for 20-30 minutes; drink 4 fluid ounces 3x/day (Winston & Kuhn, 2007).
Tincture: 1.5-2 mL (1:5, 70%), 3-4x/day (Kuhn & Winston, 2008).

Safety

The use of whole lavender bud and flower is generally considered safe.

Some specific uses of lavender essential oil have been linked to endocrine-disruptive activity. A very small study in 2007 (Henley et al., 2007) linked tea tree and lavender essential oils in body care products to breast development in three prepubertal boys. Note that breast development in each of the three boys improved shortly after they ceased using products containing these oils. Expanding upon these findings, Ramsey et al. (2019) revealed that exposure to products scented with lavender is similarly connected to a condition called premature thelarche. This condition is characterized by the early development of breast tissue in girls below the age of eight, occurring independently of other signs of puberty. These collective findings underscore the importance of being mindful of the potentially endocrine-disruptive chemical activities present in lavender essential oil (and chemically similar tea tree (Melaleuca alternifolia) oil).  Such activities should be factored into the assessment of cases involving premature breast development in girls and instances of gynecomastia in boys and adult men (Ramsey et al., 2019). It’s important to emphasize that any endocrine-disruptive effects that are primarily associated with lavender essential oil are typically temporary and resolved with its discontinuation.
