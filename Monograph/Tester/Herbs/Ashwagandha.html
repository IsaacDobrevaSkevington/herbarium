Ashwagandha

Withania somnifera

Print← ArtichokeAsian Ginseng →

Actions

Ways to use

CapsulesDecoctionGummiesPowder in warm milkTincture
Common name
Ashwagandha, winter cherry, withania, Indian ginseng, poison gooseberry
Family
Solanaceae
Ayurvedic name
Ashwagandha (Bengali); asgandh (Hindi)
Parts used
Root

Native To

Most of Africa, India, the middle East, and parts of Asia (Royal Botanic Gardens of Kew [RBGK], n.d.).

Geographic Distribution

Dry regions of India, the Mediterranean, the Middle East, and Africa. Ashwagandha has been introduced to parts of southeast Australia (RBGK, n.d.).

Botanical Description

Ashwagandha is a short shrub in the nightshade family (Solanaceae) that grows to about 2.5-5 feet high with a central stem and orange-red fruit. The leaves are ovate and alternate. Branches extend radially and are covered in fine hairs. The flowers are green on the outside and yellow inside. The ripe fruits are smooth, orange-red berries and are inside calyxes (papery coverings). The roots are long and brown.

Key Constituents

Alkaloids (including somniferine, withanine, and anaferine), steroidal lactones (including withanolides), sitoindosides, iron, magnesium, phosphorous, copper, and zinc (Afewerky et al., 2021; Chevallier, 2007; Winston, 2007).



Sustainability

According to a study by Ram et al. (2012), ashwagandha is listed as a threatened species in Rajasthan, India; however, it is not included in the IUCN Red List of Threatened Species. Seek organically grown ashwagandha in commerce.

Harvesting Guidelines

Fruit and root are harvested in autumn from plants allowed to experience 1 full year of growth. Roots can be used fresh or dried. In India, ashwagandha grows as a perennial, but it can be grown as an annual in temperate regions and harvested in the fall.

Uses

The long, brown iron-rich root of ashwagandha is commonly used as a rasayana (life-prolonging tonic) in the ayurvedic tradition of India; the leaves, root, and even berries are used in different parts of India and Africa for various purposes (Winston, 2007). Western herbalists use the root primarily as a restorative adaptogen, though it is also used for its fertility enhancing, soporific, and even antimicrobial qualities (Bisht & Rawat, 2014).  

The word ashwagandha translates to “that which has the smell of a horse, as it gives the vitality and sexual energy of a horse” (Lad & Frawley, 1986, p. 160). This name alludes to the root’s strong odor as well as its use to restore strength and vitality, and to improve and enhance sexual drive. Rather than working like an aphrodisiac, the root is believed to work gradually over time to lower stress levels that may inhibit the sex drive. Ashwagandha may also support fertility. A clinical study in 2010 revealed that ashwagandha improved semen quality in infertile males by regulating reproductive hormone levels and seminal plasma oxidative stress (Ahmad et al., 2010). Some studies have shown ashwagandha extract having positive effects on male fertility factors including sperm concentration, semen volume, and sperm motility, though further research is needed to confirm these findings (Durg et al., 2018). Ashwagandha root extract has been found to significantly increase testosterone levels in males, through a mechanism of action involving inhibiting cortisol and prolactin’s effects on the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis and hypothalamus (Skrzypiec-Spring et al., 2025).

The genus name, somnifera, which means sleep-inducing, suggests its use as a sedative. This calming property makes ashwagandha unique among adaptogens, which are typically stimulating (Winston, 2007). Its nervine and adaptogenic actions make it a good choice for those with anxiety, stress, insomnia, and lack of energy. Though it is sometimes called “Indian ginseng,” ashwagandha is not a ginseng (Panax) and is less stimulating than Asian ginseng (Panax ginseng). Therefore, it is a good option for those with a sensitive constitution, children, the elderly, as well as for people debilitated by chronic disease.

Ashwagandha has a positive effect on reducing anxiety and stress levels (Arumgam et al., 2024). One randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled study demonstrated that at least 125 mg/day of ashwagandha root and leaf extract reduced mild-to-moderate chronic stress after 8 weeks (Pandit et al., 2024). Ashwagandha’s calming nature makes it an especially excellent choice for those whose tension levels keep them up at night. Some take ashwagandha before bedtime to promote restful sleep. However, for the nervous and high-strung, the sedating qualities of the plant may calm the nerves instead of causing sleepiness. A randomized, controlled study on anxiety by Cooley et al. (2009) evaluated the use of ashwagandha in conjunction with dietary counseling, relaxation techniques, and multivitamins for patients with moderate to severe anxiety. Along with a placebo, the control group was given psychotherapy and breathing exercises. After at least 2 months on the protocols, patients in both groups were evaluated. Those in the group taking ashwagandha experienced significantly lower levels of anxiety. In addition, the study authors concluded that “significant differences between groups were also observed in mental health, concentration, fatigue, social functioning, vitality, and overall quality of life” (Cooley et al., 2009), with greater clinical benefits for the group taking ashwagandha. 

All parts of ashwagandha are used commonly in its native homelands for a huge range of human health and veterinary purposes. According to Afewerky et al. (2021), the young shoots are eaten as a good source of macro and micronutrients in South Africa and India, the leaf is used in Kenya to support eye health, and in Tanzania the flower and dried mature root is used as an aphrodisiac. In Ethiopia, the dried root is used by veterinarians for supporting various issues in cattle, while a decoction of the roots and leaves is given to livestock to enhance milk production (Afewerky et al., 2021). 

Ashwagandha is used by the Indigenous people of South Africa as both a sedative and a hypnotic, as well as in support of a myriad of diseases (Afewerky et al., 2021). It is often used in those with Alzheimer’s, bronchitis, and malaria throughout Egypt, Djibouti, and Ethiopia. The leaf and berry is used topically to address skin abrasions and inflammations, while the root extract is a common ingredient in skin lotions, shampoos, and anti-aging products. A decoction of the leaves is useful for aiding hemorrhoids and rheumatism (Afewerky et al., 2021).

In addition to reducing inflammation and modulating the immune system, ashwagandha has been shown to reduce reactive oxygen species (ROS), modulate the function of mitochondria, moderate apoptosis, and improve endothelial function (Dar et al., 2015). Like most adaptogens, ashwagandha also acts as an immunomodulant by reducing white blood cell depletion and by activating lymphocytes. One small study in 2009 showed patients taking 6 mL of an ashwagandha extract twice a day showed a major change in immune cell activation in 96 hours (Mikolai et al., 2009). 

It is used in India to support cancer protocols (Winston, 2007) and has been shown to reduce fatigue and modestly improve survival rates in women with breast cancer (Biswal et al., 2013) as well as be effective against colon, prostate, ovarian, lung, and brain cancers (Dutta et al., 2019). Ashwagandha interferes with cancer cell proliferation and its anti-inflammatory compounds also target senescence associated secretory phenotype (SASP), thought to cause chemoresistance, indicating its multiple anticancer pathways (Kumar et al., 2022).

Ashwagandha strengthens the heart muscle, regulates cholesterol, and is considered both cardioprotective and hepatoprotective; it also has the ability to regulate hypertension (Afewerky et al., 2021; Kaur et al., 2015; Paul et al., 2021). Ashwagandha has been studied for its potential benefits for people with type 2 diabetes, with one small study showing a decrease in blood glucose comparable to an oral hypoglycemic drug as well as significantly decreased low-density lipoprotein (LDL) and very-low-density lipoprotein (VLDL) cholesterol levels (Andallu & Radhika, 2000). 

The scientific literature has demonstrated ashwagandha’s potential as a neuroprotective herb with an affinity toward not only the nervous system but also the brain, as it modulates the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis (Pandit et al., 2024). Among the conditions that it has been found supportive of are Alzheimer’s, Parkinson’s, Huntington’s, ALS, ischemic stroke, insomnia, attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), bipolar disorder, depression, and obssessive-compulsive disorder (Dar et al., 2015; Jhooty et al., 2024; Paul et al., 2021). Research speculates that ashwagandha’s ability to improve signs of depression and insomnia is due to its influence on GABAergic and serotonergic pathways, as well as the HPA and sympathetic adrenal medullary axes (Speers et al., 2021). ).In a randomized, placebo-controlled study by Chengappa et al. (2013) on patients with bipolar disorder, the use of a standardized extract of ashwagandha was found to provide significant benefits for three cognitive tasks the test subjects completed, appearing to improve auditory-verbal working memory, a measure of reaction time, and a measure of social cognition, with few side effects. The researchers concluded that, “given the paucity of data for improving cognitive capacity in bipolar disorder, [Withania somnifera extract] offers promise, appears to have a benign side-effects profile, and merits further study” (Chengappa et al., 2013). 

Ashwagandha has been found to change thyroid hormone levels in mice by stimulating thyroid function, and in 2005, a Dutch woman developed thyrotoxicosis (hyperthyroidism) after taking ashwagandha capsules for fatigue (van der Hooft et al., 2005). It should be used with caution by those taking thyroid hormones and should not be taken by those with hyperthyroid disease. It may be useful for hypothyroidism, but should be taken under the care of a qualified health practitioner. 

Some cultures use ashwagandha as a tonic during pregnancy, but other cultures use it as an abortifacient. This effect may depend on amounts, parts used, and preparation. Those interested in using ashwagandha during pregnancy are advised to consult with an herbalist who has experience working with this herb during pregnancy. The root is said to be rich in iron, making it beneficial for iron deficiency, but it should not be taken by those with conditions leading to excess iron loads such as hemochromatosis. 

Ashwagandha is sweet and yet bitter, while being very energetically warming. In the ayurvedic tradition, ashwagandha is most commonly taken as a powder mixed into milk, especially before bedtime, since it is best absorbed when taken with fat. Ayurvedic practitioners Vasant Lad and David Frawley (1986) suggest a dose of 5 grams of powder twice a day in warm, sweetened milk.

In its native territory of India, ashwagandha is also used in phytoremediation of contaminated soils (Afewerky et al., 2021). 

Adult Dose

Dried root or powder: 2-6 grams dried root/day, divided into 1-3 doses (Winston & Kuhn, 2008).

Tincture: 2-4 mL (1:5, 45%) 3x/day (Winston & Kuhn, 2008).

Capsules: 400-500 mg 2x/day (Winston, 2007).

Safety

Avoid ashwagandha in those who are sensitive to plants in the Solanaceae (nightshade) family (Winston, 2007). Ashwangandha should not be used during pregnancy unless under the care of a qualified healthcare professional (Gardner & McGuffin, 2013). Because the herb is a rich source of iron, it should be avoided in cases of hemochromatosis (excess iron) (Winston, 2007). Also avoid ashwagandha in cases of hyperthyroidism due to the herb’s stimulating effect on the thyroid (Winston, 2007). In cases of hypothyroidism, use with caution and only under the guidance of a healthcare provider, as regular use of ashwagandha has been linked with thyrotoxicosis (Kamal et al., 2022). Note that ashwagandha may potentiate the effects of thyroid medications and barbiturates (Winston, 2007). Because the herb can modify blood glucose levels, those with diabetes should be under the care of a qualified healthcare professional when taking ashwagandha (Gardner & McGuffin, 2013). In The Nursing Mother’s Herbal, Humphrey (2003) gives ashwagandha root a safety rating of ‘C’, noting its galactogogue properties and that there is some potential for mostly dose-related toxicity, so consult a trusted herbal practitioner and lactation expert before use.
