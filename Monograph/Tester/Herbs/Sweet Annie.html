Sweet Annie

Artemisia annua L.

Ways to use

Cold infusionEssential oilJuicePowdered extractSteam inhalantSuppositoryTincture
Common name
Sweet Annie, annual wormwood, sweet sagewort, sweet wormwood
Family
Asteraceae
Chinese medicine name
qinghao, huag hua hao; A. annua’s main constituent, artemisinin, is known as qinghaosu.
Parts used
Aerial parts when in flower; flowers have the highest artemisinin content (Buhner, 2012).

Native To

A. annua is native to China, Vietnam, Japan, Korea, North India, Myanmar, Western Asia, southern Siberia, and Southeast Europe (Buhner, 2012). It grows naturally in grasslands and can be found as part of steppe vegetation in northern parts of the Suiyuan and Chahar provinces of China, between 3,300 and 4,900 ft. (Das, 2012).

Geographic Distribution

While native to temperate Asia, A. annua has become naturalized and can be found growing wildly throughout the world at varying elevations (World Health Organization, 2006).

Botanical Description

A. annua is an annual plant adorned in delicate, aromatic, and glabrous green leaves that embellish a straight, deep velvet-brown (WHO, 2006) to light-green colored stem. Growing anywhere from 3-6 ft in height (Hilty, 2019), this structural axis has prominent, curved ridges. The lower leaves, 3-5 cm in length and 2-4 cm in width, are ovate and tripinnate. Its middle leaves are bipinnate, further contributing to A. annua’s feather-like, graceful appearance. The upper leaves are smaller in size, sessile and less compound, with bracteal, more unambiguous, simple lobed leaves at the top (WHO, 2006).

The upper stems are embroidered in modest, globose panicle inflorescences, with drooping flower heads that are 2.0-2.5 mm in diameter, cup-shaped, and supported by six oblong, lustrous, dark green bracts. A. annua blooms from late summer into the fall (Hilty, 2019), from August to September (Plants For A Future, 2012), and produces no floral scent (Hilty, 2019). Each floret produces a single achene upon maturity that is 0.6-0.8 mm long and presents a transitional shape between oblong and ovate (WHO, 2006).

Key Constituents

Volatile oils (camphene, β-camphene, isoartemisia ketone, 1-camphor, β-caryophyllene, β-pinene, artemisia ketone, 1,8-cineole, camphene hydrate, and cuminal), sesquiterpenoids (artemisinin), artemisic acid, artemisilactone, artemisinol and epoxyarteannuinic acid), flavonoids, coumarins, proteins (β-galactosidase, β-glucosidase), and steroids (β-sitosterol and stigmasterol) (World Health Organization [WHO], 2006).

Sustainability

A. annua readily self-seeds, has high adaptability, and can easily be found growing wildly in a wide range of environments throughout the world (WHO, 2006). It prefers disturbed habitats, which can include fields, edges of yards, outer borders of forests, low-altitude areas along ponds and rivers, vacant lots, fences, gardens, along railroads, and on the side of roads (Hilty, 2019). As a result, there are no known sustainability issues with this plant.

Harvesting Guidelines

Harvest A. annua on a sunny day when it is in the early stage of flower budding (WHO, 2006), prior to its blooming from August to September (Plants For A Future, 2012). Cut down the whole crop and remove any foreign matter, then cut into shorter pieces. Dry in the sun with protection from rain and water. Once the leaves have dried, shake or garble the branches to separate the leaves from the plant. Store in a cool, dry and dark place until use (WHO, 2006).

The content of artemisinin in dried A. annua leaves varies depending on the time of harvest, temperature, geographical conditions, and fertilizer utilization. The highest artemisinin content that can be reached is 1-2%, and the plant can be tested for this before harvesting. Artemisinin content can be reduced if the leaves are harvested too early or too late in the season (WHO, 2006).

This plant should be harvested in areas where foraging is permitted. Basic principles of harvesting should be followed to maintain sustainable growth patterns: collection methods should not diminish the abundance of wild A. annua, and the natural environment should not be disturbed (WHO, 2006).

Uses

The genus name Artemisia was inspired by Artemis, goddess of the hunt, wilderness, virginity, and childbirth, protector of young children and reliever of diseases in women. She is one of the most respected Greek deities (“Artemis,” 2014), possessing a wild, impulsive, confident, and independent nature. Artemis is a fierce warrior, with a desire to protect and nurture, but when underestimated, her vengeful and unpredictable side can show itself (“Artemis,” 2018).

Artemisia annua is a potent plant; its power demands respect, like the Greek goddess herself. However, it’s easy to misconstrue the capabilities of this plant. It has a subtle, delicate, and modest appearance; its seeds are too small for the birds, and its flowers are unappealing to insect pollinators (Hilty, 2019).

Deeply cherished, this plant has long been considered holy (Culpeper, 1653/2007) and the Artemisia genus has included religious herbs in various cultures (Beyerl, 1984). In Greek mythology, Artemisia is said to have been handed down from the gods through the centaur Chiron, allowing herbalists to make use of its properties (Beyerl, 1984).

All plants in the genus Artemisia have similar actions of being systemically antibacterial and antimicrobial. However, A. annua is strongly indicated for heat in the blood, most commonly identified by nosebleeds and rashes that appear as a result of bleeding under the skin (Winston, 2013). Author and herbalist Stephen Buhner (2012) states that this plant, artemisinin, and its derivatives are best known as “systemic antihematoparasiticals” (p. 140) due to the plant’s proficiency at killing parasites in the blood (Buhner, 2012). In large amounts, some species of Artemisia can create unpleasantness and disagreeable side effects (Beyerl, 1984), resulting in these species being best used under careful regulation (Skenderi, 2004).

When used respectfully, this plant can be extremely beneficial, like Artemis’ divine nature, devoted to nurture and protect. A. annua is distinguished in herbal literature for its use for gastrointestinal (GI) tract issues (Buhner, 2012) and can be used in cases of inflammatory reactions in the colon. A 2012 study used extracts of A. annua prepared as tea infusions to perform an assay on Caco-2 cells that investigated the plant’s activities on intestinal inflammation. These extracts considerably lowered the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-8 and IL-6, which is believed to be a result of A. annua’s phenolic compound content, most notably rosmarinic and chlorogenic acids (de Magalhães et al., 2012).

The earliest recorded use of Artemisia annua was as an anti-inflammatory in 168 BCE by the West Han Dynasty of China through a text named The Recipes for 52 Kinds of Diseases. It’s also mentioned in the Shen Nong Ben Cao Jing (translated as The Divine Farmer’s Materia Medica) from 200 CE, which is considered to serve as a collection of traditional herbal knowledge gained through the centuries. Since then, it has remained an important herb in Chinese medicine texts and oral traditions for its variety of actions—most notably its anti-inflammatory, antipyretic, and antimalarial properties. Its antimalarial action is responsible for establishing the use of this plant in the Western world (Graziose et al., 2010), and won Professor Tu Youyou (who helped discover its main constituent, artemisinin) a Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 2015 (Su & Miller, 2015).

When the Chinese government was looking for new approaches to combat malaria, the number one illness compromising soldiers’ health during the Vietnam War, they started “Project 523” in 1967. This venture proposed to identify and develop long-term preventative methods against malaria, and Chinese medicine texts became fundamental for the identification and extraction of artemisinin from A. annua (Graziose et al., 2010).

Artemisia annua was one of more than 100 plant species tested in Project 523 (Su & Miller, 2015) and became the most powerful and effective antimalarial herb to date. Interestingly, head researcher Tu Youyou reviewed a traditional recipe from a text entitled, Emergency Prescriptions Kept up One’s Sleeve, which gave her the idea to lower the temperature used during the extraction method (Graziose et al., 2010). By 1972, an extract of A. annua, known as qinghaosu or artemisinin, was shown effective in human cases of malaria (Su & Miller, 2015). Now, Artemisinin-based Combination Therapies (ACTs) are approved and favored by the World Health Organization as first-line treatment for malaria (WHO, 2018).

It is important to note that when dosing artemisinin for use with malaria, the relapse rate is smaller at higher doses. Additionally, the longer artemisinin is taken, the less frequently it materializes in the blood, resulting in 24% of the original dose at day 1 being present by day 7. This makes both the length of time and amount used critical factors; artemisinin should not be used for longer than 7 days (Buhner, 2012), and its use should be supervised by healthcare professionals. The World Health Organization (2018) emphasizes the need for diagnostic testing and combination therapies for malaria, rather than the use of artemisinin alone, which can lead to resistance. In the dosage section, you will see instructions for taking the herb as a juice in instances of malaria. This preparation is very potent and effective; however, compliance will be difficult due to the bitter taste (Kuhn & Winston, 2008). You can preserve the juice by adding 20% alcohol (Buhner, 2012).

Artemisinin is a sesquiterpene lactone, which is a characteristic constituent of plants in the Asteraceae family (Ganora, 2009) and is distinguishable by an endoperoxide bridge, a peroxide group (-O-O-) that forms a link between 2 atoms that are both part of a larger molecule. This endoperoxide bridge gives the plant its antimalarial activity (Cui & Su, 2009) by releasing virulent free radicals once it penetrates the plasmodia (Meshnick et al., 1996). Sesquiterpene lactones primarily have anti-inflammatory, antispasmodic, antibacterial, and antiparasitic properties. They also act as digestive stimulants, increasing the production and release of bile from the liver, which relates back to this herb’s traditional use to improve digestion (Ganora, 2009).

However, artemisinin is poorly soluble in both water and oil, so the carbonyl group, a carbon atom double-bonded to an oxygen atom (C=O), was reduced to yield its derivatives (Cui & Su, 2009): dihydroartemisinin, artemether, arteether, and artesunate (Woodrow et al., 2005). These semisynthetic derivatives also show antimalarial activity and are collectively called artemisinins. They are effective towards combating nearly all sexual and asexual parasite stages, and within minutes can kill immature plasmodia in the erythrocytes (red blood cells) with a rate of relatively 10,000 per life cycle, preventing the progression of growth to the more pathological stages and producing fast clinical results (Cui & Su, 2009).

Since artemisin’s action is so specific to malaria, it’s thought that the mechanism of action behind artemisinin and its derivatives relates to the heme-mediated degradation of the endoperoxide bridge, which ultimately produces stable carbon-centered free radicals that react with vulnerable cellular components of parasite molecules, causing damage that leads to parasite death (Tilley et al., 2016).

The activation of artemisinins is produced by a reduced iron source, which stems from hemoglobin degradation in the host due to its digestion by the malaria parasite (Cui & Su, 2009). This degradation process releases free heme that is toxic to the parasite, thus it gets converted by the parasite into a biocrystal called hemozoin, which inhibits the body’s immune response and is thought to be responsible for the pathophysiology of malaria (Coronado et al., 2014). However, this is just a hypothesis and despite the extensive research done on artemisinin, its mechanism of action on malaria parasites is still a topic of debate (Graziose et al., 2010). Complete chemical synthesis of artemisinin has also not been achieved yet, which means that the Artemisia annua plant serves as the main source of this beneficial constituent (Cui & Su, 2009).

Even though its antimalarial actions make Artemisia annua the renowned plant it is today, traditional uses and current research show that this plant and its constituents have more to offer. Artemisinins have also expressed inhibitory effects against protozoa (Toxoplasma gondii), fungi (Cryptococcus neoformans), helminths (Fasciola hepatica and Schistosoma species), and viruses (Human cytomegalovirus) (Ho et al., 2014).

Herbalist Margi Flint (2005) describes using Artemisia annua in a formula she created for her daughter who had contracted Lyme. She employed this “Waltheria Formula,” during the third stage of the disease when it became more chronic, disbursing into the brain and bones. She combined A. annua with waltheria (Waltheria indica), American ginseng (Panax quinquefolius), and osha (Ligusticum porteri), administering it in pulses at 4-6-week intervals (Flint, 2005).

Artemisia annua’s traditional uses include lowering fevers, combating fungal infections and parasites (Winston, 2013), activating the liver, aiding in menstrual health (due to its emmenagogue actions), and as a steam inhalant for respiratory issues (Buhner, 2012).

Herbalist David Winston includes A. annua among a group of herbs he terms “fragrant bitters,” which have stimulant actions on depressed tissue states, which as herbalist Matthew Wood (2004) explains, can be associated with parasitic and bacterial infections. Winston combines this plant with boneset (Eupatorium perfoliatum), Jamaican dogwood (Piscidia piscipula) bark, lycium (Lycium barbarum) root, and tree peony (Paeonia suffruticosa) for action against dengue fever (Winston, 2013). All throughout its history, A. annua has followed in the footsteps of its namesake, Artemis, goddess of the hunt, in its powerful actions as a killing agent.

But also like Artemis, Artemisia annua can be nurturing and protective. Research indicates that artemisinins could play a key role in support for neurodegeneration. One study demonstrated a concentration-dependent neuroprotective effect on PC12 cells and primary cortical neurons that were under sodium nitroprusside-induced oxidative stress. This exhibits that artemisinin could activate the extracellular signal-regulated kinases (ERK) pathway, which communicates a signal from a receptor on the surface of the cell to the DNA in the nucleus of the cell (Zheng et al., 2016). Another study showed that artemisinin had the same inhibitory effects on hydrogen peroxide-induced oxidative stress via the ERK pathway (Chong & Zheng, 2016). Additional research indicates that it also modulates CREB, MAPK, and Akt/mTOR pathways, offering more encouragement to further study its neuroprotective actions (Lu et al., 2019).

Adult Dose

Tincture: 30-90 drops fresh aerial parts (1:2, 50-60%) up to 6x/day (Buhner, 2012; Ganora, 2009).

Infusion: 8 fl oz (1 tsp dried herb per cup of boiling water infused for 20-30 minutes) up to 3x/day (Winston, 2013). For malaria, pour 2 cups of hot water over 100 grams of fresh leaves and flowers, cover, and let sit for 12 hours. Strain and drink the entire preparation. Do this every day for 7 days and then repeat after 2 weeks (Buhner, 2012).

Juice: ¼ oz fresh herb juice 3x/day (Kuhn & Winston, 2008). For malaria, take 1 tablespoon daily for 7 days and then repeat after 2 weeks (Buhner, 2012).

Powder extract of artemisinin: For use against malaria, take 500-1000 mg on the first day, then 500 mg daily for 2-4 more days (Buhner, 2012).

Safety

Use of A. annua during pregnancy (Winston, 2013) and lactation (Alfs, 2003) should be under the care of a qualified professional. A. annua contains sesquiterpene lactones (e.g. artemisinin), which are a characteristic constituent of plants in the Asteraceae family and have the potential to create a mild to strong allergic reaction for individuals who are highly sensitive. Herbalists should use caution and discretion when using plants in the Asteraceae family for those prone to respiratory and contact allergens, or who have recurring eczema (Ganora, 2009). Usage of artemisinin and its derivatives should be monitored by healthcare professionals (Skenderi, 2004).
