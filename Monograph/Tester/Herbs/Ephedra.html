Geographic Distribution

Asian species are found in China, Tibet, India, Pakistan, Japan, southern Siberia and are also cultivated extensively (American Botanical Council, 2000). Thirteen American species are native to the Southwest region of North America, with at least two Asian species having been introduced to California (United States Department of Agriculture, 2019).

Botanical Description

Ephedra favors dry or desert-like environments but can be found in a host of habitats. It is a dioecious perennial shrub, one that is considered to belong to a primitive plant family in terms of evolution—rather like horsetail (Equisetum arvense)— and there are about 40 different species in existence (Blumenthal & King, 1995).

Ephedra’s appearance is brush or broom-like with long, jointed branching stems and tends to be low-growing. The plant exudes a piney scent and possesses an astringent taste that may be numbing to the tongue (Blumenthal & King, 1995; Kemper, 2000). Its jointed twigs are yellowish-green in color, tend to branch out from a base, and may show triangular leaves at the reddish-brown nodes, but is otherwise quite leafless in appearance (Blumenthal & King, 1995). Its blossoms tend to be yellow and its resultant cones, which look like berries, are red.

Key Constituents

Alkaloids (ephedrine and pseudoephedrine) (American Botanical Council, 2000; Hoffmann, 2003; Tierra, 1988)

Sustainability

No known sustainability issues.

Harvesting Guidelines

Ephedra is traditionally harvested in the autumn. Note that prior to the banning of ephedra products, E. sinica (Chinese ephedra) was rarely used in U.S. commerce; in fact, many species including E. equisetina were employed (American Botanical Council, 2000).
For North American species, the stems of the herb may be gathered at all times with the exception of flowering or seeding (Garran, 2008). It has been observed that rainfall significantly lowers the alkaloid content of the herb, therefore it may be ideal to harvest ephedra during dry spells (The School of Natural Healing, 2019).

Uses

Ephedra became a controversial herb following widespread employment in supplement form within the United States and is now restricted in use due to a rash of dangerous side effects. Ephedra as an herb, however, has been used for many thousands of years in the traditional herbal medicine practices of Asia. It has also been historically used in ayurvedic practices and among the native peoples as well as the settlers of North America, where its traditional indications often echo those of Eastern practices.

Ephedra’s presence in Chinese medicine dates back thousands of years and today tends to center around the use of the E. sinica species. Ephedra may also appear in the Hindu Vedas (Hoffmann, 2003), a body of religious texts from ancient India. It has been debated amongst various scholars as to whether or not ephedra was an ingredient in the ancient Vedic drink, known as soma, which was thought to confer immortality.

The species E. altissima, meanwhile, has been found along with other herbs in a Neanderthal grave in Iraq (Lietava, 1992), although it remains unknown if this appearance was accidental, symbolic, or representative of a particular ritual use. Ephedra has also been discovered in the Gumugou Cemetery of northwest China and the specimens have been dated to 3800 B.P. While there is apparently some philological evidence of ephedra worship and its use in Eurasia Steppe cultures (Xie et al., 2013), no physical evidence had been found to back up texts until the archaeological find in the cemetery, and some scholars have tentatively concluded that its presence may be indicative of possible religious and medicinal usage by the local population (Xie et al., 2013).

Ephedra has had official status in the pharmacopeias of Germany, where it was an approved herb in its Commission E monographs, but today the extraction of its alkaloid in supplement form, ephedrine, is illegal but for a prescription. It is also found in the ayurvedic pharmacopoeia (American Botanical Council, 2000).

Ephedrine and pseudoephedrine, the two major alkaloids of ephedra’s chemical makeup, became used in prescription and over-the-counter supplemental products—often to relieve allergies or nasal congestion—and these products could in turn be used to create the illegal stimulant methamphetamine (crystal meth), creating further concerns about safety. Ephedra’s use (again, primarily in supplemental form) began then to be called into question in the 1980-1990s when it appeared not only in illegal street drugs but also in supplements for weight loss and athletic performance, sometimes combined with caffeine (American Botanical Council, 2000).

By the close of 2003, the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) banned all supplements containing these constituents of ephedra (Harvard Health Publishing, 2004). At the same time, it should be noted that use of the E. sinica species was rare in U.S. commerce, where many species including equisetina were employed (American Botanical Council, 2000). Asian ephedra is significantly different in chemical constituents than American species, which may lack the same configuration of alkaloids (Tierra, 1988).

In the United States, sale of ephedra herb is now restricted in some states to licensed practitioners of acupuncture and doctors of Eastern medicine (indeed, ephedra has been the first herb to be pulled by the FDA). American species, which are considered as lacking the problematic alkaloids in potent quantities, are exempt. Plants and seeds, however, are still sold, and the dried Chinese ephedra, usually E. sinica or equisetina herb (leaves or powder) can sometimes be found for sale on the Internet as a “tea,” indulging in a legal loophole that exempts a tea from supplement status.

Ephedra’s controversial aspects can be difficult to grasp because research and news reports confuse the use of the whole herb itself, the extraction of its alkaloids such as ephedrine, or its use in compound formulas, and all the while different species of ephedra may be used. While most adverse events seem to have arisen from supplements—extractions of alkaloids or compound preparations—it should be noted that hepatotoxicity has been linked to ephedra in Chinese formulations as well (Bone & Mills, 2013).

The herb’s primary constituent, the alkaloid ephedrine, was first isolated in 1885 (Lee, 2011). This alkaloid acts upon the body as a sympathomimetic that is also structurally akin to adrenaline (American Botanical Council, 2000). After its initial isolation, ephedrine and ephedra herb were hailed as a cure for asthma and found its apex of popularity towards the end of the 1950s (Lee, 2011). At the same time, however, ephedrine was increasingly found to have unpleasant side effects, such as significantly raising blood pressure, and so fell into disuse for its first popular indication of asthma (Mabey, 1988) until revived in supplemental form for weight loss, athletic stimulants, or street drugs.

Use of the whole plant rather than an extraction of ephedrine, on the other hand, showed only a small elevation in blood pressure (Hoffmann, 2003). At the same time, using the whole plant as herbalists often do means that it contains other constituents and other alkaloids that can help to eradicate or balance out the side effects created by isolating compounds.

Another of ephedra’s main constituents, often considered its second primary alkaloid, is known as pseudoephedrine, and it actually works to lower blood pressure and heart rate, thereby removing or balancing out the problem created by ephedrine (Mabey, 1988; Hoffmann, 2003). Pseudoephedrine in general is understood to cause far less stimulation of the autonomic nervous system than ephedrine, and it was used as an over-the-counter nasal decongestant (Hoffmann, 2003). Ephedra herb is generally still considered safe if the whole herb is used in an individual with normal blood pressure (American Botanical Council, 2000).

In Eastern herbal traditions, the primary indication for ephedra has long been for asthma and bronchial complaints (followed by allergic responses, circulation issues, and bladder/kidney imbalances). And while American species are considered less powerful, lacking in the problematic alkaloids, and traditionally used differently, there are more parallels than supposed.

Ephedra’s primary, traditional Eastern indication is for asthma and bronchial conditions, followed closely by allergic responses (the herb is a bronchodilator and an anticatarrhal agent). The World Health Organization acknowledged ephedra’s efficacy in asthma and in nasal congestion (World Health Organization, 1999) and the Commission E approved ephedra herb internally for respiratory diseases manifestation with bronchospasms in both children (over age six) and adults (American Botanical Council, 2000).
Both aerial portions and the root of ephedra are strongly associated with the Lung meridian in Chinese medicine and particularly with those respiratory issues that arise from wind-cold (Stevenson et al., 2014; Tierra, 1988). For cough and asthma, ephedra is traditionally paired with apricot seed (Stevenson et al., 2014). For exterior wind-cold syndrome that presents with a number of other symptoms, ephedra may be found paired with cinnamon (Stevenson et al., 2014).

The indication for bronchial issues is also found in ayurvedic practice, which tends to use the gerardiana species. Ephedra in ayurvedic practices is commonly known as somlata or somalata and used in parallel fashion to that of the Chinese, namely for asthma, hay fever, colds, and allergic rashes and circulatory issues (Chauhan, 1999; Quattrocchi, 2012). Ephedra belongs to a class of herbs called swasahara, or Śvāsahara, which specifically relieve breathing (Dass, 2013; Pole, 2013). Its root is considered useful in allergy formulas possibly because it can be regarded as a stimulant for the adrenals (Tierra, 1988). In Ayurveda, ephedra is advised only in instances of cold and is considered to arrest the release of histamine from mast cells (Pole, 2013).

Only unsubstantiated claims point to use of American species of ephedra for asthma both by settlers and native tribes of North America in the same way as the Asianspecies of ephedra (Garran, 2008). Because North American species may be considered less powerful than their Chinese counterparts, particularly in its primary chemical constituent of alkaloids, it has been suggested that American species might be good choices for addressing asthma when blood pressure is an issue (Garran, 2008). It is also said that American species are not only less powerful but also slower to act upon the body, although efficacious if taken early on in an external illness, and that it is the root that can be used directly in place of the Chinese species (Garran, 2008). Those species that have been used in herbal practice in a similar and safe manner by some practitioners are E. viridis, nevandensis, and californica (Garran, 2008).

American traditions were not without connection to the lungs, however. In the Americas, certain species were used to aid coughs: E. torreyana was used as a cough medicine by the Keres peoples of the Pueblo in an infusion of stems (Native American Ethnobotany Database, 2019; Swank, 1932). The Navajo tribe has employed a decoction of the whole of E. torreyana for cough (Native American Ethnobotany Database, 2019; Vestal, 1952). Ayurveda, meanwhile, uses Eastern species of ephedra to alleviate coughing (an ayurvedic action known as kasahara) (Pole, 2013).

In Chinese medicine, ephedra is traditionally paired with gypsum in a renowned formula known as mahuang-shigao. This is intended for cold symptoms with bronchial asthma. Mahuang-shigao was studied recently and found to reduce asthma–but unfortunately so far only in animal studies on rats (Mei et al., 2016) although its alkaloids ephedrine and pseudo-ephedrine and their impact on asthma had been studied as far back as the early 20th century (Christopherson & Broadbent, 1934). Another recent study on rats asserted that a decoction of mahuang was effective in ameliorating the inflammation of airways (He et al., 2018). A rare clinical Chinese study, meanwhile, had earlier concluded that using an oral liquid of ephedra on human subjects was effective in ameliorating bronchial asthma as well as relieving allergic reactions and spasm (Yuan et al., 1998).

Ephedra has also been traditionally used in Eastern practices to address urinary problems. Aerial portions of ephedra are also associated with the Bladder meridians in Chinese medicine (Stevenson et al., 2014; Tierra, 1988), where there is furthermore an association with imbalances of the adrenals and a focus on assisting urinary output in general (Stevenson et al., 2014). Ephedra is a diuretic, and in edema similar to the acute nephritic edema of the Western world, ephedra is paired in Chinese medicine with gypsum (Stevenson, 2014). It is also used as a diuretic for oedema in ayurvedic practice (Pole, 2013).

Meanwhile, over in the Americas, the Shoshoni tribe were using E. nevadensis as a diuretic, decocting the twigs and branches to stimulate urination (Train et al., 1941) and the Keres people infused or chewed the stems of E. torreyana for bladder problems (Swank, 1932). The Navajo tribe infused dried E. trifurca for kidney issues (Elmore, 1944) while the Paiute infused or decocted its twigs (E. viridis) as a kidney or urinary aid (Train et al., 1941). Settlers in the Southwest, meanwhile, predominantly used ephedra to address urinary problems and/or kidney issues (Turner, 2009).

Ephedra also strongly impacts the circulatory system of the body; the herb’s actions include circulatory stimulation and vasodilation. The root of ephedra is also associated with the Heart meridian in Chinese medicine (Tierra, 1988). Ephedra has been traditionally used to address low blood pressure or insufficient circulation (Hoffmann, 2003). Subsequent research has indicated that ephedrine increases heart rate (Jing et al., 2010). Ayurvedic practice may also employ ephedra (usually E. sinensis or gerardiana) as a circulatory stimulant in cases of cold extremities (Pole, 2013).

Ephedra was often used as a blood medicine or blood purifier in American tribal practices and Dr. Christopher also suggested this indication in his own practice (The School of Natural Healing, 2019). Keeping in mind that American species of ephedra do not have the same chemical profile as Eastern species, several North American species do appear throughout extant ethnographic records: E. antisyphilitica, californica, facsiculata, nevadensis, torreyana, trifurca, and viridis (Native American Ethnobotany Database, 2019), where they were often used to concoct a beverage (tea) by both native tribes and colonials. Settlers and frontiersmen also considered ephedra to be a blood purifier, cleaning organs also such as the liver and kidneys (Turner, 2009). The stems might also be chewed to quench thirst or made into a poultice to address snake bite (Turner, 2009).

This popular preparation as a libation was sometimes referred to as “whorehouse tea” because of its reputation as efficacious in cases of syphilis and venereal diseases in general. It was also known as “Brigham Tea”, or “Mormon Tea.” E. antisyphilitica’s connections to Mormons is not entirely clear, although Dr. Christopher had noted that every autumn Mormon families would make a trek to the desert to harvest ephedra for “Brigham Tea” and that, as pioneers, they took to drinking it because they avoid on principle tea or coffee (The School of Natural Healing, 2019; Turner, 2009).

E. gerardiana appears to have been indicated for syphilis in Russia (Chauhan, 1999). Meanwhile, Texan settlers, Hispanic communities, and native peoples of the Southwest (among them Apache, Zuni, O’odham, and Akimel) were using the ephedra tea for venereal disease, perhaps related to its blood-purifying aspects, but also surely in ephedra’s ability to assist with painful urination, which is a feature of gonorrhea (Turner, 2009). The Dispensatory of the United States of America, published in the early 20th century, notes that its tannins might account for its diuretic action (Remington & Wood, 1918).

Ephedra is also traditionally indicated for rheumatism. Its traditional connections to joint pain may be amusing if one considers the Doctrine of Signatures; this is a remarkably jointed plant, visually-speaking. Anglos of the American Southwest employed ephedra for rheumatism (Turner, 2009). It has been considered tonic and traditionally used in ayurvedic practices for rheumatism (as well as syphilis) (Nadkarni, 1994), and for rheumatism high in kapha and manifesting with swollen joints (Pole, 2013). Near the turn into the 20th century, ephedra was noted by the Eclectics as indicated for rheumatism with a frequent and weak pulse, quickened respiration, scant urinary output, or indigestion with anorexia (The Eclectic Medical Journal, 1892).

While human studies are so far lacking, animal studies have been used to conclude in the eyes of science that ephedra may indeed be used therapeutically for human arthritis as it has been found to decrease levels of pro-inflammatory mediators while increasing anti-inflammatory ones in rats (Alamgeer et al., 2018) and can furthermore decrease pain in mice (Nakamori et al., 2017).

In Chinese medicine, two other major actions of ephedra are those of provoking sweating (diaphoresis) (Stevenson et al., 2014) and that of arresting diarrhea. The root of ephedra has been used in Chinese medicine to address both diarrhea and sweating complaints (such as night sweats, postpartum sweats or deficiency sweats), where its primary actions appear to be that of astringency and diaphoresis (Tierra, 1988).

Some Pueblo peoples of North American also utilized ephedra species for diarrhea or sweating, although a different plant part: the Tewa people indicated that the leaves and stems of E. antisyphilitica were chewed or a decoction made as a specific for diarrhea (Robbins et al., 1916). Meanwhile, the Keres people used the stems of E. torreyana as a diaphoretic in the sweatbath (Swank, 1932). The Shoshoni tribe also made a compound infusion for children’s diarrhea with E. viridis (Train et al., 1941).

Ephedra has also been traditionally used in Eastern practices for colds, and its volatile oil has been understood in some traditional herbal practices to inhibit the influenza virus (Mabey, 1988). A study in 2013 of combined extracts of four different herbs including ephedra sinica found a synergistic impact against gram-negative and gram-positive bacteria, offering promise for treating infectious diseases (Dashtdar et al., 2013). Granules made from four plants, one of them ephedra, was administered to healthy adults with influenza with success and compared to similar antiviral efficacy of neuraminidase inhibitors (Nabeshima et al., 2012).

As with the Chinese tradition, ephedra might be found as a cold remedy in the traditions of some indigenous American peoples; the twigs of viridis were infused or decocted by the Paiutes for colds (Native American Ethnobotany Database, 2019; Train et al., 1941). In ayurvedic traditions, ephedra works to decrease kapha (particularly in colds) and to generally increase pitta or vata doshas (Pole, 2013).

Ephedra has also been used to effect weight loss, which we have noted was part of the problem leading to its ban due to isolation of constituents and use of weight loss supplements with dangerous side effects. Much research on this issue has been undertaken on mice and rats but a handful of clinical trials on human subjects have concluded that it is indeed effective in stimulating weight loss, namely: in a randomized, double-blind trial, ephedra was found—in conjunction with a low-calorie diet—to lower body mass index in healthy but obese Korean women (Kim et al., 2008); in another trial, ephedra modulated gut microbiota in overweight Korean women, resulting in loss of weight (Kim et al., 2014).

Ephedra remains tangled up in its largely supplemental controversy; its banned or restricted reality; the “otherness” of largely Eastern traditions, paired with the disuse of its often parallel native American and colonial traditions; and a lack of scientific study on its traditional applications using human trials. It is to be hoped that not only the herbal use of Eastern species of ephedra may be re-evaluated by the modern Western world in the future and re-opened to herbal practitioners but also that the virtues of North American species might be revisited.

 

Adult Dose

Tincture: 3-9 grams/day**; 1-4 ml, 3x/day (1:4 in 45% alcohol)***; 3-10 grams****
Decoction: of aerial portions, 2-4 grams of sinica and for Western species, 4-12 grams, while for root, 6-15 grams*; 3-12 grams/day**; 1-2 teaspoons of herb per cup of water, 3x a day
*** Dosage information from *Planetary Herbology by Michael Tierra; **Principles and Practice of Phytotherapy by Kerry Bone and Simon Mills; ***Medical Herbalism by David Hoffmann; ****Handbook of Traditional Chinese Medicine by Xutian Stevenson et al.

Safety

American ephedra species have been found to contain little or no alkaloids. Therefore, contraindications and drug interactions for Asian species of E. sinica, vulgaris, equisetina, or intermedia may not apply (Brinker, 2010).
Ephedra is contraindicated in high blood pressure, glaucoma, anxiety, restlessness, adenoma of the prostate with accompanying accumulation of residual urine, thyrotoxicosis, pheochromocytoma, and poor circulation of the cerebrum (American Botanical Council, 2000).
Ephedra is not to be used with insomnia due to adrenergic stimulant effects; risk of suicide may be increased due to sympathomimetic activity of ephedrine; and it is generally avoided in psychiatric conditions, where it may also increase anxiety (Brinker, 2010).
Ephedra is not recommended in cases of headaches, nausea, vomiting, insomnia, irritability, motor restlessness, urination problems, or tachycardia (American Botanical Council, 2000). In higher doses, a marked increase in blood pressure may occur, cardiac arrhythmia, or the creation of a dependency (American Botanical Council, 2000).
Prolonged usage is to be avoided in cases of potential dependency, abuse, or appearance of kidney stones (Brinker, 2010). It should be avoided also in glaucoma, stomach ulcers, children under age 6, impaired cerebral blood flow, kidney failure, pregnancy, nursing, organic heart disease or cardiovascular imbalances, within 24 hours prior to surgery, diabetes, thyroid disease, or in cases of bulimia, which is due to how the alpha-adrenegic activity of alkaloids contracts the bladder and prostate (Brinker, 2010).
Ephedra may also interact with other drugs. This is known to occur when combined with guanethidine (sympathomimetic effect being enhanced), MAO-inhibitors (enhancement of ephedrine’s sympathomimetic action); cardiac glycosides or halothane (heart rhythm disturbance); or oxytocin or secale alkaloid derivatives (hypertension development) (American Botanical Council, 2000).
In Chinese medicine, ephedra is contraindicated in cases of hypertension or yin deficiency (Tierra, 1988). In Ayurveda, ephedra is traditionally not used when heat signs are present or when the vata dosha is disturbed and ephedra in general is perceived as injurious to bodily fluids (Pole, 2013).
