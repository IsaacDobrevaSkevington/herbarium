Ceylon cinnamon

Cinnamomum verum (syn. C. zeylanicum)

Geographic Distribution

Sri Lanka, southern India, Southeast Asia, Central America, Madagascar, and Africa

Botanical Description

Cinnamon was not cultivated until 1776 (Grieve, 1931/1971). The plant originally grew in tropical forests on the island of Sri Lanka in a wild state. However, the bulk of the bark gathered today comes from cultivated plants (Ambroziak, 2018). The plant is a tropical evergreen tree that grows to 26-59 feet (8-18 meters); the pruned shrub has soft reddish, aromatic brown bark, and clusters of yellow flowers (Chevallier, 1996). Today the plant is widely cultivated in the tropics (Chevallier, 1996).

Key Constituents

Tannins, cinnamaldehyde, eugenol, trans-cinnamic acid, phenolic compounds, catechins, monoterpenes, sesquiterpenes, gum, mucilage, resin, starch, sugars, and coumarin (Bruneton, 1995; Hänsel et al., 1992; Leung & Foster, 1996; Newall et al., 1996; Wichtl & Bisset, 1994).

Sustainability

Seek fair-trade Ceylon cinnamon to ensure that the farmers and harvesters who grow and process the herb are paid fairly.

Harvesting Guidelines

Cinnamon farmers grow the unpruned tree for 2-5 years and then prune it very harshly so that a shrub, rather than a tree, grows (Ambroziak, 2018; Cinnamon, n.d.). Twice a year, after the rainy season, farmers harvest the cinnamon. The farmers cut the smaller shoots first; these are covered and left to ferment before the outer bark is stripped off. The inner bark of the new shoots is gathered every second year (West Coast Institute of Aromatherapy, n.d.). The softer, inner bark is rubbed down and peeled off from the twig. Workers stack the bark, which curls naturally as it dries to form cigar-like “quills” or cinnamon sticks (Cinnamon, n.d.). On the lower portion of the tree, where the more flavorful and older bark can be found, the bark is scraped off and used to make ground cinnamon.

Uses

Cinnamon bark is a beloved herb in the herbal toolkit for its pungent and slightly sweet taste as well as its warming and stimulating nature, and is used in both whole form as cinnamon sticks as well as powdered form in kitchens and herbal apothecaries across the globe. Its tannins lend to its astringent nature, while its mucilage content provides a demulcent action, and it is often used as a harmonizer,  catalyst, and flavor enhancer in herbal formulations. Cinnamon is especially valued for digestive, cardiovascular, and respiratory support, as well as for female reproductive concerns.

Cinnamon bark has been used for several thousand years in traditional Eastern and Western herbalism (Leung & Foster, 1996). It is an important spice and ancient herbal preparation that was first described in the Torah (Chevallier, 1996). Traditionally used for warming in energetically cold conditions and for digestive complaints, cinnamon has been used in Egypt from 2000 BCE and parts of Europe from 500 BCE (Chevallier, 1996). 

The culinary cinnamon spice purchased in the United States could be one of two types. “True” cinnamon or Ceylon cinnamon (Cinnamomum verum, sometimes known by the synonym C. zeylanicum) has a light brown color, is softer, easier to grind, and very expensive (FSSAI, n.d.). The second type is the darker-colored, reddish cassia cinnamon (Cinnamomum aromaticum, sometimes known by the synonym C. cassia) (FSSAI, n.d.). Most supermarket cinnamon is most likely cassia, not true cinnamon, as it is much less expensive. Sometimes a blend of the two cinnamon types is sold. (Please refer to the separate monograph for cassia cinnamon).

Cinnamon is used as a circulatory stimulant to encourage peripheral blood flow to the hands and feet (Chevallier, 1996). Cinnamon is also regarded as a safe and effective herb for primary dysmenorrhea (Jaafarpour et al., 2015). Herbalists employ cinnamon’s astringent action to control excess menstrual flow and postpartum bleeding (Easley & Horne, 2016). However, more research is recommended to study the efficacy of cinnamon on reducing menstrual bleeding (Jaafarpour et al., 2015). 

Cinnamon is used in multi-herb cough, cold, and fever formulas for its antimicrobial, diaphoretic, and demulcent actions. Its stimulating nature moves circulation to the periphery, warming the body, yet ultimately cooling a fever. Its demulcent nature soothes irritated tissues irritated by sore throat or cough, and helps movement of stuck mucus and congestion with its warming and moistening actions.

As a digestive stimulant and carminative, cinnamon bark helps reduce bloating, gas, and other digestive discomforts such as dyspepsia, nausea, flatulent colic, and spastic conditions of the gastrointestinal tract (Bruneton, 1995; Easley & Horne, 2016; Karnick, 1994; Newall et al., 1996; Wichtl & Bisset, 1994). 

There have been many studies to identify modern applications for cinnamon looking at its traditional use as well as in vitro and preclinical in vivo studies. Ranasinghe et al. (2013) conducted a systematic review of the literature and concluded that the available evidence suggests that C. verum has many beneficial health effects. However, since data on humans are sparse, randomized controlled trials in humans are necessary to better establish whether the effects have public health implications.

Gunawardena et al. (2015) examined the anti-inflammatory activity of C. verum and identified e-cinnamaldehyde and o-methoxycinnamaldehyde as the most active compounds, and other research has supported the herb’s anti-inflammatory and antioxidant capacity (Baker et al., 2013).

Hyperglycemia after a meal is a known risk factor for the development of several health disorders including type 2 diabetes and cardiovascular diseases. Cinnamon has been studied in randomized controlled trials (RCTs) for its glycemic-lowering effects, but studies have been undertaken with small population samples and the results have been inconclusive and conflicting (Allen et al., 2013; Leach & Kumar, 2012). Cinnamon extracts have been reputed to help manage blood glucose by reducing carbohydrate digestion. However, their effects on inhibiting digestion of carbohydrates have been poorly analyzed. Beejmohun et al. (2014) investigated the acute effect of a specific Ceylon cinnamon hydro-alcoholic extract on carbohydrate digestion and post-meal blood glucose reduction and found that this may provide a natural and safe solution for controlling blood glucose after a meal. A review of published clinical trials by Akilen et al. (2012) concluded that use of cinnamon had a beneficial effect on glycemic control, although the researchers included both Ceylon and cassia cinnamon in their review. 

Magistrelli (2010) points out that previous studies have used both ground and aqueous forms of cinnamon (e.g., Cinnulin, a proprietary water-soluble extract of Cinnamomum burmannii), both of which have demonstrated health benefits. A study by Magistrelli & Chezem (2012) compared declines in the postprandial glycemic response in average-weight and obese subjects after ingestion of 6 grams of ground cinnamon. The results of this study suggest cinnamon may be effective in moderating postprandial glucose response in both average-weight and obese adults, but once again the type of cinnamon used is not described (Magistrelli & Chezem, 2012). A brief review of the literature demonstrates that there is evidence supporting the possible efficacy of both Ceylon cinnamon and cassia cinnamon and their extracts, but there is still much research to be done in this area. Reports of cinnamon enhancing glucose metabolism need to be carefully scrutinized to determine which species was used in the research. 

In another meta-analysis of randomized, placebo-controlled clinical trials, Akilen et al. (2013)  reviewed and evaluated the effect of short-term administration of cinnamon, species not identified, on blood pressure regulation in those with prediabetes and type 2 diabetes. Although the results suggested that cinnamon has some lowering effects on blood pressure, a larger randomized, placebo-controlled clinical trial is needed to better understand the effect of cinnamon on blood pressure control among those with type 2 diabetes mellitus (Akilen et al., 2013). Cinnamon bark has also shown strong lipolytic (ability to hydrolyze fats) action (Leung & Foster, 1996), but the species of cinnamon was not stated in the research.

Both bark and leaf essential oils are produced by distillation and are used for fragrance and flavoring (West Coast Institute of Aromatherapy, n.d.). Both oils have strong aromas and should be used sparingly. The primary constituent of cinnamon bark oil is cinnamaldehyde, while the primary constituent of cinnamon leaf oil is eugenol (Tisserand & Young, 2014), which is why their applications and safety properties are slightly different. Cinnamon bark oil should not be applied to the skin or mucosa as it may cause irritation; it is also a strong sensitizer (Tisserand & Young, 2014) and is warming and stimulating (Ody, 1993). 

Cinnamon extracts and essential oils have demonstrated strong antibacterial and antifungal activities in vitro (Bhawana, 2013; Bruneton, 1995; Ranasinghe et al., 2013;). The essential oil from Cinnamomum verum leaves was tested for activity against Candida as well as its effect on acrylic resin in removable dentures; the substance tested had a satisfactory level of safety and tolerability, supporting new advances involving the clinical use of essential oil from C. verum leaves (Oliveira et al., 2014). 

Adult Dose

Cut or powdered bark: 2g/day; higher doses may cause gastrointestinal upset (Easley & Horne, 2016).

Infusion or decoction: 0.7-1.3 g in 150 mL water, 3x/day (Blumenthal et al., 2000).

Fluid extract: 0.7-1.3 mL (1:1) 3x/day (Blumenthal et al., 2000).

Tincture: 30-60 drops (1:5, 60% alcohol, 5% glycerin) up to 3x/day (Easley & Horne, 2016).

Safety

Cinnamon may provoke allergic or sensitization reactions of the skin or mucous membranes (European Medicines Agency [EMA], 2011; Gardner & McGuffin, 2013). Some individuals who are allergic to balsam of Peru (Myroxylon balsamum) might experience allergic reactions to cinnamon; cross-reactivity is common as the two plants share similar compounds (Gardner & McGuffin, 2013). While culinary use is generally considered safe, avoid larger doses during pregnancy except under the supervision of a healthcare practitioner due to its action as an emmenagogue; its safety during lactation has not been determined (EMA, 2011; Gardner & McGuffin, 2013). 

Cinnamon is also contraindicated in cases of gastrointestinal ulcers and compromised livers (Blumenthal et al., 2000). The Food Safety and Standards Authority of India (FSSAI, n.d.) suggests that reports of toxicity may be due to higher levels of coumarins in different types of cinnamon. Ceylon cinnamon contains very low levels of coumarin. Cassia cinnamon (Cinnamomum aromaticum), on the other hand, contains high levels of coumarin and large amounts of this should not be consumed (FSSAI, n.d.). Very high quantities of cassia cinnamon may be toxic and are discussed separately in the cassia monograph.

Many people do not suggest the topical use of cinnamon bark essential oil as it may irritate the skin and mucosa and is a strong sensitizer. Although not suggested for children, Tisserand and Young (2014) suggest 0.07% as a dermal maximum concentration for cinnamon bark essential oil and up to 0.6% for cinnamon leaf essential oil.
