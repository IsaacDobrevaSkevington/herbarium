Poke

Phytolacca americana (formerly Phytolacca decandra)

Native To

Mexico and most of North America with the exception of areas of central and western Canada and the following states: Alaska, Colorado, Hawaii, Idaho, Montana, Nevada, North and South Dakota, Utah, and Wyoming (Balogh & Juhász, 2008; Royal Botanic Gardens Kew [RBGK], n.d.a; Woolum et al., 2022).

Geographic Distribution

It was introduced to Europe in the 17th century, to China in 1935, and can now be found in Europe, Asia, Australia, and New Zealand (Balogh & Juhász, 2008; Bailly, 2021).

Botanical Description

This perennial herb grows 3-7 meters tall with features of large, alternating, ovate-lanceolate green leaves (Nienaber & Thieret, 2024). The leaves of this plant are green and emit a somewhat fetish odor when damaged that some describe as similar to the smell of horseradish. The stem of this plant increases its red tones as the plant ages, and while this plant lacks true flower petals, five sepals of greenish white, pink, or purple coloring unfurl on a pink raceme. This plant typically grows with ten stamens, a pistil with carpels, seeds that are lenticular in shape, and berries that start off as green and become a deep purple to almost black color as they mature, appearing in opposite clusters; the berries mature in late summer and early autumn (Nienaber & Thieret, 2024; Slattery, 2005).

Poke flowers from July through the early fall season (Abbey, 2022), and much of the pigment is attributed to the strong alkaloid content in the plant (Hoffmann, 2003). This plant regrows annually from a fleshy taproot which grows deep, spreads horizontally, and whose crown can grow to upwards of approximately six inches in diameter, in many ways resembling the root of ginseng. The stem of this plant appears trunk-like but hollows as it matures. Each berry contains about nine seeds, and pokeweed can produce several tens of thousands of seeds, per plant. The seeds are the only way this plant proliferates and they can stay viable for decades, contributing further to the plant’s hardy nature (Sellers & Ferrell, 2021).

Key Constituents

Roots: unsaturated sterol, triterpenol and triterpenoids, esters (fatty acid), saponin, starch, gum, oxalate, potassium nitrate, hemicellulose, and phytolaccatoxin (Krochmal & LeQuesne, 1970), phytolaccagenin (sapogenin) (Krochmal & LeQuesne, 1970; Bailly, 2021; Marciano, 2011), GABA, resin, phytolaccic acid, mucilage proteins, tannins), phytolaccanin, lectins, phytolaccosides (Marciano, 2011)

Whole plant part: (triterpene) pokeweed saponins (Nienaber & Thieret, 2024; Xu et al., 2023), phytolaccatoxin, phytolaccin, histamines, and oxalic acid (Nienaber & Thieret, 2024), phytolaccine (Xu et al., 2023), glycosidase (pokeweed antiviral protein) (Zhabokritsky et al., 2014)

Sustainability

This plant has a weedy and hardy nature and can grow well in both disturbed and undisturbed areas. Poke thrives in forests, in pastures, under power lines, on roadsides, in damp fields, near fence rows, in open or edge habitats, and in spaces where birds congregate (Taylor, n.d.; Slattery, 2005). While it can be beneficial to many animals, particularly birds, it is considered a high-risk, invasive species and can pose a threat to plants due to its ability to displace the biodiversity of native species (Xu et al., 2023). Wild harvesting of it is encouraged over intentional propagation. Moreover, this plant contains toxins that are harmful to many animals, including humans, if ingested in a raw or improperly prepared manner. Given that its roots can quickly grow to a large size, it can be difficult to oust once it has taken hold in a habitat (Abbey, 2022). Interestingly, this herb exhibits molluscicidal properties and may help to control populations of freshwater snails (Krochmal & LeQuesne, 1970).

Harvesting Guidelines

Given the toxins in this plant, great caution must be used when harvesting poke. There are documented cases of animals who have been poisoned with raw ingestion of this herb as well as closely related herb species (Valle et al., 2016; Beressa et al., 2020). It is suggested to harvest this plant using gloves in early spring, as this is when the leaves and sprouts are young with less potency of toxins; one should ensure that the poke is thoroughly washed, preferably with warm water. “Green Deane” Jordan, author and respected ‘master forager,’ suggests harvesting spring shoots that are less than six inches (Ziedrich, 2020; Jordan, 2022). This herb is considered a hyperaccumulator, and uptakes many heavy metals from the soil, so one should consider the soil quality as well when wild harvesting (Bailly, 2021).

Uses

Formerly known as Phytolacca decandra, poke is an herb so memorable that it has appeared in several famous songs, including “Poke Salad Annie” by Tony Joe White and “Poke Salad Annie” by Elvis Presley. This plant may get its name from the Algonquian words pakon or puccoon which translates to dye, reflecting the colorful, purple berries full of juice (Balogh & Juhász, 2008; Nafici, 2014). Its Latin genus name, Phytolacca, derives from the Greek word phyton, meaning plant, and lacca, meaning crimson lake, also referring to the vibrant berries’ color and their associated liquid (Greayer, 2024). This herb traditionally has been used as a dye, such as by the Pennsylvania Dutch for both ink and coloring wine (Krochmal & LeQuesne, 1970). Given its alternate spelling of “polk,” this plant was worn by supporters of James K. Polk during his campaign as 11th president of the United States (Greayer, 2024). Though considered a plant with herbal uses, the actions of poke are so diverse that they include other practical uses as well; an example of this is that the dye from mature berries can be used to coat fiber-based solar cells, thereby increasing the cells’ ability to convert solar energy into electricity (Navarrete-Tindall, 2011).

It is important to note that this herb has toxins throughout the plant that can result in a variety of adverse effects, if ingested without proper preparation. The root, notably, should never be ingested under any circumstances. The effects can range from gastrointestinal in nature such as diarrhea, vomiting, intestinal cramping, and internal bleeding, to cardiac symptoms of rapid heartbeat and low blood pressure, to neurological conditions such as convulsions and generalized weakness, to even death if consumed in high enough doses (Gardner & McGuffin, 2013; Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center, 2021). These effects are typically resolved with washing and cooking the shoots and young leaves several times thoroughly to remove the toxins. Despite the various toxins in all parts of this plant, it has been eaten throughout the years in different cultures. In the United States South, the leaves and shoots of this herb are often eaten in the form of poke “sallet” or salad after careful preparation, and some use the berries to create jam after removing the seeds and ensuring that it has been thoroughly cooked for toxin removal (Nafici, 2014).

Pokeweed is used traditionally by Native Americans for a variety of conditions. It may help to simulate metabolism, treat skin irritation, treat the kidneys as a “blood purifier”, and address ringworm, speaking to its alterative properties and bitter nature (Garrett, 2003). This herb may decrease phlegm and help relax the chest in those with severe cough, potentially supporting its function as a bronchodilator, expectorant, and anti-catarrhal agent, particularly when combined in leaf form with lemon balm (Garrett, 2003). Given its anti-inflammatory properties, it is used for conditions such as arthritis, nephritis, and rheumatism (Bailly, 2021). In Appalachia, pokeberry wine is used for rheumatism (Krochmal & LeQuesne, 1970). The root of this herb even appears in the National Library of Medicine in a homeopathic product for rheumatoid arthritis pain management; the preparation also includes Aesculus hippocastanum, Caulophyllum thalictroides root, Ruta graveolens flowering top, and silicon dioxide (National Institutes of Health [NIH], 2013). Less indicated for acute rheumatic conditions, this herb is better suited for addressing chronic rheumatism (Hoffmann, 2003). The berries of this herb can also be made into a tea or infusion that can address rheumatism as well as dysentery (Plants For A Future, n.d.; Franklin et al., 1806) when consumed at low doses.

The gastric effects of this herb are primarily as a laxative, diuretic, or purgative, depending on the dose. Poke has been described as aiding the removal of “catabolic wastes and the products of fatty degeneration” (Hoffmann, 2003). The saponins are likely responsible for this action as well as the “sudsy diarrhea” that may accompany this process as saponins help to regulate the synthesis and breakdown of fats (Woolum et al., 2022). Similar to its sister plant “Indian poke,” Phytolacca acinosa, this herb has been utilized in traditional Chinese medicine for constipation, anasarca, and ascites. While its draining function is reflected in its internal indications, it has a similar function externally on swellings and nodules (Polito et al., 2016). In the words of Eclectic physician Harvey Wickes Felter, in high doses, this plant can create “violent emeto-catharsis”, typically with accompanying nausea (Felter & Lloyd, 1909, as cited in Whelan, 2011). In the United Kingdom, pokeweed is documented as an emetic with additional properties as a narcotic, purgative, and antirheumatic agent (Krochmal & LeQuesne, 1970).

Topically, this plant can be used for several inflammatory and infectious conditions. Scientifically, this may be supported by the high potassium content in the ash of this plant, making it an ideal salve for ulcers and cancerous skin growths (Plants For A Future, n.d.). Ash can help skin with inflammation, infection, and cancer given its properties as a general antiseptic and antibacterial, and due to its ability to help regulate cells during their proliferative phase, although the research thus far has been in animal studies (Shaikh, 2009). The Spanish also use this herb for skin conditions in the form of a pomade for treating skin eruptions, rashes, and even ringworm (Krochmal & LeQuesne, 1970). According to Felter, it can also be used for chronic eczema, psoriasis, tinea capitis, and varicose ulcers (Felter & Lloyd, 1909, as cited in Whelan, 2011). In the form of a steam from a decoction of the root, this herb has been used for easing symptoms from hemorrhoids (Franklin et al., 1806), though this is not a contemporary usage of poke due in part to safety concerns. An infused oil or salve of poke root can act similarly. As described in King’s American Dispensatory, poke has historically been helpful for resolving inflammation and infections of the mouth and throat including conditions such as tracheitis, laryngitis, influenza, and any affliction in which there is sputum (Felter & Lloyd, 1909, as cited in Whelan, 2011); for this purpose, it can be used as a topical salve, drop dose application of the tincture, or homeopathic. Extract of pokeweed has even been shown to inhibit bacterial pathogens implicated in the development of periodontitis and dental caries (Patra et al., 2014).

Some of the most key homeopathic indications of this herb are its lymphatic properties, its ability to ease issues of the breast, and its affinity for the upper respiratory tract. This was suspected even back in the 19th century by physician Harvey Felter who wrote in King’s American Dispensatory that “Phytolacca acts upon the skin, the glandular structures, especially those of the mouth, throat, sexual system, and very markedly upon the mammary glands; also upon the fibrous and serous tissues, and mucous membranes of the digestive and urinary tracts” (Felter & Lloyd, 1909, p. 1473, as cited in Whelan, 2011). Its pain-easing actions are partly due to its strong lymphagogue action, making it a key ingredient in Lymphocil, a homeopathic preparation appearing in the National Library of Medicine’s DailyMed database as being indicated for lymphatic congestions and swellings (NIH, 2023). Aside from mastitis, this herb is often indicated homeopathically for upper respiratory tract symptoms such as sore throat, tonsillitis, parotitis, mumps, catarrh, and swollen glands of the neck (Hoffmann, 2003). 

Given the mild anodyne or analgesic function of this herb, the berries of the plant were traditionally eaten for easing pain, though this may more safely be done topically for conditions such as mastitis, sore nipples, and cystic breast tissue (Franklin et al., 1806; Marciano, 2011).  Some traditions advocate for swallowing the berry whole as this method of ingestion prevents the individual from crushing the toxic seed.

In more recent studies, Phytolacca americana has been examined intensely for its role in combating the human immunodeficiency virus (HIV). The pokeweed antiviral protein demonstrated ability to inhibit HIV-1 replication and its ability to stop proliferation of B-lineage Acute Lymphoblastic Leukemia blasts (Wishon, 2010). While not specifically considered to be a pathological similimum for acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS), this herb has an affinity for affecting lymphocytes, demonstrates anti-HIV activity, and conditions with similar key features of AIDS do appear in the materia medica for this herb, making it a possible consideration for this condition (Irwin, 1988). Further research demonstrates that the glycosidase pokeweed antiviral protein alters the splicing ratio of HIV-1 RNA leading to decrease in activity or regulation of virion expression, which is an essential regulatory protein in the virus for transporting more genetic information; this action is responsible for the decrease in virus protein expression (Zhabokritsky et al., 2014). An in vivo study also demonstrated the anti-HIV activity of American pokeweed in an intravaginal murine study without adverse effects, impacts to reproductive capability, or treatment-related deaths in the hosts (D’Cruz et al., 2004). 

Expanding on poke’s potential to address cancer, this potential was even suspected back in 1752 through the words of Benjamin Franklin, who described the berries appearing as “large as peas,” the skin with “crimson juice” and the juice to be “thickened by evaporation in the sun” and a “specific remedy for cancers” that “caused great pain” (Franklin et al., 1806). Pokeweed antiviral protein demonstrated efficacy to alter levels of caspase, a family of enzymes that control inflammation and cell death, demonstrating potential for both breast and colon cancer (Wishon, 2010). In vitro studies have also demonstrated the ability of pokeweed seed extract to affect apoptosis, or programmed cell death (Demirkan et al., 2022). Ghosh et al. (2013) evaluated the cytotoxicity profile of Phytolacca decandra on skin melanoma, which may be constituted in its homeopathic form for this condition. Results indicated that application of this herb, after extract from ethanol, led to a significant reduction in proliferation of A375 cells, which, per Imanis Life Sciences, are a human melanoma cell line extracted from a solid tumor of a 54-year-old female. In this experiment, Akt and Bcl2 were down-regulated, while p53, Bax, caspase 3, and cell death number were all upregulated, demonstrating cytotoxic effects on the A375 cell line without damage to the healthy peripheral blood mononuclear cells (Ghosh et al., 2013).

Pokeweed can also be used for reproductive health concerns. It is considered to be an emmenagogue, however, so should not be taken during pregnancy as it may stimulate uterine contractions (Hoffmann, 2003). The fruit, roots, and leaves of this plant can be used for fibroid growths (Iwalewa et al., 2007). Traditionally, it has also been used for oophoritis (Hoffmann, 2003). In males, it can address orchitis (Hoffmann, 2003). This may, in part, be due to its ability to address mumps, which has a common complication of orchitis, even resulting in infertility in 30% of cases (Wu et al., 2021). 

While there are many traditional indications for this herb and some research on its constituents via in vitro studies, scientific findings in vivo on humans are limited. This is largely due to its toxicity potential, despite several murine studies and human cell line studies demonstrating safety at controlled doses (D’Cruz et al., 2004). The lack of data in clinical trials is unfortunate, as pharmacological studies have demonstrated the benefit of this herb and to some extent, its safety profile, particularly regarding its ability to address inflammatory conditions. An expanded look at the bioactivity and toxicology of this plant and its extracts would help expand safety understanding (Bailly, 2021). Inevitably, more research is needed to better understand the safety and efficacy of Phytolacca americana on humans for various conditions.  If one considers utilizing this herb, it should be carefully prepared and cautiously administered at a low dose under the guidance of an advanced level practitioner.

Adult Dose

Poke is a low-dose herb to be used under guidance of an experienced practitioner.

Decoction: 8 fl oz (made with 1/4 teaspoon dried root per 8 fl oz water, brought to a boil then simmered 15 minutes) up to 3x/day (Hoffmann, 2003; Marciano, 2011). Note that poke in this amount is for short term use only. If any side effects are felt, adjust the dose immediately.

Food: When safely parboiled, the young green shoots of this herb can be eaten cooked (see Harvesting Guidelines). This is done through several rounds of blanching where the herb is boiled for 20-30 minutes, with the water being changed at least two times, though other foragers suggest boiling the shoots for 1 minute with a water change and then for another 15 minutes with fresh water (Ziedrich, 2020).

Infused Oil: Wearing gloves, wash the fresh, spring- or fall-harvested root. Chop into pieces. Allow to air dry, fully. Add root pieces to a jar and add oil to fully cover roots. Olive oil is suggested to prevent rancidity. Allow to sit for six weeks, adding oil as needed to ensure roots stay covered. Remove roots, leaving infused oil in a jar (Wood, 2023). Oil can then be applied to the unbroken skin of the affected area as needed.

Poultice: Thoroughly wash poke root and crush or juice until soft. Wrap in a wet compress on the unbroken skin of the affected area (Hoffmann, 2003; Whelan, 2011). It should be noted that direct contact of this plant in fresh form can cause erythema, blistering, and adverse reactions in certain individuals (Marciano, 2011), so it is suggested to spot test a small patch of skin first.

Salve: Grate 1 tbsp of beeswax per 1 ounce of infused pokeweed oil. Warm oil on a low temperature setting and add grated beeswax, stirring until fully melted. Once fully mixed, pour into a jar and allow to cool. Salve can then be applied to the unbroken skin of the affected area as needed (Wood, 2023).

Tincture: 0.2 mL (1:10, 45%) 3x/day; max dose of 10 mL per week (Marciano, 2011; Hoffmann, 2003).

Safety

Given that each part of poke can be poisonous due to its saponin, oxalate, and/or alkaloid content, with the root and seeds being the strongest in toxicity and the berries (containing the seeds) the least, poke is considered a low-dose herb and should only be used in carefully prepared and low-dose quantities as large doses may cause severe illness and even death (Sellers & Ferrell, 2021). According to the American Herbal Products Association, internal use of poke should be avoided, unless under the supervision of a qualified practitioner; higher than the suggested drop doses can cause nausea, vomiting, intestinal cramping, diarrhea, and other symptoms (Gardner & McGuffin, 2013). Poke is contraindicated in pregnancy; during lactation, it should not be applied topically to the nipple (Gardner & McGuffin, 2013); and use is heavily cautioned against in young children (Hoffmann, 2003). 

Given poke’s ability to uptake metals from the soil, soil quality is another consideration when contemplating safety of this plant (Bailly, 2021).
