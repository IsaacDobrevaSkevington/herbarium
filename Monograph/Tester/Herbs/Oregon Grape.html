Oregon Grape

Berberis aquifolium (syn. Mahonia aquifolium)

Common name
Oregon grape, Oregon grape root, mountain holly, mountain grape, mahonia, creeping barberry, herba de sangre, holly grape, Oregon root
Family
Berberidaceae
Parts used
Roots, inner stem bark (primary), leaves, and berries.

Native To

Native to regions of North America and found in mountainous areas, slopes, canyons, coniferous forests, oak woodland, chaparral, and open sites (Foster & Hobbs, 2002).

Geographic Distribution

 Berberis species are distributed from Alaska to Northern Mexico and east to the eastern edges of the Rocky Mountains (Drum, n.d.).

Botanical Description

The name Oregon grape applies to several members of the Berberis genus (formerly classified as Mahonia spp.; see explanation in Uses section) including B. aquifolium, B. repens, B. nervosa, and B. pinnata. B. aquifolium is a native self-fertile evergreen shrub, between 3-6 feet high (Moore, 1993). It has an informal, irregular form with upright stems with limited branching with suckers forming colonies. The plant has a deep vertical taproot and a network of fine roots in the upper layer. B. aquifolium is the larger of the species with five to nine leaflets per leaf, with each leaflet having one central vein. Leaves are alternate, compound, leathery, and glossy, with reddish-bronze new growth and purplish-bronze in winter (Foster & Hobbs, 2002). Berries appear from August to October and may remain on the plant until winter. The seeds ripen from August to September. Flowers are bright yellow, insect-pollinated, and honey-scented in terminal racemes. B. nervosa, also known as dwarf or dull Oregon grape can be found in lower elevation forest understories, as well as higher second-growth forests, while B. aquifolium prefers full sun (Davis, 2017). Kloos (2017) describes B. nervosa as having nine to nineteen oval to lance-shaped leaves with sawtooth edges, which are dull in color as opposed to the shine of B. aquifolium leaves. It has yellow flowers in dense racemes at branch tips (Foster & Hobbs, 2002) that bloom from early spring to early summer, followed by purplish-blue, sour-tasting berries (Kloos, 2017). B. repens is a smaller version of the former two. It has wavy, ivy-like leaves that resemble its root system, and a cluster of yellow flowers (Moore, 1993). B. pinnata is a much stouter low shrub with densely compound leaves resembling B. aquifolium. Moore (1993) says that the leaves of B. pinnata are the spiniest of the four species and if growing in adverse conditions have higher levels of berberine.

Key Constituents

Root and stem: Isoquinoline and benzylisoquinoline alkaloids: berberine, berbamine, oxyancanthine, canadine, mahonine, magnoflorine, and jatrorrhizine (Kane, 2017; Moore, 1993; Salmón, 2020).  

Leaves: Alkaloids: isocorydine, corydine, thaliporphine, glaucine (Salmón, 2020)

Sustainability

AT-RISK PLANT. SEEK CULTIVATED SOURCES OR ALTERNATIVE PLANTS.

United Plant Savers (UpS) includes Oregon grape on its “At-Risk” list, indicating that cultivated sources of the plant should be used whenever possible (UpS, n.d.). This is especially true for Oregon grape root, the harvest of which has a larger ecological impact than the stem bark and the part of the plant most commonly found in commerce. Also consider the use of alternative plants as a way to conserve wild populations of Oregon grape. One analogue herb to consider is barberry (Berberis spp.), which has a similar concentration of the antimicrobial alkaloid berberine; other alternatives include dandelion (Taraxacum officinale) and yellow dock (Rumex crispus), which have similar liver-stimulating and digestive properties (Drum, 2000). 

With the increase of trade in herbs, issues of sustainability and adulteration of herbs is a pressing topic. When purchasing Oregon grape roots and stems in bulk, look for bright orange-yellow color and strongly bitter taste (Rose, 2008). Note that Oregon grape has been used in the adulteration of goldenseal (Hydrastis canadensis) based on its lower price point, berberine content, and root color (Tims, 2016). 

Harvesting Guidelines

Due to sustainability concerns, cultivating Oregon grape is encouraged. If opting to harvest wild plants, which is not recommended, a more sustainable option is to limit harvest to only the inner bark of woody stems rather than the roots. When harvesting the root from cultivated plants, gently pull the stem until you can cut the root free or until it pulls away; the crown of the plant can then be replanted (Kloss, 2017). The harvesting and processing of Oregon grape is determined by which part of the plant is used. The root and stem of Oregon grape can be used fresh or dried for tincturing, making decoctions or oil infusions. Oregon grape is primarily composed of alkaloids, which are heat sensitive and therefore tincturing is often the preferred option (Green, 2000). Berberine is found in the yellow inner bark of the root and lower stem. Berberine is extractable in aqueous and water-alcohol solutions (Drum, n.d.).  Green (2000) mentions, as do other herbalists, that breaking down the roots into small pieces helps to expose their softer internal tissues and hasten extraction. Soon after harvesting and while the plant is still flexible, rub the outer bark off the root and stem, using the back of a knife or scraper. Chop the root and stem into ½” to 1” pieces, which contain the white inner core (Kloos, 2017) or peel the yellow inner bark with a small knife, avoiding the white inner root (Davis, 2017). If the roots and stems are to be dried, it is essential to dry slowly in a paper bag or on screens away from the sun (Davis, 2017). Another option for drying is to use a dehydrator set to between 95 and 115 degrees Fahrenheit, unless you live in a humid climate, where you may want to set your temperature as high as 125 degrees Fahrenheit (Van Doren, 2016). Once the roots and stems are dry, they will last several years if stored in the dark.

Roots and stems of Oregon grape can be collected from mid-summer to winter (Moore, 1993) before the plant has flowered or after it has fruited. Leaves are collected May through mid-fall (Moore, 1993). For those harvesters who observe the phases of the moon, harvest the bark during the three-quarter waning moon (Green, 2000). Berries are collected when ripe and used fresh or dried. 

Uses

Many Western herbalists revere Oregon grape as a significant North American herb. Its use and visibility greatly increased when it was designated as the state flower of Oregon in 1899. Oregon grape is a common name that is used to describe several different Berberis species. Barberry (Berberis vulgaris), another highly regarded herb, is often thought of as North America’s premier source of berberine, yet Oregon grape contains similar properties. In fact, some Western herbalists view Oregon grape as a counterpart to the Northeastern U.S. barberry (Tierra, 1980). Various Berberis species are used in Chinese medicine and in Ayurveda, as well as by Native American tribes, and were historically used by the Eclectics. It is a valuable and indispensable plant in our current materia medica. Known best for their health supporting properties, Oregon grape berries are also made into a delicious jam or wine. Various parts of the plant are used for dyeing fabric and fiber, while the leaves are used in floral arrangements as a substitute for holly leaves.   

There is considerable confusion about whether Mahonia or Berberis is the correct binomial for Oregon grape in the literature. Berberis was synonymous with barberry, with which Oregon grape shares similarities. The name Mahonia originated from the Irish-American botanist Bernard MacMahon (Garran, 2008). Davis (2017) explains that this confusion comes from the initial classification of several Oregon grape species based on leaf morphology. Davis (2017) further points out that this confusion was due to hybridization of the plants. Presently, this distinction is deemed immaterial, and botanists now advocate categorization of Oregon grape under the Berberis genus. Although botanists have defended this move, there is still considerable confusion with many recently published books listing Oregon grape under Mahonia. The USDA Natural Resources Conservation Service’s Plants Database (n.d.) lists Berberidaceae as the family, Mahonia as the genus, and aquifolium as the species. The U.S. National Plant Germplasm System (2008), on the other hand, lists Oregon grape as the genus Berberis. In regards to its common name, Herbs of Commerce, the industry standard for botanical nomenclature identifies Oregon grape as the common name (AHPA, 2010).  

Originally, herbal knowledge of the use of Oregon grape comes from Indigenous people. Based on collected ethnographic information, we know that Native American tribes including the Blackfoot, Cherokee, Swinomish, Karuk, Okanagan-Colville, Samish, Sanpoil, Squaxin, and Thompson utilize the roots and branches of all species of Oregon grape for addressing a variety of ailments. Dr. Enrique Salmón (2020), Indigenous Rarámuri ethnobotanist who primarily discusses the similar species M. repens and M. fremontii, rather than M. aquifolium, notes that an infusion of Oregon grape is drunk to address coughs, dysentery, hemorrhage, and kidney issues, as well as to support the delivery of the afterbirth following labor. The Cherokee people use a decoction of the roots for kidney support, to stop internal bleeding, as a gastrointestinal aid, and for headaches (Salmón, 2020)..  The raw or roasted fruit is also used as a food or drink by the Blackfoot, Cheyenne, Hualapai, and other Indigenous people and the bark, stem, and leaves are used as a dye for fabrics (Davis, 2017; Salmón, 2020). Indigenous Native American tribes in Washington state, where Oregon grape grows profusely, use both B. aquifolium and B. nervosa for a variety of conditions similar to other Native American tribes (Gunther, 1973). Gunther, an ethnobotanist and passionate supporter of Indigenous people’s rights, describes how the Swinomish and Samish tribes make a tea of the root for a general tonic and the Sqauxin use a decoction as a gargle for sore throats (Gunther, 1973). Both tribes drink a decoction of the roots for a spring tonic to purify the blood. The use of B. nervosa by Native American tribes includes eating the berries for shellfish poisoning, though with caution, based upon the perception that Oregon grape was very potent (Pojar & MacKinnon, 1994). Western settlers on the frontier learned of the value of Oregon grape from Native Americans and incorporated it into their home preparations. A popular beverage marketed in the late 1800s was made from Oregon grape and used to address jaundice (Kowalchik et al., 1987).

Later on, Oregon grape gained popularity when the Eclectics hailed it for its beneficial attributes. The Eclectics, a group of healers representing a branch of American medicine in the latter part of the 19th and first half of the 20th century, employed botanicals in their practice. The Eclectics became familiar with Oregon grape when it received increased notice in a publication entitled “New Preparations” published in Detroit in 1877 by Parke, Davis, and Company (Brinker, 2003). Dr. J.H. Bundy, an Eclectic physician in Colusa, California, provided information for the publication based on a paper he had written on Berberis aquifolium in addressing syphilis, which at the time, was one of the leading national public health concerns before the advent of antibiotics (Felter & Lloyd, 1898). In an article in an 1882 publication, “New Remedies,” Mr. H.B. Parsons announced the presence of berberine and oxyacanthine in Oregon grape (Wood et al., 1894). In an article in “New Preparations” published around 1900, Oregon grape was reported to improve extreme cases of psoriasis (Brinker, 2005). In the 1894 Dispensatory of the United States of America (Wood et al., 1894), Oregon grape berries and root were reportedly used by clinicians for a range of conditions including as an alterative, laxative, tonic, and diuretic. Anecdotal evidence indicated that it was useful in chronic scaly skin diseases, convalescence from malaria and other fevers, and in cases of chronic uterine conditions (Wood et al., 1894). Oregon grape was thought to have a distinctive action upon the liver, making it valuable in supporting those with chronic liver issues. In 1905 to 1950, Berberis aquifolium officially became recognized in the United States Pharmacopeia.  

Concurrently with the resurgence of herbalism in the 1960s back to nature movement in the United States, there followed a renewed interest in Oregon grape. Dr. John R. Christopher (1976), a naturopathic doctor and writer wrote, “Oregon grape is one of the best blood purifiers and liver stimulants. Its actions improve appetite, promote digestion, improve absorption of nutrients and increased strength and vitality. It also beneficially influences the bowel (gentle tonic-cathartic) and genito-urinary tract, and is very healing to the lymphatic and skin tissues” (para. 4). Dr. Christopher (1976) suggested the following combination of herbs for syphilis: Oregon grape, red clover (Trifolium pratense), burdock (Arctium lappa) seeds, Cascara sagrada, blue flag (Iris versicolor), prickly ash (Zanthoxylum americanum) and bloodroot (Sanguinaria canadensis). This formula was based on his classification of it as a blood cleanser or “blood sweetener” (Christopher, n.d., para. 3).

Berberine, a primary chemical constituent in Oregon grape, is a yellow-staining plant alkaloid that is present in the roots, rhizomes, and stem bark of many plants. Berberine-containing plants have been used throughout the world including in Chinese and Ayurvedic herbalism (Birdsall & Kelly, 1997). Several other plants that contain berberine include goldenseal (Hydrastis canadensis), barberry (Berberis vulgaris), and Chinese goldthread (Coptis chinensis). It is thought that berberine has a similar beneficial role across these various plant species. Davis (2017) speculates that the level of berberine in Oregon grape ranges somewhere between the 2.5-6% found in goldenseal and the 13% found in barberry. Herbalist Ryan Drum (n.d.) advances the theory that the alkaloids present in Oregon grape protect the stems and rhizomes against attack from microbial and fungal invasion. Drum (n.d.) goes on to state that water and alcohol extracts of Oregon grape have demonstrated broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity in humans against a host of viruses, bacteria, fungi, and protozoa, echoing the same sentiments voiced by Dr. Christopher decades earlier.  

Oregon grape’s taste is primarily bitter, and as such, it has both historical and contemporary uses as a bitter tonic to stimulate digestion, helping to break down and assist in the absorption of fats and oils. Kane (2017) proposes that Oregon grape assists in the digestion of food in the stomach and small intestine by its ability to stimulate hydrochloric acid, pepsinogen, and bile. Moore (1993) suggests taking 15 to 20 drops of Oregon grape tincture in a little water before eating to stimulate salivary secretions. Moore (1993) states “An easy rule of thumb is the following: if you have indigestion regularly, have teeth or gum problems and a white or yellowish coat on your tongue in the morning, use the herb as a bitter tonic” (p. 194). Oregon grape is often used in cases of sluggish digestion with symptoms of reflux, constipation, and bloating (Tierra, 1980), confirming its use by some Indigenous Native American people for digestive disturbances. Kloss (2017) describes Oregon grape’s ability to alter internal mucosa, which has the effect of removing waste products in the blood. The Eclectics also spoke of Oregon grape as stimulating to the digestion and increasing the absorption of food, “stimulating waste and repair” (Elllingwood, 1919, p. 293). Oregon grape and other berberine-containing herbs are used by many herbalists in combination with other carminatives in digestive bitter formulas. Kane (2017) suggests combining Oregon grape with wild iris (Iris missouriensis), which is also indicated in cases of sluggish digestion. Davis (2017) advocates that moderate to high doses of Oregon grape bark and root can be used with doses ranging as high as 5 mL TID (3x/day) depending on the condition, noting that gastrointestinal tract infections might require this high of a dose.  

The presence of berberine also means that Oregon grape may play a role in inhibiting pathogenic gastrointestinal microbes (Kane, 2017). Clinically, Oregon grape is used to address pathogenic microbes in cases of food poisoning, Giardia infection, amebiasis or other microbial/parasitic conditions (Kane, 2017). Drum (n.d.) describes using extracts of Oregon grape for acute and chronic cases of Giardia, stating that Oregon grape does not kill Giardia cells, but renders the intestinal environment unfriendly to their continued existence. Kane (2017) speculates that Oregon grape is effective in cases of food poisoning by inhibiting microbes and the harmful effect of related endotoxins.

Throughout the literature, Oregon grape, known for its mild laxative qualities, is usedo in cases of chronic constipation (Hoffmann, 2003). For cases of chronic constipation, Dr. Christopher (1976) suggests combining 2 parts of Oregon grape with 1/2 part Cascara sagrada. Tierra (n.d.) mentions Oregon grape’s use for cases when there is both constipation and diarrhea, particularly in the case of chronic dysentery, or in instances of damp heat with alternating dry stools or yellowish, foul-smelling watery stools. The astringent action of Oregon grape suggests that it can be used in cases of traveler’s diarrhea (Cech, 2000). In general, plants containing berberine have been found to be useful in a range of instances including in cases of bacterial diarrhea, intestinal parasites, and for their anti-inflammatory qualities (Birdsall & Kelly, 1997).  

Oregon grape is long known for its ability to address chronic scaly skin conditions (Hoffmann, 2003), including psoriasis and eczema. Dr. Douglas Schar (n.d.), who has written extensively on his personal clinical experience with Oregon grape, noted, “If I had to keep one herb around for chronic skin disease, this would be the one.” Oregon grape’s modern usage parallels its historical use in cases of intractable moist eczema, dandruff, acne, and pruritus (Elllingwood, 1919). Kane (2017) points to Oregon grape’s ability to slow excessive cellular proliferation and lipid peroxidation in cases of psoriasis. Moore (1993) suggests combining Oregon grape with buckbean (Menyanthes trifoliata) in cases of dry or scaly skin, eczema, or psoriasis combined with arthritis. The concurrent use of Oregon grape internally and externally in cases of systemic skin conditions might improve results further (Kane, 2017). Further evidence of Oregon grape root’s effectiveness for skin diseases was corroborated by Brinker (2005) who published a compilation of Eclectic case histories pointing to the success of using Oregon grape extracts in reticent cases of psoriasis. Brinker (2005) noted that topical use of Oregon grape for psoriasis has two advantages in that it allows direct contact of the alkaloids to affected tissues and addresses compliance issues based on its bitter taste. In a report compiled from three clinical trials of patients using topically applied 10% Berberis aquifolium cream for psoriasis, the findings suggest positive reactions (Gulliver & Donsky, 2005). In another randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial with 200 subjects, a topical cream containing a homeopathic dose of B. aquifolium extract was used. Subjects were given directions to apply the cream 2x/day for 12 weeks. The results indicated that there were significant reductions in psoriasis with the use of the cream containing Oregon grape compared to the control group, with infrequent side effects (Bernstein et al., 2006).  

Due to its disinfectant and antimicrobial properties, Oregon grape is often used in topical preparations for abrasions (Moore, 1993). Kane (2017) indicates that topical use of Oregon grape addresses a wide variety of conditions including nail fungi, and that internal use addresses bacterial or mold-induced sinus infections. Oregon grape is useful in damp heat conditions, including cases of inflamed skin eruptions such as athlete’s foot, ringworm, and jock itch (Kloos, 2017). Bone & Mills (2013) explain that Oregon grape’s berberine content is proven effective against specific bacteria including E. coli and MRSA, along with an antimycotic action against Candida and Aspergillus. Davis (2017) suggests using a topical wash with Oregon grape for staph infections and MRSA. Garran (2008) suggests using a decoction of Oregon grape in a sitz bath or as a douche for cases of vaginal conditions characterized by damp heat.

Oregon grape, like other plants with berberine, has a specific effect on the liver. Its bitter taste has a stimulating action upon the liver and can be helpful for those with constitutional impaired hepatic function (Moore, 1993). Many herbalists view Oregon grape as a gentle hepatic-biliary stimulant that increases the flow of bile through the liver and gallbladder (Tierra, 1980), enhancing its blood detoxification properties. Kane (2017) writes about its ability to normalize liver enzyme elevations and the inflammatory response in association with hepatitis, cirrhosis, and liver toxicity. Kane (2017) speculates that Oregon grape’s hepatoprotective properties stem from its influence on the liver’s cytochrome P450 pathway, pointing towards its alterative action. Tierra (2003) also confirms this use and suggests combining it with dandelion (Taraxacum officinale) and fennel (Foeniculum vulgare) for cases of jaundice, enlargement of the liver and spleen, gallstones, and arthritis. Kane (2017) suggests combining Oregon grape with desert parsley (Lomatium dissectum) to assist in supporting liver function.

Traditionally, Oregon grape was known for its ability to improve “bad blood.” It is considered an alterative in traditional herbalism and known as a blood purifier. Tierra (2003) notes that alterative herbs help to improve or alter the body’s ability to function by increasing blood flow to the tissues, thereby detoxifying the blood, particularly in cases of infections, inflammation, arthritis, cancer, and skin eruptions. Members of the Squaxin tribe reported drinking a decoction of Oregon grape in the spring to purify the blood (Gunther, 1973). Kane states that Oregon grape was viewed as a classic alterative to address “bad blood” comparable to both echinacea (Echinacea spp.) and stillingia (Stillingia sylvatica). Some Spanish people use Oregon grape, known as yerba de la sangre or “herb of the blood,” for cases of syphilis. Tierra (2003) confirms the use of Oregon grape for its detoxifying actions to assist in clearing the body of waste products and toxins. Tierra (n.d.) suggests using Oregon grape in cases where there is a yellow coat near the base of the tongue, which typically indicates damp heat. 

Kane (2017) suggests that Oregon grape could play a role to support those with fibrocystic breast disease or uterine fibroids due to its role in the detoxification of blood. The use of Oregon grape for cleansing the blood is also discussed by Garran (2008), who has written extensively on Oregon grape. Garran (2008) classified it in the Chinese Medicine category of “clearing heat” and “draining damp”. Garran (2008) justifies this classification upon the use of other berberine-containing herbs in Chinese medicine and his own clinical experience. Rose (2008) concurs with its use in situations of damp heat, specifically for someone with halitosis, gas, constipation, sluggish digestion, tiredness, and little appetite. Garran (2008) offers, “Oregon grape’s bitter and cooling nature drains and cools heat while draining damp for the middle burner (jiao).” He further suggests combining Oregon grape with lavender (Lavandula spp.) and gentian (Gentiana lutea) for cases of bloating, foul gas, belching, and a thick yellow tongue coat. Garran (2008) goes on to suggest using Oregon grape, the whole root including the outer bark, in cases of yin deficient heat, much like the use of Phellodendron amurense (huang bai) in Chinese medicine.  Yin deficient heat is characterized by a malar flush, mental restlessness, night sweating, five-palm heat, and a low-grade or afternoon fever (Maciocia, 1989). Tierra (n.d.) also described using Oregon grape,combined with marshmallow (Althaea officinalis), to address cases of yin deficient heat. 

Herbalists use Oregon grape for a variety of other conditions. Tilgner (1999) refers explicitly to its use for internalized heat, referencing its use in headaches that start in the occipital area and spread over the right side of the head to the eyes, with temporal throbbing, pointing to its effect on the liver. Tierra (1980) combines Oregon grape with chaste berry (Vitex agnus-castus), cramp bark (Viburnum opulus), dang gui (Angelica sinensis), and ginger (Zingiber officinalis) to balance premenopausal symptoms, again pointing to Oregon grape’s effect on the liver. Kane (2017) points to Oregon grapes broad anti-inflammatory actions and suggests its use in internal febrile states, including in cases of autoimmune inflammation, chronic allergies, psoriatic arthritis, and lupus. The scientific literature has  expounded on the use of berberine to include its applicability in metabolic syndrome, and type 2 diabetes (Schor, 2012). It has also been demonstrated to have antitumor qualities (Salmón, 2020). As research continues, evidence of the usefulness of berberine in addressing diseases is quickly expanding.  

In promising new research, findings suggest that berberine may play a positive role in addressing Alzheimer’s disease and improving cardiovascular health. Several studies are exploring the role that berberine might have in limiting the formation of amyloid plaques and neurofibrillary tangles, factors implicated in Alzheimer’s disease (Cai et al., 2016). As further research is undertaken on the potential neuroprotective role of berberine, there is speculation that it might apply to other neurological disorders. Still other research indicates that berberine has positive cardioprotective effects in cases of hypertension and hypertrophy (Li et al., 2014). In one study, berberine had the effect of inhibiting atherosclerosis related to regulating lipids, inflammation, and blood sugar (Wu et al., 2010). 

The bioavailability and absorption of berberine is a debated topic in herbalism. Drum (n.d.) points to the limited discussion in the literature on the poor absorption of berberine. As the role of gut microbiota is increasingly understood, there is some question of how this might impact the absorption of berberine. One study indicates that a healthy gut microbiome plays a role in the absorption of berberine and that the intake of antibiotics can decrease its absorption, stating “[g]ut microbiota is thought to reduce berberines (BBR) into its absorbable form of dhBBR, which then oxidizes back to BBR after absorption in the intestine tissues and enters the blood” (Feng et al., 2015). The bioavailability and absorption of dietary supplements is a growing field of research. Bio-enhancers are substances that have been found to assist with the absorption of phytochemicals; for example, piperine (contained in black pepper) is believed to enhance the absorption of curcumin. Another preliminary study indicates that an active compound in milk thistle (Silybum marianum) (silybin) could be a bio-enhancer for berberine, but further research is needed (Di Pierro et al., 2013).    

Brinker (2005) suggests increasing Oregon grape’s palatability and therefore client compliance by combining it with equal parts of a tincture of yerba santa (Eriodictyon californicum), based upon an older official pharmaceutical preparation. There are many other more palatable herbs that can help mask the bitterness of Oregon grape including mints and licorice (Glycyrrhiza uralensis), and/or the addition of 10% glycerin.

The leaves of Oregon grape are also used topically. The leaves can be powdered and used as an antimicrobial for skin abrasion (Moore, 1993). An infusion of the leaves can be made into a tea to be used as a wash for mouth sores or wounds. Salmón (2020) mentions the Cherokee people use Oregon grape root on boils, cuts, and wounds, while an infusion or poultice of the leaves as a general analgesic for bodyaches or rheumatic pains. The leaves can also be infused into oil, then made into a salve. Moore (1993) suggests combining 3 ounces of Oregon grape leaves with 1.5 fluid ounces of 95% alcohol to soak for 2 hours. Combine the wet leaves and 21 fluid ounces of oil in a blender, pulsating until you have a thick mixture. The mixture is strained for use as is as an oil or made into salve. Rose (2008) suggests combining Oregon grape leaves with alder (Alnus rubra) and mugwort (Artemisia vulgaris) leaves in an all-purpose salve.   

Adult Dose

Decoction: 3-9 g up to 2-3x/day (Tierra, 2003). 

Tincture: 10-30 drops fresh plant (1:2, 60%) or dried plant (1:5 70%) up to 3x/day (Tierra, 1980). 

Capsule: 1-2 capsules (00) up to 2-3x/day (Kane, 2017). 

Fluid extract: 10-20 drops (1:1, 60%) up to 2-3x/day (Kane, 2017). 

Glycerite: 0.4-1 tsp (dried root 1:5) up to 3x/day (Easley & Horne, 2016).

Safety

Oregon grape is contraindicated in cases of either emaciation or weak digestion. Use with caution during pregnancy (Easley & Horne, 2016). Berberine, a constituent found in Oregon grape, can cause hemolysis in babies with G6PD deficiency (Kane, 2017). Like other cholagogues, do not use this plant if there is a gallbladder blockage (Kane, 2017). Other contraindications include lactation, bile-duct obstruction, septic cholecystitis, hepatic cancer, other hepatocellular diseases, hyperthyroidism, and acute inflammation (Alfs, 2003). Information from a systematic review of scientific literature indicates that berberine is found to decrease the efficacy of many herbs, supplements, and drugs (Berberine, 2008).
