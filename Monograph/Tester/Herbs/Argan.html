Argan

Argania spinosa

Geographic Distribution

Argan is endemic to Morocco (Khallouki et al., 2005) and Algeria (Kechebar et al., 2013).

Botanical Description

This thorny evergreen can grow up to 8-10 meters in height (Orwa et al., 2009) and has a lifespan of 200-250 years (Khallouki et al., 2005). The green, hairless, tough, leathery leaves are small, approximately 2 cm long and 8 mm wide; they are clustered and oblong-spatulate to oblanceolate (Morton & Voss, 1987). The clusters of axillary hermaphrodite flowers are 5 mm wide, greenish-yellow with a bell-shaped corolla, and deeply 5 lobed, with two bracts per flower (Morton & Voss, 1987). Argan’s flowering period is generally between May and June, but this can vary depending on drought conditions (Khallouki et al., 2005).

The fruit has a thick epicarpis and is green when young and yellow when ripe; the flesh is bitter and gummy, and the fleshy mesocarp and endocarp contain latex (Khallouki et al., 2005). Each fruit contains 1-3 nuts, which are very hard shelled, oval, and brown;  generally, they are 2 cm long with 1-3 almond shaped kernels (Khallouki et al., 2005). Argan is a hardwood, hence the name “Moroccan ironwood” (Morton & Voss, 1987). It is impenetrable by insects and grows in clusters or interlaced stems (Khallouki et al., 2005), with a main trunk circumference of up to 6m (Morton & Voss, 1987).

Argan lives in very arid environments and is incredibly adapted to drought conditions. It has become known for its ability to withstand very dry ecosystems, particularly by its observed ability to go dormant for several years in severe drought years; in this instance, this resilient tree sheds all of its leaves when moisture becomes sparse and refoliates as soon as it senses ambient moisture which is enough to encourage it to push out new leaves (Khallouki et al., 2005). 

Key Constituents

Argan oil contains triacylglycerols, fatty acids (stearic, oleic, palmitic, and linoleic acids), triterpenes (squalene), sterols, vitamin E, carotenoids, and phenolic compounds (including tyrosol and vanillic, ferulic, and syringic acids) (Khallouki et al., 2003; Khallouki et al., 2005).

Sustainability

The geographical area where Argania spinosa is most prominent houses 1-3 million people, and over 2 million people make their living directly or indirectly from the argan tree (Charrouf & Guillaume, 2011). Argan has been under some stress for the last 70 years mostly due to unsustainable use, land development for agriculture, and severe droughts (Guillaume & Charrouf, 2011). The value of argan has always been known to the rural population, and this tree has been protected through customary law since the early 1900s (Morse, 2003). Argan has suffered enough degradation that between 1960 and 1980, the number of trees per hectare decreased from 100 to 30 trees (Morse, 2003).

In 1984, a cooperative process was created when collaboration between villages in argan forests and Moroccan researchers found ways to protect argan trees by means of promoting the market value of the oil made from the kernels (Charrouf, 1995; Dossa, 2011; Morse, 2003). In an attempt to alleviate deforestation by creating an economic interdependence between the tree and the people to protect both the argan forests and generate income for rural women, this model gave the possibility for Amazigh (indigenous people of Morocco) women to create a sustainable income and have the infrastructure needed to continue protecting the argan forests. Through cooperation between the inhabitants of the argan region, non-governmental organizations (NGOs), and governmental branches, the development of protocols to protect areas from exploitation continues (Dossa, 2011; Morse, 2003).

In 1998, the United Nations Educations, Scientific, and Cultural Organization (UNESCO) protected the argan forest by declaring it a biosphere reserve (Guillaume & Charrouf, 2011). Interestingly, argan does not figure as of yet in the IUCN red list as a species of concern of endangerment (International Union for Conservation of Nature, 2021).

Harvesting Guidelines

The tree begins to bear fruit between 5-6 years of age, with the maximum production of fruit occurring around 60 years of age (Khallouki et al., 2005). July through August is usually the height of harvesting season (Morse, 2003). However, the tree may have a second flowering during the fall, which can extend the harvesting period into October (Morton & Voss, 1987). There is a clear relationship between the tree and goats; herds of goats climb the tree to eat the foliage and fruits (Morton & Voss, 1987). It is widely mentioned that the nut has traditionally been gathered after goats or camels have eaten the fruit and expectorated the seeds; however, this practice is uncommon today and has been replaced by gathering the fallen fruit from under the tree (Morse, 2003).

Traditionally, the oil is extracted solely by Amazigh women (Charrouf & Guillaume, 2011). The first step after harvest is peeling the fresh, or dried argan fruit removing the pulp to get at the nut (Charrouf & Guillaume, 1999). Once the almond-like nut is extracted, it is crushed between two stones to extract the seeds or kernels, which are the part of the fruit used for the production of oil; the seeds are left to dry in the sun in a clay plate (El Monfalouti et al., 2010) and ground into a paste by means of a traditional stone hand mill (Bellakhdar, 1997). The paste is then hand mixed with the addition of tepid water for several minutes and yields a nutty-tasting oil; traditionally, salt is added for the preservation of the oil (Bellakhdar, 1997).

This traditional process takes approximately 10 hours to yield 1 liter of oil (Bellakhdar, 1997; Charouf & Guillaume, 1999; Khallouki et al., 2005). Mechanical presses have been introduced for easier and faster extraction, yielding 4-6 liters in 13 hours by a single person (El Monfalouti et al., 2010). The traditional extraction, however, is still very common. The use of solvent for extraction is most common in the mass production of cosmetic-grade oil extraction; in this case, a volatile lipophilic solvent such as chloroform or acetone is often used to extract cosmetic-grade oil (Charrouf & Guillaume, 1999).

Uses

Argan is one of the best examples of the intrinsic ethnobotanical relationship between people and plants. This endemic species is restricted to the south-central Atlas Mountains regions of Morocco and has been used for centuries in herbalism and as a staple food, cosmetic, fodder, and fuel (Khallouki et al., 2005). The name argan derives from the Amazigh word arjân meaning to be “locked up in a limited space,” a reference to its limited geographical region (Khallouki et al., 2005). Argania spinosa is the only known species of the tropical Sapotaceae family residing in the Mediterranean (Guillaume & Charrouf, 2011).

The argan tree itself dates back to the Tertiary Period (Guillaume & Charrouf, 2011; Khallouki et al., 2005; Morton & Voss, 1987). Steadily declining since the Ice Age, this tree was common in all of North Africa for 80 million years (Dossa, 2011). The first written record of argan dates back to 11th century in works by the Andalusian botanist Ibn al-Bayṭār (Bellakhdar, 1997).

By facilitating water infiltration of the soil, the deep roots of this tree replenish groundwater and stabilize the soil, helping to prevent erosion and desertification (Khallouki et al., 2005; Orwa et al., 2009). This process also aids in the creation of fertile soil from the moisture it pulls out and the organic matter (fruits and leaves) it drops on the ground (Kechebar et al., 2013; Khallouki et al., 2005). The shade, moisture, and fertile soil under argan trees is sometimes used to plant grain crops such as barley and wheat (le Polain de Waroux & Lambin, 2013). Various accumulative factors have led to the slow denigration of the arganeraies (argan forests), such as unprecedented long drought periods, less yield in fruit, difficult germination, overgrazing, and use for fuel (Charrouf & Guillaume, 2011, Nouaim et al., 2002).

The reforestation process of the arganeraies has been difficult due to the overharvest of seeds, which leaves none for the growth of new trees. Coppicing has been explored as a means of rejuvenating mature argan forests (Nouaim et al., 2002). Also, research conducted in Morocco demonstrated that germination was best if the nut weight was higher, and the exocarp thinner; both approaches can be used in reforestation attempts (Nouaim et al., 2002).

The known importance of argan trees has warranted many efforts to protect it. A special dahir (a traditional rural law) has been in place since 1925 in order to protect argan by managing harvest protocols, uses for fuel, and grazing rights (Charrouf & Guillaume, 2011). Today, arganeraies are usually state property, but rights for harvest are granted to local communities and regulated by customary rules (Charrouf & Guillaume, 2011; le Polain de Waroux & Lambin, 2013).

The degradation of the arganeraies and consequent concerns of erosion and desertification, as well as interest in argan oil’s traditional uses and the academic research on its pharmacological uses, were catalysts for the creation of a single species protective policy. The “triple win” model based on sustainable development included the revitalization of rural economy, the reforestation of argan trees, and the creation of rural women-led cooperatives (le Polain de Waroux & Lambin, 2013). For example, Targanine, a centralized cooperative network created in 1996, aims to educate local populations about the protection of argan and provide “the means for them to organise the traditional production of Argan oil and to develop its commercialisation” (Stussi et al., 2005, p. 38).

Researchers hope that the development of viable harvesting and processing methods as well as environmental protection will assure abundant income for rural populations and safety for argan trees for generations to come (Charrouf & Guillaume, 2011; Khallouki et al., 2005). Thus, argan is one of the best examples of the connection between the protection of an ecosystem, alleviation of poverty, and sustainable development. The benefits of this tree on a biocultural level are many and demonstrate that the relationships between people and the environment are intrinsic to the survival of both.

Used for over 900 years, argan oil is the main herbal product derived from this remarkable tree (le Polain de Waroux & Lambin, 2013). In traditional use, argan oil is considered to be fortifying, anti-inflammatory, hepatoprotective, choleretic, and supportive in cases of prediabetes; among the Amazigh, it is often consumed as a regular part of the diet (El Monfalouti et al., 2010). The oil is also used topically for acne, chicken pox scars, cuts, burns, stretch marks (particularly after pregnancy), oily skin, dry skin, aging skin, as a general restorative and emollient for optimum skin health, to promote hair brilliancy and health, and to strengthen brittle nails (Bellakhdar, 1997; El Monfalouti et al., 2010).

Research on the traditional uses of argan has been conducted for over 35 years (Charrouf & Guillaume, 2014). Although many of the traditional uses have not been scientifically researched, some new possibilities that go beyond the scope of traditional herbalism have been found.

Argan oil is twice as rich in antioxidant tocopherols as olive oil, with ratios of 620 mg/kg compared to 320 mg/kg in olive oil (Charrouf & Guillaume, 1999). This high tocopherol content has led some to give even greater acclaim to argan oil’s benefits over olive oil (El Monfalouti et al., 2010). Argan oil is also rich in squalene, a compound that has shown anticarcinogenic activity (Khallouki et al., 2003). In vitro and in vivo studies on the anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and antiatherosclerotic activities of argan oil and its constituents have led researchers to believe that it could be beneficial as a supportive measure against cancer, high cholesterol, heart disease, and obesity if consumed regularly (Cherki et al., 2006; El Monfalouti et al., 2010). However, further evidence from clinical trials are needed to confirm these effects in humans (Cherki et al., 2006; El Monfalouti et al., 2010).

Argan oil has become very popular as a base for some cosmetic creams (Guillaume & Charrouf, 2011; El Monfalouti et al., 2010), with its cosmetic uses in the international market based on traditional uses. Generally they include anti-aging, moisturizing, and anti-sebum properties for oily skin, as well as vulnerary uses (Guillaume & Charrouf, 2011). Although these uses are mostly based on traditional knowledge, there is also scientific evidence (Guillaume & Charrouf, 2011, in particular relating to argan oil’s ability to decrease excess sebum on the epidermis and improve the appearance of oily skin after 4 weeks (Dobrev, 2007).

A study by Boucetta et al. (2014) on the use of argan oil during postmenopause showed that the benefits of argan oil on skin dehydration were quite clear whether used topically or ingested. No negative side effects were found in the study participants during the 60-day period of research. To the contrary, the researchers indicated that the effects of argan oil also contributed positively to the participants’ overall health and suggested that its use may be especially beneficial during postmenopause (Boucetta et al., 2014).

Argan has also been found to have antiviral properties, particularly in what are called “press cakes.” The press cakes are a byproduct of oil extraction and are generally used as fodder. In this instance, evidence suggests that arganine C found in the press cakes should definitely be considered as a lead compound for new antivirals (Chafchaouni-Moussaoui et al., 2013). Another study investigating argan fruit pulp suggests that the camphor content of this matter could also be useful as an insect repellent (Harhar et al., 2010).

Argan oil is consumed raw, sometimes as a dip for bread; while it can be used for cooking, argan oil is not recommended for long-term frying (Yaghmur et al., 2001). One of the most delectable ways of consuming argan oil is amlou; this sweet and nutty spread is known throughout Morocco and valued by many Moroccans and is known as a staple food for the Amazighen people (Nabbouti et al., 2014). The preparations differ depending on region, but amlou is generally made from a mixture of argan oil, almond butter, and honey; in other regions, a spread called bsis is made with barley flour, argan, and honey (Bellakhdar, 1997; Nabbouti et al., 2014). Children are often given bsis as a treat known for its high nutritive properties (Bellakhdar, 1997).

There isn’t much information on the use of argan in Ayurveda. In the limited information currently available on the subject, it is suggested for vata imbalance, which can create dry and rough skin, wrinkles, fatigue, anxiety, constipation, and frizzy hair conditions  (“Argan oil,” 2020). It is also used for out-of-balance pitta with manifestations like skin inflammation, acne, boils, blemishes, microbial infections, and digestive complaints (“Argan oil,” 2020).

Having gained fame in the world as a moisturizing and anti-aging ingredient in cosmetic creams, argan oil export prices reach over $400 (United States dollars) a liter on the international market, the main recipients being Europe, Japan, and the United States (le Polain de Waroux & Lambin, 2012; le Polain de Waroux & Lambin, 2013).

There are currently many who are dedicated in ensuring a safe future for Argania spinosa, and there are many factors to consider in its protection and revitalization (Lybbert et al., 2011). It is clear that argan’s benefits are enumerable, and its global heritage and environmental benefits invaluable.

Adult Dose

Oil: 25 mL/day internally (Guillaume & Charrouf, 2011).

Topical application: Apply oil on affected area 2x/day (Bouchetta et al., 2014; Sijelmassi, 1996).

Safety

Argan oil is generally considered safe, with no known toxicity from acute or regular use; however, one case of an allergic reaction has been reported (Guillaume & Charrouf, 2011). There have also been a few reports of gastroenteritis in infants fed with the milk of argan-grazing cattle (Bellakhdar, 1997). More recently, there has been mention of cases of hypersensitivity pneumonitis in factory workers during extraction of argan cakes (Paris et al., 2015).
