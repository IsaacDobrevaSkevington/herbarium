Turmeric

Curcuma longa

Botanical Description

Turmeric is an herbaceous perennial that grows to just over 3 ft (1 m) tall, with green elliptic to lanceolate leaves that are 3.5 in (9 cm) long. The pale yellow flowers appear in short dense spikes. The root system consists of the bright orange, thick rhizomes that are generally 1-3 in (2.5-7.5 cm) in length by 0.4-0.8 in (1-2 cm) in diameter.

Key Constituents

Volatile oil, curcuminoids (including curcumin), resins.

Sustainability

None known.

Harvesting Guidelines

Often grown as an annual in temperate climates, turmeric takes 7-10 months to mature. Wait until the plant has drooped and withered before harvesting the rhizome.

Uses

Turmeric is a plant in the ginger family (Zingiberaceae), and like its cousin ginger (Zingiber officinale) has been a staple of Indian food traditions for millennia and has at least 4,000 years of use in Ayurveda, Siddha medicine, and Chinese medicine. Turmeric is known in Sanskrit as kanchani, the “golden goddess,” and other auspicious names alluding to its use in sacred ceremonies and offerings as well as reflecting a longstanding reverence for this powerful plant (Gallant, n.d.). Ayurvedic practitioners David Frawley and Vasant Lad (2001) write that turmeric emanates the energy of the divine feminine and helps grant prosperity. Curcuma takes its name from the Persian kirkum, meaning “saffron” because of its bright golden yellow hue, easily seen in the Indian curry spice blend. Its signature color is also used to add yellowness to foods such as mustard and cheese, as well as textiles. 

The part of the plant used is the rhizome, which can be consumed dried, cooked, or raw, and has a slightly peppery, mildly bitter, and earthy flavor. Curcumin is what gives turmeric its bright color and is the component most studied; therefore, perhaps unfairly, most of turmeric’s beneficial attributes are attributed to curcumin. 

Turmeric is a choleretic, cholagogue, hepatoprotective, anti-inflammatory, hypolipidemic, antimicrobial, and antioxidant herb. In Indian cuisine and Ayurvedic tradition it is used to aid digestion, particularly protein breakdown, and promote proper metabolism. Ayurvedic Doctor Vasant Lad (1984) notes that turmeric is a blood purifier (alterative) and digestive tonic, and is also soothing to respiratory challenges like asthma. It can be used as a topical paste, as a tea, in food, and boiled with milk. A gargle of warm water and turmeric powder can be used for a sore throat. It has also been used as an inhalant to ease congestion by sprinkling the dried or powdered rhizome over a fire and inhaling the fumes (Gallant, n.d.). 

In Hawaiian tradition, a gargle made from turmeric is also used as an oral aid, while a preparation of the rhizome is inhaled for nasal issues; internally, a juice of the plant is taken as a blood-cleansing alterative (Kaaiakamanu & Akina, 1922).

Most modern Westerners consider turmeric an anti-inflammatory agent, and indeed, traditionally this rhizome is often used in an ayurvedic approach to soothe respiratory infections, inflammation of the throat and tonsils, and as an anti-inflammatory herb for many other aches and pains including muscle aches, back pain, cramping, and joint pain. When it comes to the joints, turmeric improves flexibility and eases arthritic discomfort (de Jager, 2003). A randomized 2009 study examined the efficacy and safety of turmeric in individuals with knee arthritis (an inflammatory condition), concluding that turmeric was as effective and as safe as ibuprofen (Kuptniratsaikul et al., 2009). A later similar study revealed that not only was turmeric as effective for knee osteoarthritis, but that abdominal pain was significantly more common in the ibuprofen group (Kuptniratsaikul et al., 2014). The antioxidants in turmeric are believed to help neutralize the inflammatory agents called free radicals that can damage tissue and cause inflammation and pain. Knee osteoarthritis continues to be a well-studied disorder for researchers interested in studying turmeric and curcumin, and all 17 randomized controlled trials included in a 2025 systematic review found turmeric preparations to significantly reduce the pain of knee osteoarthritis (Wai et al., 2025). 

Turmeric is also a common herbal support for autoimmune conditions, especially rheumatoid arthritis (RA), at least partly because of its effect on inflammation. A 2023 meta-analysis of randomized, controlled clinical trials affirmed that turmeric and curcumin reduces levels of inflammatory markers and improves antioxidant activity (Dehzad et al., 2023a), and a 2012 pilot study reported that curcumin (used alone) proved to more effective at reducing swelling and tenderness in people with mild or moderate RA than a non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug or a combination of the two (Chandran & Goel, 2012). A more recent systematic review of clinical trials assessing curcumin’s effectiveness in managing RA symptoms affirms its use in RA management (Bagherniya et al., 2021).

Along the same lines, turmeric can help to calm the overstimulated or hypersensitive immune system that results in seasonal allergies and other forms of allergic rhinitis, potentially due to its ability to inhibit histamine release (Kurup & Barrios, 2008). In an interesting randomized controlled trial, curcuminoids with piperine or a placebo were given to young women with premenstrual syndrome and primary dysmenorrhea from 7 days before until 3 days after menstruation for three consecutive menstrual cycles due to the relationship between these imbalances and allergic disorders. The participants who were given curcuminoids with piperine had a significant reduction in serum levels of IgE, an antibody related to allergic reactions, compared to placebo (Bahrami et al., 2022).   

On the other hand, turmeric is a common ingredient in immune-stimulating folk herbal formulas. At first glance, this may seem contradictory—how can the same herb stimulate immune function and also help to calm immune hyperfunction? It may be that a more appropriate term for turmeric (and many other herbs that are considered anti-inflammatory) is inflammation modulant, due to its ability to downregulate certain proinflammatory cytokines while also enhancing the body’s response to pathogens.

Turmeric’s inflammation-modulating and antioxidant properties also benefit the cardiovascular system. Sometimes referred to as a general tonic to promote vascular health, turmeric can also benefit individuals with vascular dysfunction from diabetes, hyperlipidemia, and other cardiometabolic diseases. Turmeric’s action on the liver and its bitter flavor are also at work here. In a meta-analysis of randomized, controlled clinical trials, researchers confirmed that turmeric and curcuminoids can lower fasting blood glucose, A1c, and measures of insulin resistance in individuals with metabolic disease (Yuan et al., 2022). Another meta-analysis revealed improvements in blood levels of cholesterol and triglycerides (Dehzad et al., 2023b). 

Because it has been found that curcumin is rapidly metabolized by the liver and intestines, its beneficial properties may not be as effective unless it is consumed with black pepper, which has been found to enhance the bioavailability of the curcumin in turmeric by 2,000% (Shoba et al., 1998). Interestingly, many of the traditional recipes involving turmeric include black pepper. 

In Western herbalism, turmeric is used as a cholagogue and choleretic to stimulate production and secretion of bile, thereby supporting digestion; likewise, in Ayurveda, turmeric is a warming herb that kindles digestive fire, or agni. Like other bitter herbs that stimulate bile production, turmeric can help not only promote digestion but also ease gastrointestinal issues. A randomized, double-blind crossover study used ultrasound to examine the gallbladder after administration of curcumin in 12 healthy volunteers, with researchers concluding that curcumin induces gallbladder contraction (Rasyid & Lelo, 1999). In a double-blind, placebo controlled study in Thailand, curcumin was compared to placebo and an over-the-counter remedy for indigestion; 87% of the curcumin group had full or partial cessation of indigestion after 7 days, compared to 53% of the placebo group (Thamlikitkul et al., 1989). Two very small human studies have shown turmeric’s promise for reducing symptoms of ulcerative colitis and Crohn’s disease (Taylor & Leonard, 2011), and a review of clinical trials demonstrated that the anti-inflammatory effects of turmeric and curcumin can improve irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) symptoms (Jafarzadeh et al., 2022). 

Along with its ability to stimulate bile production in the liver, turmeric is also hepatoprotective (liver protectant). This herbal action has been validated in at least one study in humans. A randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial by Kim et al. (2013) investigated the hepatoprotective effect of fermented turmeric powder on liver function in people with elevated alanine transaminase (ALT) levels, which is associated with liver damage, hepatitis, and other liver health issues. After 12 weeks, the turmeric powder group showed a significant reduction in ALT levels compared with the placebo group. Measurements of other serum levels normalized, and there were no reported severe adverse effects or abnormalities. Researchers concluded that fermented turmeric powder is effective, safe, and generally well tolerated in those with elevated ALT levels (Kim et al., 2013). 

Curcumin has been studied extensively for its potential anticancer action, including its ability to inhibit cell growth and metastases. The consumption of turmeric is correlated epidemiologically with lower rates of cancer, including breast, prostate, lung, and colon cancers. Turmeric may help reduce the numbers of invading cancer cells and tumor size (Park & Conteas, 2010; Johnson et al., 2012).

Curcumin may also help protect against Alzheimer’s disease because it triggers a gene that codes for antioxidant production, which helps protect the brain from oxidative damage (Mishra & Palanivelu, 2008). Curcumin’s potential to halt Alzheimer’s is linked to the low incidence of the disease in India, where turmeric is consumed widely (Krishna & Amirthalingam, 2014). In 2012, a case series was published detailing three individuals with Alzheimer’s disease who were given turmeric, at least two of whom came to recognize their family again after a year of turmeric ingestion; researchers have concluded that turmeric indeed reduces plaque in the brain and improves cognitive symptoms (Hishikawa et al., 2012). 

As a vulnerary, turmeric can be prepared as a paste or mixed with honey and applied to insect bites, abrasions, itches, bruises, and other minor skin irritations. Traditionally, turmeric is used in India for skin care, blemishes, abrasions, and inflammatory skin conditions due to its antiseptic properties (Krishna & Amirthalingam, 2014; Patnaik, 1993). Research supports these benefits—turmeric’s volatile oils have been shown to have potent anti-inflammatory effects (Patnaik, 1993; Prasad & Aggarwal, 2011). A 2009 study on aqueous extract of turmeric showed good antimicrobial action against such pathogens as E. coli and two species of Staphylococcus (Niamsa & Sittiwet, 2009).

While turmeric can be taken as a tincture, many herbalists suggest following the traditional use of decocting or using in food, preferably with a bit of black pepper for its synergistic effects. Because curcumin is fat soluble, consuming turmeric with fat aids in absorption and enhances efficacy as well; this is why traditional decoctions in milk (or nondairy versions such as coconut milk) are often preferred over water decoctions.



Adult Dose

Decoction: 2-4 fl oz (1 oz dried rhizome in 1 quart milk, milk alternative of choice, or water) 3x/day (Easley & Horne, 2016).

Powder or capsules: 1-3 grams 3x/day (Easley & Horne, 2016). 

Tincture: 10-30 drops (1:5, 50%) up to 2x/day (Buhner, 2012).

Standardized extract: 1-2 g curcumin per day in divided doses (Johnson et al., 2012).

Safety

Turmeric eaten in food is considered safe. For some individuals, turmeric should not be used at high doses long term (Mills & Bone, 2005), as overuse may cause gastrointestinal disturbances (e.g., diarrhea, nausea, ulcers) in some susceptible people (Ehrlich, 2014; McIntyre, 2005). Turmeric is considered contraindicated in the case of biliary tract obstruction due to potential bile-stimulating activity, and those with a history of biliary tract obstruction should seek professional advice before using turmeric (Gardner & McGuffin, 2013; Mills & Bone, 2005); turmeric may cause colic in those with gallstones (Smith, 2009). Those with congestive heart failure should avoid turmeric (Chopra & Simon, 2000).

Because turmeric may have antiplatelet and blood-thinning activity, individuals on blood-thinning medications should exercise caution when considering use and speak with their healthcare professional or pharmacist prior to self administration (Mills & Bone, 2005). For similar reasons, it is also generally advised that individuals undergoing surgery stop consuming therapeutic (not culinary) doses of turmeric at least 2 weeks prior to their procedure (Ehrlich, 2014).

Eating foods with turmeric during pregnancy is generally considered safe; however, turmeric should not be taken in high doses during pregnancy due to its potential emmenagogue effects (Ehrlich, 2014; McIntyre, 2005).

Lastly, one small study concluded that consumption of supplemental doses of turmeric can significantly increase urinary oxalate excretion which may subsequently increase risk of kidney stone formation in susceptible individuals (Tang et al., 2008).
