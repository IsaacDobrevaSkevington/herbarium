Spilanthes

Acmella oleracea; syn. Spilanthes acmella

Native To

Spilanthes is native to Brazil where it is cultivated throughout the year as an ornamental, herbal, and culinary plant (Prachayasittikul et al., 2013).

Geographic Distribution

Acmella oleracea has been introduced and grows in equatorial regions of South America and Asia, as well as parts of Africa, Cuba, and Germany (Royal Botanic Gardens of Kew, n.d.).

Botanical Description

Spilanthes acmella is a fast-growing, erect herb with shiny, smooth, opposite leaves that may have a purple tinge. The ovate-lanceolate leaves are wedge-shaped at the base with toothed or wavy margins and a pointed tip. The conical red and yellow flower heads occur singly on terminal peduncles. The achenes are dark brown, oblong, and flattened in shape and enclosed separately in scales. Spilanthes grows as a perennial in warmer climates, but is highly frost sensitive and grows as an annual in cooler climates. It is an eye-catching addition to the garden and attracts fireflies when in bloom (Rondanelli et al., 2019). As a low-growing plant that reaches heights of 15-30 centimeters, it makes a colorful ground cover or nice border plant. It is easy to understand why one of spilanthes’ common names is eyeball plant when you see the vibrant rayless yellow flower heads with their dark orange centers peeking out above the leaves (Fedco, 2019). 

 

Key Constituents

Alkaloids (the majority being lipophilic alkylamides including spilanthol), alkylamides (acmellonate), phytosterols, triterpenoids, volatile oils (including limonene and β-caryophyllene), glycosides, tannins, phenolic compounds, sesquiterpenes (α– and β-bisabolenes and cadinenes), flavonoid glucosides, vanillic acid, coumarin, fats, fixed oil, and carbohydrates (Arora et al., 2011; Prachayasittikul et al., 2013; Rondanelli et al., 2020).

Sustainability

Acmella oleracea, known as jambu in Brazil where it is native, has always had cultural value in that region. However, it has gained popularity in the past 10 years due to the alcoholic drink Cachaça de jambu made from Acmella oleracea (Osborn, 2017). With this rise in popularity, there are a few commercial jambu plantations in Southeast Brazil. Growing jambu on a large scale threatens the genetic diversity of the plant. It has also led to the implementation of conventional agricultural practices, which threaten the traditional agroecology practices of the people who have grown jambu on a smaller, more sustainable scale (Slow Food Foundation for Biodiversity, n.d.).

Harvesting Guidelines

The flowering tops and leaves can be harvested throughout the growing season. The whole plant including the root can be dug when in flower. Leave some flowers to self-sow, and collect the seeds from others to propagate new plants. 

Uses

The name spilanthes is slightly confusing, because the plant’s modern binomial is actually Acmella oleracea. However it was previously known as Spilanthes acmella, thus giving rise to the common name spilanthes. Spilanthes acmella is now considered a synonym for Acmella oleracea. You will still find both names in use in the scientific literature, and it is helpful to know that they are referring to the same plant (Rondanelli et al., 2019).  

Spilanthes has a long history of use for a wide variety of herbal and culinary purposes throughout many tropical and subtropical parts of the world. Spilanthes was first introduced to North America from Brazil in the 1860s and was originally listed as “para cress” in American seed catalogs (Fedco, 2019). Spilanthes is now grown in many colder parts of North America as an annual.

The aerial parts of the plant including the leaves and flowering tops are most commonly used in herbalism (Skenderi, 2003). However, the entire plant, including leaves, flowers, roots, and stems, has also been used (Prachayasittikul et al., 2013). In some herbal traditions, there may be a specific focus on one part of the plant, such as the roots or the flowering tops (Dubey et al., 2013). One study showed that the highest concentration of the main active constituent of spilanthes, an alkamide called spilanthol, is found in the flowers of the plant (Dixit & Saraf, 2002).

The leaves and flowers of spilanthes are also used for culinary purposes in Brazil and Southeast Asia (Jambú, n.d.) and, more recently, in the United States and Europe (Donnelly, 2015). Zingy spilanthes leaves are a key ingredient of the northern Brazilian dish called tacacá, a hot soup made from cassava root starch, shrimp, peppers, and spilanthes (Hernandez, 2023). The flower heads of spilanthes are used as a spice in certain Japanese appetizers (Prachayasittikul et al., 2013) and in various dishes in parts of Asia. Throughout Brazil, spilanthes is prized for its herbal properties and is also used as a vegetable (Prachayasittikul et al., 2013). Spilanthes is used in sauces, soups, stews, rice, omelets, and even eaten raw in salads! It is also a popular ingredient in artisanal alcoholic drinks called cachacas (Slow Food Foundation for Biodiversity, n.d.). The fresh flower heads and leaves can also simply be chewed straight from the garden.

Anyone who has had the pleasure of munching on a spilanthes leaf or flower won’t soon forget the instantaneous tingling and saliva-inducing sensation that comes from chewing on the plant. Commonly known as toothache plant, spilanthes has been used traditionally by many different cultures to numb the mouth and address toothaches and other types of oral pain (Prachayasittikul et al., 2013).

In the northern regions of Brazil, the aerial parts of spilanthes are used as a common household herb to address oral and throat conditions (Rondanelli et al., 2019). Jambu, the traditional name for spilanthes in Brazil, has been used for centuries to ease pain, especially oral pain (Rondanelli et al., 2019).

While spilanthes is probably best known for its widespread use in easing toothaches, the plant has traditionally been used by a wide range of cultures to address everything from digestive issues and coughs to skin infections and snake bites (Prachayasittikul et al., 2013). Other important traditional uses include addressing rheumatism, malaria, tongue paralysis, and fevers (Dubey et al., 2013). Spilanthes is also used traditionally in various cultures including in India and Sri Lanka as a diuretic (Young-Chang et al., 2017) and for dissolving urinary calculi (Ratnasooriya et al., 2004). Preclinical studies have shown strong diuretic activity of the plant (Ratnasooriya et al., 2004; Yadav et al., 2011), but clinical studies have yet to confirm this action in humans.  

Topically, spilanthes makes an excellent poultice for any type of skin inflammation from insect bites to blisters or burns. Ongoing studies are investigating the use of spilanthes, and specifically the use efficacy of spilanthol, in anti-aging skincare products (Hernandez, 2023). 

Spilanthes is a very important herb to the Mising tribes, an Indigenous community inhabiting parts of the Indian states of Assam and Arunachal Pradesh (Sharma & Pegu, 2011). Special food items are prepared from a different species of spilanthes, Acmella paniculata, for religious festivals. This species is also used to ease toothaches and mouth ulcers, as well as bronchial issues and dysentery (Sharma & Pegu, 2011). A paste made from spilanthes roots is traditionally used to address throat problems in the Chhindwara and Betul districts of Madhya Pradesh, India (Vijendra & Kumar, 2010). 

Spilanthes is used to address a variety of imbalances in Chinese medicine, where it is known as tian wen cao (Zhou et al., 2011). The whole herb is used to settle coughs and asthma, as well as address the common cold, pertussis, and tuberculosis. It is also indicated for diarrhea, enteritis, malaria, sores, boils, rheumatic fever, and poisonous snake bites. Energetically, it is indicated for resolving toxins and disinhibiting dampness, while dispersing swelling and easing pain (Zhou et al., 2011).

In Ayurveda, the whole plant of spilanthes, called sarahattika, is used to address toothaches, gum and throat infections, psoriasis, scabies, and scurvy. It has also been used to calm stammering in children (Abdul Rahim et al., 2021; Dubey et al., 2013).  

In King’s American Dispensatory, Eclectic herbalists Harvey Felter and John Lloyd (1898) describe spilanthes as an acrid, aromatic sialagogue (meaning that it induces increased salivation). They suggested taking spilanthes internally for flatulence, to improve digestive function, and to dispel nausea and vomiting. While there are no scientific studies looking specifically at the digestive qualities of spilanthes, it is interesting to note that many of the cultures in places where it grows abundantly use the plant in recipes and as an appetizer (Slow Food Foundation for Biodiversity, n.d.). Perhaps the traditional use of this plant to stimulate salivation before eating also helps support digestive function. 

Felter and Lloyd (1898) also suggested using a small dose of strong tincture for “inflammations of the mouth and throat” (para. 2). They wrote about the way in which Indigenous peoples have used spilanthes in the places where it is native. These uses include: 

…gouty and rheumatic affections, in uric acid gravel, in dropsical effusions, and even to remove intestinal worms. A tincture of it placed on cotton, and introduced into the cavity of a decayed tooth, will, it has been remarked, promptly relieve toothache (Felter & Lloyd, 1898, para. 2). 

They note that at the time of their writing spilanthes was not in use in the United States (Felter & Lloyd, 1898). 

Some of the most common applications for spilanthes in Western herbalism today include its use as an antibacterial and antifungal herb (Easley & Horne, 2014). A 2011 in vitro study on the antimicrobial action of spilanthes validated many of the traditional folk uses and modern Western uses of spilanthes to address bacterial and fungal infections (Arora et al., 2011). A number of different extraction methods were used to isolate the active constituents of spilanthes in order to further study the phytochemical and antimicrobial action of spilanthes leaf extract (Arora et al., 2011). 

All of the various extracts were rich with a variety of phytoconstituents, which varied slightly depending on the menstruum used in extraction. Methanol and ethyl acetate extracts were found to contain alkaloids, glycosides, tannins and phenolic compounds, flavonoids, and carbohydrates. A petroleum extract contained many of the same phytochemicals along with triterpenoids, sterols, fats, and fixed oil. These extracts were then tested against several strains of bacteria and fungi, along with standard drugs commonly used to treat infections with those same strains of bacteria or fungi (Arora et al., 2011). 

This study showed that the methanol and ethyl acetate extract was more effective than the standard drug Doxycycline against Klebsiella pneumoniae (Arora et al., 2011), a gram-negative bacteria known to cause pneumonia, bloodstream infections, wound and surgical site infections, and meningitis (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2010). In fungal strains, the ethyl acetate and water extract were found to be more effective against Rhizopus arrhizus and Rhizopus stolonifer respectively than the standard antifungal drug Fluconazole. Researchers concluded that the presence of phytoconstituents including tannins and flavonoids provided the antimicrobial action in the various extracts of spilanthes (Arora et al., 2011). 

Spilanthes is also used in Western herbalism to help stimulate mucous membrane secretions and support the immune system during respiratory infections (Easley & Horne, 2014). It is used as a local anesthetic to ease inflammation and pain where there is an infection, including infected or painful teeth and gums. Spilanthes is very often a main ingredient in herbal formulas for dental health (Easley & Horne, 2014). 

The major active constituent found in spilanthes is an isobutylamide called spilanthol (Dubey et al., 2013). Spilanthol was first isolated from an alcoholic extract of spilanthes flowers in 1945 (Prachayasittikul et al., 2013). Chemically speaking, spilanthol is N-isobutylamide, which has a bitter taste and is thought to be the active constituent that induces salivation (Dubey et al., 2013). Spilanthol has a strong and pungent taste as well as being bitter, and is thought to strongly contribute to the astringent and anesthetic effects of spilanthes (Dubey et al., 2013). Another minor constituent called acmellonate has been isolated from spilanthes and is considered to contribute to the mouth numbing and tingling sensation experienced when chewing spilanthes, but to a lesser degree than spilanthol (Ley et al., 2006). 

Preliminary studies indicate that the analgesic action of spilanthes, which has made it such a popular folk herb for tooth pain, may be due to the presence of flavonoids in the plant that decrease inflammatory prostaglandins known to contribute to pain perception (Prachayasittikul et al., 2013). It is also believed that the specific anti-inflammatory action of the constituent spilanthol helps reduce prostaglandin production and thus lowers pain perception, contributing to spilanthes’ analgesic actions (Prachayasittikul et al., 2013). Along with the specific analgesic quality of spilanthes, this herb has also been prized more generally for its anti-inflammatory action (Dubey et al., 2013). Again, spilanthol is understood to be the main active constituent contributing to the overall anti-inflammatory action, which it does by increasing the scavenging of free radicals (Prachayasittikul et al., 2013). 

Research suggests the antinociceptive (the action of blocking the detection of painful stimuli) effect of spilanthes is related to different processes, including inhibition of prostaglandin synthesis, activation of opioidergic, serotonergic, and GABAergic systems, and anesthetic activity through blockage of voltage-gated sodium channels. This had led researchers to speculate that spilanthes has great potential for future use in pain management, particularly “in chronic degenerative diseases, where pain is a significant critical issue” (Rondanelli et al., 2019, p.1).

Not only has spilanthes proven to be effective in reducing tooth pain, but science has backed up the traditional use of spilanthes to reduce oral infections (Sathyaprasad et al., 2015). A 2015 in vitro study compared the efficacy of spilanthes and calcium hydroxide (a popularly used intracanal medicament) against common root canal pathogens. Enterococcus faecalis, Candida albicans, Staphylococcus aureus, and Streptococcus spp. were tested against different concentrations of spilanthes and calcium hydroxide. Results showed that spilanthes “possesses remarkable antibacterial and antifungal activity against common root canal pathogens which are responsible for repeated endodontic failures such as E. faecalis and C. albicans when compared with medicaments like calcium hydroxide” (Sathyaprasad et al., 2015, p. 528).

Spilanthes is often listed in the literature as an antimalarial herb and is traditionally used in parts of Africa and India for malaria (Spelman et al., 2011). A 2011 preclinical study found that indeed, spilanthes exhibits in vitro and in vivo antimalarial action, attributed mainly to the alkylamides and flavonoids present in the plant, and again, specifically the anti-inflammatory and bioactive constituent spilanthol. Interestingly, this study found that a relatively low concentration of spilanthol in a water-based extract was more effective at reducing the parasitemia (presence of parasites in the blood) than a 95% ethanol extract (Spelman et al., 2011). 

Spilanthes has also been identified as an important bioinsecticide, which may provide an ecological way of controlling insect populations (Dubey et al., 2013). In a study using cockroaches, researchers found that a direct application of spilanthol had high acute toxicity, and spilanthol was the most potent compound when compared to three conventional insecticides (Kadir et al., 1989). 

Various extraction methods of spilanthes have been found to have significant larvicidal properties against mosquitoes, which are known vectors of malaria (Dixit & Saraf, 2002). Researchers are hopeful that extracts of spilanthes may prove useful in ongoing attempts to control malaria, given that many chemical insecticides have been banned due to their harmful effects on humans and the environment (Dixit & Saraf, 2002). The spilanthol present in the ethanolic extract of spilanthes demonstrated ovicidal, larvicidal, and pupicidal action. A 7.5 ppm concentration exhibited 100% mortality of eggs, larvae, and pupae of mosquitoes in the Anopheles, Culex, and Aedes genera (Dixit & Saraf, 2002). A later study focused on developing a system for biocontrol of malaria and filarial mosquito vectors using plant-based insecticides found that out of three spilanthes species, Spilanthes acmella proved to be the most potent (Pandey et al., 2007).

Adult Dose

Infusion: 1-4 fl oz (1 g herb per each fl oz water) 1-4x/day (Easley & Horne, 2016).

Tincture: 5-30 drops fresh herb (1:2, 95%) or dried herb (1:5, 50%) 3x/day (Easley & Horne, 2016).

 

Safety

Spilanthes is generally considered to be a safe plant with no known drug interactions or adverse side effects (Gardner & McGuffin, 2013). There are no studies on the safety of the use of spilanthes during pregnancy or nursing, however, the consumption of spilanthes as a cooked vegetable and ingredient in salads in traditional south Asian cuisine provides some indication that this plant is generally considered safe (Gardner & McGuffin, 2013). There has been one case report of a healthy 42-year-old male who went to the emergency room with swollen, painful lips. The suspected cause was the ingestion of spilanthes in a culinary dish several days earlier, since no other explanation was determined (Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center, 2019).
