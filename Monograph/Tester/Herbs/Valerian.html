Valerian

Valeriana officinalis

Ways to use

CapsulesCold infusionInfusionTeaTincture
Common name
Valerian, phu, all-heal, amantilla, setwall, capon’s tail
Family
Caprifoliaceae
Chinese medicine name
Xie cao
Parts used
Rhizome, roots

Native To

Europe, parts of Asia

Geographic Distribution

Europe, Asia, North America

Botanical Description

Valerian is a perennial that can grow up to 1.2 m tall. Leaves are pinnate and divided, and flowers are pale pink-white.

Key Constituents

Alkaloids, gamma aminobutyric acid, irioids (valepotriates), volatile oils, valeric acid, sesquiterpenoids.

Sustainability

None known.

Harvesting Guidelines

Remove flowering tops as they grow to help root develop. In the fall, remove the aerial parts and dig up the rootstock.

Uses

Valerian is a beautiful, easily cultivated and hardy perennial plant with small pink flowers, and is believed by many to take its name from the Latin verb valere, which means “to be strong” or “to be well.” In ancient Greece, it was called “phu” because of its strong odor, which many people find extremely offensive and which has been described as reminiscent of dirty feet or socks and/or rotten cheese. However, the scent of valerian is truly in the nose of the beholder because despite its stinky reputation, it has been at times employed as a perfume and room freshener! While valerian has a sedating quality in most humans, cats display a similar ecstatic reaction to the scent of valerian as they do to catnip. Rodents are also said to love the scent, and valerian purportedly played a role in the legendary Pied Piper’s ability to escort large numbers of rats from the town of Hamelin.

Valerian is truly the go-to herb for normalizing the nervous system, offering neuroprotective, anxiolytic, antidepressant, anticonvulsant, and other benefits. For thousands of years, valerian has been used as a relaxant, mild sedative, soporific, and sleep aid, and is considered especially useful when muscle tension is leading to or caused by anxiety and/or an inability to sleep. These effects are attributed to the compounds valepotriates and valeric acid, two substances exclusive to valerian (Murray, 1995). In fact, evidence suggests that valerian may have been the first anticonvulsant used for epilepsy, with Italian botanist Fabio Colonna reporting in 1592 that he cured his own epilepsy with powdered valerian root (Eadie, 2004).

One clinical trial showed that during 6 weeks of treatment, valerian extract showed a comparable effect on primary insomnia as oxazepam, a benzodiazepine (Ziegler et al., 2002). Other research on valerian for insomnia has shown similar positive results, but some research has shown valerian to be either ineffective, no better than placebo, or results have been inconclusive. However, it should be noted that these studies used a wide variety of dosages and preparation methods, including unspecified “extracts” of the root, and it is not clear if traditional preparation methods were followed. Herbalist Rosemary Gladstar (1993) also points out that valerian’s efficacy is highly dose dependent, and sufficient quantities must be used in order for it to be effective.

Most herbalists agree that although it is a root, valerian should not be decocted, but rather steeped gently at a relatively low temperature to avoid destroying the volatile oils that are believed to be active constituents. Herbalist David Hoffmann (2003) even suggests preparing a cold infusion, using 1 cup of cold water to 2 teaspoons root, steeped for 8-10 hours. Author Mark Pedersen (2010) says that valerian is best extracted using alcohol or extracts fixed in oil because the active principles are so highly volatile, but Michael T. Murray, N.D. (1995) perefers the aqueous extract, because valeric acid, which is one of the constituents found to have sedative effects, is water soluble.

However you decide to prepare it, because of the volatility of its oils, valerian should be stored in an area that is not in danger of overheating. The concentration of oils in the root has also been shown to decrease over time, so it is best used as soon as possible after harvesting. Opening a good, potent container of valerian should quickly fill a room with its scent!

Valerian is used in cases of both anxiety and depression, helping to soothe frayed nerves and tension and gently lift the spirits. It is believed to normalize the nervous system, helping to sedate those who are agitated, but stimulate those who are depressed (Murray, 1995). This dual nervine action is different from another possible effect of valerian: producing a calming quality in most people, but causing nervousness in a small percentage of others. Rosemary Gladstar (1993) suggests that these folks may not have the digestive capabilities required to convert the volatile oils into their sedative properties. Because of this anomaly, make sure to try a small dose of valerian first, before taking the full dose. Also, valerian may potentiate the effects of sedatives, so it should not be combined with other sedatives unless under the guidance of a healthcare professional.

Valerian, like the antianxiety benzodiazepine drugs Xanax (alprazolam) and Valium (diazepam), appears to increase the amount of gamma aminobutyric acid (GABA) in the brain, which aids in the regulation of nerve cells and has a calming effect in cases of anxiety (Ehrlich, 2011). One review of 11 herbs found that valerian showed the most promise to address anxiety and insomnia in bipolar disorder (Baek et al., 2014). An 8-week pilot double-blind randomized trial of obsessive-compulsive disorder patients taking 765 mg of valerian or 30 mg of a placebo daily for 8 weeks found that the valerian group showed a significant difference at the end of treatment, suggesting that the root has anti-obsessive and anti-compulsive effects (Pakseresht et al., 2011).

As an antispasmodic and analgesic specifically indicated for pain with tension, valerian is not only used for tension headaches and skeletal muscle spasms, but also for uterine and intestinal cramping. Gladstar (1993) suggests valerian combined with cramp bark for menstrual cramps as well as to “ground” the often-traumatic ups and downs experienced during menopause. This suggestion is supported by research that found that valerian significantly reduced the severity and frequency of hot flashes during menopause when compared to placebo (Mirabi & Mojab, 2013).

Valerian may also be helpful for people with restless legs syndrome (RLS), which is a peculiar, crawling sensation that typically worsens when lying down, keeping sufferers tossing and turning at bedtime. Believed to be a nervous system disorder, the exact cause of RLS is unknown but is associated with various factors including iron deficiency, diabetes, and other medical conditions. Valerian may help with RLS through a combination of its action on the nervous system and its ability to relax muscles. In addition, valerian is extremely high in calcium, which is a crucial element for optimal cell signaling throughout the nervous system (Pedersen, 2011). Valerian also contains high amounts of magnesium, which helps muscles relax. A prospective, triple-blinded, randomized, placebo-controlled, parallel design study published in 2009 investigated the effects of patients with RLS taking 800 mg of valerian for 8 weeks; the results of this study found that symptoms of RLS improved and daytime sleepiness decreased, leading researchers to conclude that valerian may considered for symptom management and to improve health outcomes and quality of life in those with RLS (Cuellar & Ratcliffe, 2009).

Lastly, valerian has been shown to exhibit neuroprotective effects in vitro (Sudati et al., 2009), likely due to its high concentration of antioxidants.

Adult Dose

Infusion: 4-8 fl oz (made with 1 ounce dried root in 1 quart water) 30 minutes before bed (Easley & Horne, 2016).

Tincture: 10 drops – 3 mL fresh root (1:2, 95%) or dried root (1:5, 60%) up to 4x/day (Easley & Horne, 2016). David Hoffmann (2003) suggests that single doses of 2.5-5 mL may be needed for efficacy and that up to 10 mL can be given at one time.

Capsule: 500-1000 mg up to 4x/day (Easley & Horne, 2016).

Safety

A small percentage of people experience stimulation or anxiety when taking valerian. Valerian shouldn’t be used when driving or operating machinery. Do not take with alcohol or other sedatives. Avoid valerian when taking medications that are metabolized in the liver.
