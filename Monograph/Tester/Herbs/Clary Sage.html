Clary Sage

Salvia sclarea

Family
Lamiaceae
Parts used
Flower, seed, leaf (Grieve, 1931), root (Culpeper, 1850).

Native To

Native to southern Europe, the Mediterranean region, northern Africa, and the Middle East (Royal Botanic Gardens Kew [RBGK], n.d.).

Geographic Distribution

Clary sage has been introduced to parts of the northern United States and Canada, Germany, and Great Britain and is widely cultivated throughout many temperate regions of the world. It is currently cultivated in central Europe, Russia, England, France, Morocco, and the United States (Arctander, 1994 as cited in Battaglia, 2018; Lavabre, 1990; RBGK, n.d.).

Botanical Description

As a member of the aromatic Lamiaceae family, clary sage has a square stem. Clary sage is a biennial herb, hardy to zone 6. In its first year of growth, a clump of velvety heart-shaped leaves appears with grey tones and fine silver-white hairs. In its second year of growth, the flower stems grow to 3-4 feet tall and are topped by inflorescences of small, tubular, two-lipped flowers that are pale blue, violet-pink, or white (Missouri Botanical Garden, n.d.). Flowers bloom from June to August with large spikes of aromatic blossoms. 

Clary sage is mainly propagated by seed in  a well-drained, fertile soil; it prefers moist soil but will grow in dry conditions and responds well to full sun. Most commonly, the seeds are sown in spring, although seeds can also be sown in August or September and allowed to overwinter, which can result in larger plants. Stored clary sage seeds can maintain viability for 3 years. The plants attract butterflies and are considered deer tolerant. Cutting the flower stalks before they set seed can encourage third-year growth and limit self-seeding, which can become quite aggressive in some climates (Missouri Botanical Garden, n.d.).  

Key Constituents

β-sitosterol,  α-amyrin, ursolic acid, oleanolic acid, betulinic acid, sclareol, salvigenin, acacetin and norartocarpetin, ursolic acid, oleanolic acid, betulinic acid, salvigenin, acacetin, and norartocarpetin (Masood et al., 2017).

Volatile oil: linalyl acetate, linalool, (E)-caryophyllene, and sclareol (Kačániová et al., 2023; Peana et al., 2002; Tisserand, 2010).

Sustainability

No known sustainability issues.

Harvesting Guidelines

Harvest leaves in the second year just as the flowers are coming into bloom. For highest oil content, harvest early in the morning after the dew has dried from the plant or in the late afternoon. When making essential oil, the plant is allowed to field dry, then steam distilled into an essential oil.

Uses

Plant names often point to the beneficial actions of a plant; in the case of clary sage, the binomial name Salvia sclarea is derived from the Latin word salveo, meaning to save or heal (Missouri Botanical Garden, n.d.). The common name “clary” is derived from the Latin word clarus, which means “to clear.” The name clary was modified into “clear eye,” indicating the use of the seed in clearing eyesight.  

Although there is limited information on the historical use of clary sage, the bulk of the current information available is from the aromatherapy field where clary sage essential oil has found a loyal following (this is discussed further below). The presence of clary sage growing throughout the English hillside was noted by John Gerard, an English herbalist and author, in 1597 (Grieve, 1931). English astrological herbalist Nicholas Culpepper outlines an early account of clary sage use. Culpeper paired plants with diseases and planetary influences, and placed clary sage under the moon sign due to its moistening and emollient qualities (Culpeper, 1850).  

The most notable and frequently mentioned use of clary sage, as its name implies, is for supporting inflammation of the eye. The seeds are soaked in water, forming a thick mucilaginous gel, which is then used as an eyewash for irritated eyes or the removal of dust and debris. Traditionally, the leaves were also made into a tea that could also be used as a general eyewash. 

The aforementioned seed preparation was noted to be useful for drawing splinters and thorns from the flesh as well as to help resolve tumors and swellings (Culpeper, 1850). This drawing action, coupled with clary sage’s astringency (Grieve, 1931), seems to have been recognized for its use in multiple skin- and tissue-related concerns. In Jamaica, clary sage was considered to be cooling and cleansing for skin ulcers, and the leaves were decocted in coconut oil for use in scorpion stings (Grieve, 1931). Culpeper (1850) suggested an application of the leaves infused in vinegar to address skin inflammation and boils. Clary sage leaf was also noted for its use as a gargle in addressing sore throats and as a skin wash for wounds.

A well-known action of clary sage includes its historical use as an antispasmodic and carminative (Grieve, 1931). “It has mostly been employed in disordered states of the digestion, as a stomachic, and has also proved useful in kidney diseases” (Grieve, 1931, para. 15). This corresponds to contemporary use of the leaf infusion in supporting digestive problems such as gas and gastric spasm as well as abdominal pain and constipation (McIntyre, 1996). 

Clary sage is also used for calming and easing emotional distress, uplifting and balancing the spirit (Price & Price, 2007). Grieve (1931) suggested a combination of clary sage along with chamomile (Matricaria chamomilla) flowers, avens (Geum urbanum) root, and burdock (Arctium lappa), caraway (Carvum carvi) seed, and coriander (Coriandrum sativum) seed for “violent cases of hysteria or wind colic.” Interpreting historical references based on changes in language can be challenging in determining contemporary usage; since the term hysteria historically referred to both emotional and physical distress relating to menstruation and the “wandering womb,” Grieve’s text may point to use of clary sage as both an antispasmodic and nervine. Indeed, McIntyre (1996) also refers to clary sage as helping to lift the spirit both in those with depression and postnatal depression. 

Although the aerial parts are more commonly used, Culpeper references the use of clary sage root: “The powder of the dried root put into the nose provokes sneezing and thereby purges the head and brain of much rheum and corruption” (Culpeper, 1850, p. 50). Powdered tobacco and herbs blown up the nose became popular in Europe during the 1700s, reflecting the influence of historical use of herbal snuff by prior generations.  

According to historical references, clary sage was also used as a tonic. Culpeper (1850) describes a culinary dish made by dipping fresh clary sage leaves in a batter of flour, eggs, milk, and fried in butter. “Much use both for men and women that have weak backs and helps to strengthen the reins” (that is, the kidneys) (Culpeper, 1850, p. 50). Some herbalists also regard clary sage as a tonic and calming herb that helps ease premenstrual problems (Price & Price, 2007). Clary sage leaves were juiced and drunk after childbirth to help expel the placenta. McIntyre (1996) describes clary sage as a relaxing tonic for the nervous system, and one that can support mental and physical exhaustion in those who are convalescing.

Clary sage flower enjoys a traditional use as a substitute for hops in beer and in winemaking. The late herbalist Stephen Buhner (1998) shared a Clary Sage Ale recipe in his book, noting that the use of clary sage in beer brewing was thought to increase the inebriating quality of the beer. German winemakers were known to add clary sage along with elder (Sambucus spp.) flowers to create a substitute muscatel, as evidenced by its common name, muscatel sage. Culpeper (1850) issued a warning about mixing wine and clary sage: “the seeds or leaves taken in wine, provokes to venery.” Liqueurs, vermouth, and Rhine wines also benefit from clary sage’s strongly aromatic flavor profile (Nicolescu et al., 2026). 

Its aromatic qualities take center stage when used in the many dishes it is commonly incorporated into in European, North African, and central Asian traditions: eggs, soups, pastries, sweets, salads, puddings, relishes, sauces, and beverages (Nicolescu et al., 2026). Clary sage leaves enhance a delicious rice, vegetable, and herbs dish called sarma which is Indigenous to Turkey and the Balkan islands (Dogan et al., 2015). 

Clary sage is similar in some ways to its familiar cousin, common sage (Salvia officinalis), but the two are not entirely interchangeable. While both sages are considered energetically warming, common sage has a slightly cooler energy than clary sage. Both sages have a bitter flavor, and are used to support digestion due to their bitter and carminative qualities. Clary sage and common sage leaves are both used in cooking, though common sage adds a much stronger pungent and aromatic flavor, especially when dried, as compared to the milder flavor of clary sage. Both common and clary sage have astringent properties that lend them to topical applications and tissue support, though common sage is more strongly astringent than clary sage. Common sage and clary sage have historically been used for muscular tension associated with menstrual issues; the warming quality of common sage may be useful in helping to address amenorrhea and dysmenorrhea, while clary sage has antispasmodic actions that help ease menstrual cramping, a use which has been confirmed in preliminary murine studies (Wong et al., 2020). 

The uplifting, intoxicating aroma of clary sage lends itself to use in spiritual practices, and in some magical traditions, clary sage is associated with visionary states. It is said to help enhance the ability to dream and remember, connecting one with the dream world and enhancing meditative presence. 

Salvia species are widely distributed throughout the Middle East, northern Africa, and central Asia, with clary sage (known as dağçayı and paskulak in the Middle East and shalfey muskatnyy in Central Asia) specifically having an extensive history of use throughout these regions (Nicolescu et al., 2026). Besides its value as a flavorful aromatic, clary sage leaf and decoction is known in its homelands to have a wide range of traditional uses, from respiratory support during colds and flus, to easing digestive complaints such as constipation and ulcers, to soothing nervous system concerns from headaches to epilepsy (Eminagaoglu et al., 2017; Nicolescu et al., 2026). In Europe, clary sage decoction, infusion, and essential oil have commonly been used to support similar body systems, in addition to circulatory, urinary tract, and endocrine system issues. Topically, its traditional use is primarily to address wounds, warts, and sunburns, while its effects on the musculoskeletal system give credence to its appearance in protocols for osteomyelitis and arthritis, according to ethnobotanical researchers Nicolescu et al. (2026). In South Africa, clary sage decoction is particularly heralded for swelling or sores on the skin, but also for stomach discomfort or diarrhea (Nicolescu et al., 2026). Clary sage extract also has a history of traditional use in addressing gingivitis and canker sores, and shows potential for use with periodontits in preliminary murine models (Kostić et al., 2017). 

Nanoparticles from aqueous extracts of clary sage have been shown to have antibacterial (Gram-positive moreso than Gram-negative), anticandidal, and scolidal properties, indicating clary sage’s potential role in certain surgical applications (Zarei et al., 2025).

Today, clary sage is often used as an essential oil for internal and external consumption. While in general, essential oils should only be used internally when under the guidance of an experienced aromatherapis, clary sage essential oil is classified as generally safe for human consumption (GRAS) by the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) which approves its use as an additive in food.

A randomized, placebo-controlled trial of 72 individuals with diabetic foot ulcers and associated symptoms of depression found that inhalation of clary sage essential oil or a linalyl acetate derivative alleviated stress, anxiety, and depression at various levels; while the essential oil was most helpful in mild depression, linalyl acetate was most effective for moderate to severe depression (Son et al., 2025). Other studies have shown clary sage essential oil’s antidiabetic effects on subchronic and acute diabetes (Raafat & Habib, 2018), though further research is needed.  

Clary sage essential oil contains the ester linalyl acetate; esters are known to help balance and soothe the sympathetic nervous system (Emnett, n.d.). Peter Holmes (1993) describes that the euphoric effect of clary sage essential oil is particularly suited to addressing depression arising from burnout. In a clinical study, the use of inhaled clary sage essential oil was found to alleviate depression and stress during menopause, resulting in decreased cortisol levels and increased levels of serotonin (Lee et al., 2014).

Numerous claims indicate that clary sage essential oil has estrogen-stimulating action. It is thought to regulate hormonal output by promoting efficiency of the endocrine glands themselves (Price & Price, 2007). Aromatherapist Robert Tisserand reviewed the purported estrogenic properties of the constituent sclareol. He concluded that “based on its structure, sclareol is unlikely to have any estrogenic action” (Tisserand, 2010, para. 18). However, as Tisserand points out, the lack of estrogenic activity doesn’t mean clary sage essential oil isn’t useful—simply that any beneficial effect is unlikely to be related to its effect on estrogen levels. Clary sage is used for promoting menstruation, easing menstrual cramping and premenstrual moodiness, increasing libido, hormone regulation, and support during menopause (Emnett, n.d.). A clinical trial found that abdominal massage using diluted clary sage essential oil, along with lavender (Lavandula spp.) and marjoram (Origanum majorana) oil, was effective in reducing menstrual cramps in patients with primary dysmenorrhea (Ou et al., 2012). Clary sage essential oil can be hypotensive; while it is sometimes used for aromatherapeutic support to assist with contractions during labor and delivery, it may not be appropriate to use in case of excessive blood loss or hemorrhage (Price & Price, 2007; Wallbank, 2009).

Clary sage essential oil’s anti-inflammatory, antimicrobial, antibacterial, insecticidal, and antifungal activities have also been researched (Kačániová et al., 2023). Individual compounds in clary sage and the complete essential oil have shown in vitro activity against pathogenic Staphylococcus bacteria (Różalski et al., 2007; Sienkiewicz et al., 2015), and esters found in clary sage essential oil are considered antifungal. Its chemical constituents linalyl acetate and linalool are also identified as having anti-inflammatory activity (Peana et al., 2002).

Adult Dose

Dried herb: 4-6 g/day (van Wyk & Wink, 2017).

Essential oil: Aromatherapy: 2-3 drops in a diffuser. Skincare: 6 drops in 1 fl oz of carrier oil (Wessels, 2017).

Safety

Clary sage plant is generally recognized as safe for human consumption as both a natural seasoning/flavoring and as a plant extract (Simon, 1984). There is a dearth of general information on the safety of clary sage for children; however, in The Nursing Mother’s Herbal, clary sage leaf and stem receives a safety rating of ‘A’ for safety (Humphrey, 2003). Due to its uterine stimulating properties, it should not be used in pregnancy except under the guidance of an experienced practitioner. Clary sage has moderate interactions with chloral hydrate and hexobarbitone and can reportedly potentiate narcotic effects (Carey, 2010). 

Clary sage essential oil should be diluted in a carrier oil before topical application; clary sage absolute should be diluted to 0.25% or less for use on skin to avoid skin sensitization and adverse reactions (Tisserand & Young, 2014). The oil is classified as generally safe for human consumption (GRAS) by the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) whichand approves its use as an additive in food. (Note that in general, essential oils should only be used internally when under the guidance of an experienced aromatherapist.)
