Honeysuckle

Lonicera japonica; Lonicera periclymenum; Lonicera spp.

Geographic Distribution

Asian honeysuckle, the main species of which is Lonicera japonica, is cultivated in Taiwan, China, Korea, and Japan (Reid, 1995). It is now present in at least 38 states . (Yancey, 2014). Various other native species of bush honeysuckle are found in the Americas, and in Europe the native species most often used, Lonicera periclymenum, is also found as far south as Northern Africa, as far north as Sweden, and as an exotic in Canada as well as in the U.S. state of Maine (NatureServe, 2017).

Botanical Description

Lonicera japonica, the Asian honeysuckle that is most commonly utilized in herbal medicine, grows in a vine form that can strangle nearby plants and is semi-evergreen (Yancey, 2014). It is, in fact, a perennial woody vine. Of the two native European honeysuckles, the one that seems to have been most employed in herbal traditions, Lonicera periclymenum, is twining and climbing. The native species of Lonicera often found in North America, however, grow in a bushy form (up to 20 ft. in height).

The oval leaves of honeysuckle are opposite, smooth or hairy, and may vary in shape. They are dark green on the upper sides. The fragrant flowers, which are tubular and two-lipped, may vary from white to yellow to pink, and they present themselves in clustered form at the end of the plant’s shoots. The blossoms are attractive to birds, mice, and moths, and the nectar is edible for humans as well.

The diameter of the berries (black for “bush” honeysuckle and orange to red in color for the Japanese) may be between 1/5 to 1/2 inches in size (Yancey, 2014).

In the southern areas of U.S. honeysuckle can be evergreen (Munger, 2002) and may elsewhere be persistent into winter (Yancey, 2014).
Native American honeysuckles will have solid rather than hollow stems and generally do not pose an invasive threat (Yancey, 2014).

Key Constituents

Flavonoids, triterpenoids, iridoids, volatile oils (Wang et al., 2016); inositol, organic acids, tannin, trace elements (Zhao et al., 2017); luteolin (Tierra, 1988); saponins (Reid, 1995).

Sustainability

Asian honeysuckle species are an invasive exotic in the U.S. and have been estimated to have now impacted at least 38 states (Yancey, 2014). It is therefore important to distinguish carefully between these invasive species and native ones. Honeysuckle is often controlled through a variety of chemicals and herbicides (Yancey, 2014). As of 2002, Asian honeysuckle was prohibited in some places for sale, such as in the state of Illinois (Munger, 2002).

At the same time, Asian honeysuckle is a key species during scarce periods for white-tailed deer, in some places contributing nearly 50% of their diets, and it also supplements the diets of various birds (Munger, 2002).

Harvesting Guidelines

Stems and leaves of the Asian honeysuckle Lonicera japonica are harvested in autumn and winter in small bundles, while the flowers are harvested in spring, generally May through June (Foster & Chongxi, 1992).

The flowers are picked while still in bud form when free of dew. They are traditionally dried in the sun—not the shade—on the same day as the picking, without moving or turning; if there is damp present in the environment or if it is a rainy day, they are baked at a low temperature or dried indoors to prevent blackening—and in this second method, must be turned periodically throughout the day (Foster & Chongxi, 1992).

Flowers are gently sifted to remove leaves or debris and if the preparation calls for it, they are “charcoaled” by stir-frying them until black outside, with water being sprinkled on them during the cooking in order to keep them from burning at too fast of a rate (Foster & Chongxi, 1992).
The stems are washed and soaked in water for a little while, then wrapped around with damp cloth until all is moist, before being cut down to smaller size and dried in the sun (Foster & Chongxi, 1992).

Ingesting the fruit, a recent trend in folk use, involves gathering the fruit either the last 10 days of September or first 10 days of October and then stir-frying them until hot and sticky to the touch (Foster & Chongxi, 1992). See below under Safety.

Uses

Both delighted in for its fragrance and reviled for the invasion of its Asian species into the Western world, honeysuckle is not today a widely known or used botanical in Western herbal practice. Instead, honeysuckle forms a vital part of Chinese herbal medicine.

In both Asian and Western European traditions, however, as well as in Native American traditions, a similar herbal history emerges that prioritizes the use of honeysuckle (whether a European, American, or Asian species) for hot and inflamed conditions—as well as one that shares in a handful of the other major indications over time and across cultures.

Earliest indications of honeysuckle’s place in Chinese medicine may be dated back to 500 BCE, during which time the flowers were mentioned in the Ming Yi Bie Lu herbal attributed to Tao Hon-jing (Foster & Chongxi, 1992). In another book attributed to the same author, the use of the stem was instead indicated (Foster & Chongxi, 1992). Indeed, in the 1985 Chinese Pharmacopeia, the stem and the flower of honeysuckle were regarded as two separate medicines (Foster & Chongxi, 1992).

The official species employed in Chinese herbal medicine is Lonicera japonica, the Asian honeysuckle now naturalized in the United States, but other species in the genus are sometimes turned to for substitutes. Honeysuckle has also been featured in Arabic medicine, where the roots are employed in preparations (Wood, 2008). Lonicera spp., which includes European honeysuckle, grows in a bushy or shrub form, while Lonicera japonica is a vine form that can wreak havoc with its invasive growth (Yancey, 2014).

The European honeysuckle found in old folklore records from the Western world, especially UK and Ireland, may more often than not have been Lonicera periclymenum (otherwise known as a common honeysuckle, ordinary woodbinde or woodbine), or even Lonicera caprifolium, the Italian honeysuckle.

Mrs. Grieve suggested that about a dozen different species were traditionally worked with as herbal remedies (Grieve, 1931). This European honeysuckle has been deemed unsafe by some as its traditional dosage parameters have not been retained by contemporary practices (Barker, 2001). Both the European honeysuckle and possibly the later Lonicera japonica, however, occupy a prominent place in old records.

Some native American species of honeysuckle—perhaps around eight (Moerman, 2009) would have been used also in the new world and there are several historical instances of their use by various Native American tribes, which are mentioned here as they parallel the Asian and European traditions (tracking the herbal history of honeysuckle among the colonists is rather more challenging and is set aside here).

Herbalist John Parkinson (1567-1650), a collector of many centuries of herbal knowledge—and apothecary to King James I as well as royal botanist to Charles I—discussed a few of the different European honeysuckle species in his work Theatrum botanicum, some of which were apparently only used in gardens or to decorate or cool the sides of houses, not as herbal medicine (Parkinson, 1640).

By 1806, however, honeysuckle more as we know it was introduced to both England and the United States from China. A man named William Kerr—who had been working in Canton collecting botanical specimens for Kew Gardens—introduced the plant to England, and it also arrived as an ornamental in the States (Foster & Chongxi, 1992). Roughly a century later, in 1912, the more hardy variety Lonicera japonica—again, the official species in Asian herbal medicine—was introduced in New York and from there became a hated invasive, particularly in the Eastern regions of the United States (Foster & Chongxi, 1992).

Lonicera, one of its official Latin botanical names, was taken directly from the name of German botanist Adam Lonicer (also known as Lonitzer or Lonicerus, died c. 1596). The name was bestowed by Linnaeus as an honorary nod to Lonicer and his herbal work—Naturalis historiae opus novum, which Mrs. Grieve asserted contained herbal information of interest (Grieve, 1931).

The Chinese name for honeysuckle, meanwhile, Jin-yin-hua, means “gold and silver flower.” Observation of the flowers reveals that the white blossoms turn yellow in the course of one or two days.

Honeysuckle’s Latin botanical family name is Caprifoliaceae and the plant in French is commonly called Chèvre-feuille. Both names mean “goat-leaf”; the plant is traditionally said to be a favorite snack of goats. This is paralleled by the names Geisblatt in German and Capri-foglio in Italian, and incidentally chickens may also enjoy the berries as sustenance (Grieve, 1931).

Children have long played a game of pulling the stamen through the flower’s base in order to enjoy a single drop of nectar pulled out with it, which is edible (hence the name “honeysuckle”). This has been done the world over and was also done by children of the Native American Saanich or Nitinaht tribes with the species Lonicera ciliosa (Turner, 1971; Turner et al., 1983).

In plant astrology, honeysuckle was regarded as ruled by planet Mars and linked with the sign of Scorpio (Mac Coitir, 2015) or as a plant in the sign of Cancer, and an herb of Mercury (Culpeper, 1880).

Meanwhile, Celtic folklore—particularly Irish—demonstrates that the ability of honeysuckle’s vine to harden with age led to it becoming symbolically synonymous with strength and power. And so a saying emerged in Gaelic:

“Chomh righin leis an táith-fhéithleann, ‘as tough as the honeysuckle’ ” (Mac Coitir, 2015, p. 103).

Honeysuckle occupies a place of prominence in Irish stories, especially one known as “The Death of King Fergus”: the King of the Leprechauns, while visiting King Fergus, observes a servant toss a piece of honeysuckle, a plant also known as “King of the Woods”, into the fire. He is disturbed at the servant’s ignorance in that doing so is to bring misfortune. The King of Leprechauns then recites an edifying song:

O servant to King Fergus, make sure the fire’s good,
On land or on sea, do not burn the kind of the woods.
High king of Irish woods, that no army can hold,
It is no weak service tough trees to enfold.
But burn the weak woodbine and warning cries will sound
Of danger at spear point, or strong waves to drown” (Mac Coitir, 2015, p. 104).

The burning of the honeysuckle is a foreshadowing of the King’s death as well as wordplay; honeysuckle’s Gaelic name, féithleann, also means “chief” (Mac Coitir, 2015).

Meanwhile, in Old Irish laws pertaining to shrubs and trees, honeysuckle could be used as a replacement for arbutus in the category known as “the lower division of the wood” and could also sometimes be linked to the Irish Ogham alphabet, namely the letters G and UI—this is notable because honeysuckle had no reputation for economic gain and so must have been well regarded to be applied as a substitute in this way (Mac Coitir, 2015).

In the folklore of the UK and Ireland, honeysuckle was frequently linked to milk and witchcraft. It was used to divert the evil eye, spirits, or witchcraft in drink form and in Scotland it was hung (along with rowan, usually) on May Day above the doorways of cow-houses in order to keep milk from being spoilt (Mac Coitir, 2015).

At the same time, honeysuckle was appears to have a role in Celtic witchcraft itself (Mac Coitir, 2015), likely because the symbolism of its ability to bind and to choke off other plants.

Symbolically, honeysuckle also had notably romantic associations that entwined with poetic themes and stories (for more on this, see Mac Coitir, 2015). Elsewhere, on the European continent, honeysuckle’s romantic associations were also visible. In the medieval French manuscript known as Manuscrit de Noailles, a song by an anonymous author, “Lai du Chèvrefeuille” (The Lay of the Honeysuckle) also associates honeysuckle with love:

Through courtliness I strip away
low behavior and all pride,
for I am moved to commit to paper
what my eyes have pursued;
I begin a lay about it,
the Lay of the Honeysuckle.

Sustained by love, I wish to begin
the melody of the Honeysuckle;
like it, no longer do I lament
over the ills which are wont to distress me;
but henceforth I receive
a generous welcome from love… (Alla Francesca, 1996)

Nor are honeysuckle’s associations with witchcraft and love entirely specific to Europe; the vines of Lonicera dioica L. were decocted and then drunk as an emetic in order to remove the effects of love medicine by the Iroquois tribe of America or, indeed, sometimes used as the love medicine itself (Herrick, 1977).

The flowers and leaves of honeysuckle have made their way into potpourri mixes and the stems employed in weaving baskets. In Ireland, beekeepers would smear hives with the flowers so that the bees would come to regard the hives as a home and return (Mac Coitir, 2015). The bark was in use in ancient times (Barker, 2001) but has fallen out of contemporary popular practice.

Honeysuckle was at times considered useful enough as a remedy that it was ideal that it be readily on hand in the household; Culpeper remarked that it was “…fitting a conserve made from the flowers should be kept in every gentlewoman’s house…” (Culpeper, 1852).

In Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM), long-term use of Lonicera japonica is said to be beneficial for longevity and vitality, although herbalist Reid further notes:

“This is another herb that should only be used for as long as it takes to relieve the symptoms for which it is being applied. If results are not obtained within 7 days, switch to a different herb with similar properties” (Reid, 1995, p. 140)

Honeysuckle’s flowers are the primary and most usual plant part used (but often leaves as well) in its herbal traditions and their cooling classification has generally been deployed in dispersing heat and inflammation. Beyond general inflammation, here follows the major categories of traditional indications, among them respiratory complaints, liver issues, fever, infections, skin problems, as well as the other, less-frequently mentioned indications (but this is by no means exhaustive).

In Western as well as in Eastern traditional use, one of honeysuckle’s major herbal applications is that of reducing inflammation. The Asian species Lonicera japonica is today worked with in such a fashion in Traditional Chinese Medicine and we may also find it now creeping into Western materia medica in this role.

The high salicylic acid found in the European honeysuckle Lonicera periclymenum, the species most often found in old traditions, may well have made it useful, too, for inflammatory diseases (Barker, 2001). Although the flowers were primarily utilized in European traditions, the leaves were as well: honeysuckle is especially found in the traditional records of the UK and Ireland for inflammations of the mouth, even more particularly for thrush, both in gargles and lotion form.

It is therefore very curious that three major herbal writers of the 17th century went on wee rants against this very traditional usage by the “common people”: Nicolas Culpeper (1616-1654) is most often cited and he included the native honeysuckle (Lonicera periclymenum) in his herbal works, objecting to honeysuckle’s use among common folk for inflammation.

But Culpeper’s herbal commentary is based on Gerard’s earlier remarks (1540-1612). Gerard looked back at Galen’s writings in saying that experience showed that water of honeysuckle was good for sore throat, and that distilled leaves or flowers made good medicines for sore mouths, cankers, and ulcerations in the genitals, but in addressing honeysuckle’s herbal “temperature,” he oddly asserted:

“There hath an errour in times past growne amongst a few, and now almost past recovery to bee called againe, being growne an errour universall, which errour is, the decoction of the leaves of Honisuckles, or the distilled water of the floures, are rashly given for the inflammations of the mouth and throte, as though they were binding and cooling. But contrariwise Honisuckle is neither cold nor binding but hot, and attenuating or making thinne. For as Galen saithm both the fruit of Woodbine, and also the leaves, do so much attenuate and heate, as if somewhat too much of them be drunke, they will cause the urine to be as red as bloud, yet do they at the first only provoke urine” (Gerard, 1636, p. 891).

Parkinson (1567-1650) followed Gerard in his herbal work by adding a complaint about honeysuckle’s use in inflammation of the genitals as well as the mouth (he was likely referring to thrush):

 “We in our land have by tradition continued so long in this errour to use the leaves, and flowers, in all gargles and lotions for inflammation in the mouth, or the sore privy parts of man or woman, that I thinke the custome has growne too strong by time for me with a few words to shew the inconvenience, that it may be reformed, for they are neither cooling or binding, as they are taken to be: but are of a clensing, resolving, consuming, and digesting quality, as Hyssope, Origanum, and Winter Savoury are, that with Figges and Licoris, are effectuall to expectorate flegme from the chest and lungs, wherewith they are filled: and that it is not fit to be used in inflammations the very taste of the herbe holding a leafe in ones mouth will declare, by the burning heate will be felt therein, and as Dioscorides and Galen say, that the decoction thereof being drunke sixe days together, will render the urine as blood…” (Parkinson, 1640, p. 1461).

In some editions of Culpeper (possibly later ones, and it is not immediately clear if this is Culpeper or later additions) the same general complaint about inflammation is present and the text is further enlarged with experiential illustration:

“Take a leaf and chew it in your mouth, and you will quickly find it likelier to cause a sore mouth and throat than cure it. If it be not good for this, what is it good for? It is good for something, for God and nature made nothing in vain” (Culpeper, 1852).

All this complaint surrounding the common folks’ use of honeysuckle for inflammation and the insistence that it is a hot remedy is striking—and confusing. In Gerard’s herbal it is furthermore underscored that honeysuckle is a heating remedy; he strikingly remarks that making an oil of the flowers (infused by sunlight) is good for anointing a body that is numb and cold (Gerard, 1636).

Honeysuckle was in fact popularly noted in Irish folk use, across Counties Wicklow, Sligo, Wexford, and the Aran Islands (Allen & Hatfield, 2004), for its cooling and anti-inflammatory indications. Whatever the reasons for this repeated dissent, honeysuckle was and is indicated for inflammation and honeysuckle is still held to be an herb primarily cooling in its properties. Honeysuckle was still noted as a good for inflammation and gargles in the early 20th century (Grieve, 1931).

The Asian honeysuckle in TCM is also specific to inflammation, and that includes indications for sore throat, intestines, urinary tract, and reproductive organs. Meanwhile some Native American tribes such as Thompson tribe used the leaves and stems of native species for sore throat (Turner et al., 1990).

Recent scientific research, too, is supporting above and below-mentioned traditional usages—namely the anti-inflammatory properties of honeysuckle’s flowers (Han et al., 2016; Nikzad-Langerodi et al., 2017; Tang, 2016).

Traditional usage of honeysuckle’s cooling properties has in the East also often been centered on fever, and this indication is also not unknown in the West.

In TCM, honeysuckle is used specifically for a Wei fever—a wind-heat condition or surface fever/disease that affects the lung and stomach heart and spleen organs and meridians (Tierra, 1988; Reid, 1995). Chinese formulations may often be for blood fever. One formula combines forsythia flowers, echinacea, and a bit of licorice, and Formula 15 includes them specifically for clearing heat (Tierra, 1988).

In European traditions, meanwhile, the powdered leaves of Lonicera periclymenum were apparently considered a preventative for fever in Ireland (Mac Coitir, 2015). Over in North America, the Iroquois tribe gave decocted Lonicera dioica to children for fevers (Herrick, 1977).

TCM also employs honeysuckle for a variety of infections—conjunctivitis, for instance, but also applies it antimicrobial activity to staphylococcus, streptococcus, salmonella, and pseudomonas (Tierra, 1988).

Honeysuckle is considered in TCM an antibiotic herb, and has been often twinned with forsythia fruit in treatment (Tierra, 1988). The herb may be found in an antibiotic tea for influenza, for example, where it is frequently combined with chrysanthemum flowers to intensify the antibiotic action of honeysuckle—herbalist Michael Tierra asserts that honeysuckle is in fact close in nature to Chinese Echinacea (Tierra, 1988).

Indeed, honeysuckle’s antibiotic compounds are increasingly being studied in recent scientific forays (Han et al., 2014; Yang et al., 2017).

Meanwhile, a few Native American tribes infused native honeysuckle species (such as Lonicera involucrata) to serve as a wash for sore eyes (Moerman, 2009; Smith, 1929); the Wet’suwet’en tribe employed its bark for generally for infections (Gottesfeld, 1992); and the Okanagan-Colville tribe put the herb to use for sores and skin infections (Turner et al., 1980). (See more under about the skin below.)

Both East and Western traditions share in honeysuckle’s old indication for problems of the liver. In the European folk traditions, one form of this indication can be seen the historical treatment of jaundice in the Irish counties of Leitrim or Cavan (Mac Coitir, 2008).

European honeysuckle was applied for headaches—perhaps also signaling liver congestion—in Norfolk and the Highlands (Allen & Hatfield, 2004). It was suggested that an “elegant water” could be made of the flowers as a distillation for headaches of a nervous variety (Waller, cited by Grieve, 1931).

In her 1930s in collection of herbal lore, Maud Grieve noted that the native honeysuckle of England possesses laxative properties and that the leaves were decocted for liver and spleen diseases (Grieve, 1931). The indication for the spleen can be traced back to Gerard, who may have been echoed by Culpeper, who expressed that honeysuckle took away “the evil of the spleen” (Culpeper, 1852)—and of course Lonicera japonica is used in TCM for the spleen, although the two systems may not agree entirely on its significance and specific applications.

Recent scientific research into the anti-inflammatory connections between honeysuckle’s flowers and liver health are showing support for its traditional use in respect to liver conditions. For example, studies treating nonalcoholic steatohepatitis present only one example of honeysuckle’s potential applicability to liver health. For this particular condition, a study on Lonicera caerulea found that the herb positively ameliorated inflammation (Wu et al., 2017) while an animal study of an ethanol extract of Lonicera japonica asserted its ability to regress the condition (Tzeng et al., 2015).

European species of honeysuckle were popularly indicated for respiratory difficulties and specifically for asthma. Lonicera caprifolium, obtained from gardens as it was introduced into Europe, was used traditionally in Devon as an infusion for asthma (Barker, 2001). European honeysuckle was also mentioned in Gerard’s herbal for breathing difficulties, and that a syrup of the flowers, taken with a bit of wine, was good for diseases of the lungs (Gerard, 1636).

Culpeper, writing in the mid-17th century, enthusiastically remarked:

“…if the lungs be affected by Jupiter, this is your cure… I know no better cure for the asthma than this” (Culpeper, 1852).

In the early 1930s Maud Grieve added:

“Our native Honeysuckle [Lonicera periclymenum] has expectorant… properties. The flowers in the form of syrup have been used for diseases of the respiratory organs and in asthma” (Grieve, 1931).

In the folklore of the UK and Ireland, honeysuckle’s application to the respiratory system is clear. Honeysuckle crops up in records of Counties Cork and Wicklow, where it was specific for whooping cough or consumption (Mac Coitir, 2015). There are fewer records originating from England, but they do show that honeysuckle was used for asthma and bronchitis in Norfolk and the Scottish Highlands (Allen & Hatfield, 2004).

Over in North America, meanwhile, tribal use of different native species was no exception: the leaves of Lonicera ciliosa were decocted by as a remedy for tuberculosis by the Lummi tribe (Gunther, 1973) as well as the Lonicera dioica by the Iroquois (Herrick, 1977). The Nuxalkmc tribe was applying leaves or even bark of Lonicera involucrata for coughs (Compton, 1993) and Costanoan tribe concocted a syrup of an unidentified honeysuckle species for coughs (Bocek, 1984).

Science is delving into honeysuckle’s application to respiratory infections but so has only progressed via animal studies. One such study asserted its anti-upper-respiratory infection action among children (Lu, Zhang, & Huang, 2015) while another study opined that an extract of Lonicera japonica could be quite effective against not only asthma but also other inflammatory diseases (Hong et al., 2013).

Lesser-known folk remedies that survive in documentation pertain to the skin, for pain, infection, and for beauty; for a remedy for a fall-back remedy for adder bites in Devon, stings in Hampshire; or skin remedies from the Highlands for freckles or sunburn as well as toothache cure (Allen & Hatfield, 2004).

Some 17th century herbals also pointed to its use as an ointment for removing skin of sunburns, freckles, and other discolorations; Culpeper noted amusingly that “…the maids will love it” (Culpeper, 1852; Parkinson, 1640).

External skin application is also found in TCM where an infusion of the fresh flowers serves as a wash for sores, abcesses, and general skin infections (Reid, 1995). In the New World, native tribes such as Mendocino and Yuki applied the root of Lonicera interrupta to swellings (Moerman, 2009). We also find instances of several tribes applying honeysuckles to sores, and not only human; the Diegueno tribe applied a decoction of Lonicera subspicata var. johnstonii Keck to wash sores on horses (Hedges, 1986).

Honeysuckle seems to have had a vulnerary role as well, which make sense given that it possesses antiseptic and astringent actions; it was used in County Louth, Ireland for burns (Allen & Hatfield, 20014). Meanwhile in North America, some native tribes used the species Lonicera involucrata as a dressing for burns (Moerman, 2009).

Indeed, its use for skin and infections was widespread by native Americans; the Yuki tribe favored the leaves of Lonicera interrupta for skin sores (Murphey, 1990); the Bella Coola tribe turned to Lonicera involucrata as a spit-paste for boils and itchy skin (Turner, 1973), the Northern Carrier tribe for gonorrhea and the use of the bark for sores and for open sores (Smith, 1929); and the Costanoan tribe utilized an unidentified species to disinfect sores (Bocek, 1984).

This monograph is not exhaustive in outlining all indications for the various species of honeysuckle, but some other recurrent actions and indications for the plant include its diuretic action in both European (cf. Culpeper, 1852) and in many Native American tribal uses for troubles of the urinary system.

The herb was also mentioned in the 17th century as an antispasmodic for cramp, convulsions and palsy (Culpeper, 1852) while in America Lonicera ciliosa was a specific anticonvulsive for epilepsy in the Thompson tribe (Turner et al., 1990).

Honeysuckle’s use for gynecological health is hinted at in the 17th century in such herbal works as those by Culpeper and Parkinson, where it receives mention for hastening along labor (Culpeper, 1852; Parkinson, 1640)—Gerard indicates that this may have been via the seed of honeysuckle (Gerard, 1636).

Meanwhile, the Northern American Squaxin tribe prepared Lonicera ciliosa as an infusion for generalized womb troubles; the women of both the Makah and Quinault tribes chewed on the leaves of Lonicera involucrata during their confinements (Gunther, 1973); the Okanagan-Colville tribe worked with the branches of Lonicera involucrate for women post-partum (Turner, 1980); and the Iroquois decocted the branches of Lonicera oblongifolia in cases of a fallen womb (Herrick, 1977).

Honeysuckle has been used in China in cancer treatment, particularly breast (Tierra, 1988). As with many investigations into herbal medicine, science has been delving into herbs and cancer treatments. Among other studies, a recent scientific study noted the use of Lonicera japonica in traditional Korean herbal medicine not only for the respiratory system but also for its anticancer agency and found that its polyphenolic compounds are anticarcinogenic; they induce apoptosis (Park et. al, 2017).

There is much that remains to be learned about honeysuckle from a Western perspective: the knowledge of the herb is clouded by traditional use of European native species, which are not active in contemporary practice; by historical dissent over its basic energetics and folkloric uses; by the current herbal standard Lonicera japonica not currently occupying a prominent place in Western materia medica; and by the fact that there is equally much to learn about honeysuckle from its Eastern traditions, wherein it is a classic remedy.

Adult Dose

Dried: 9-15 grams*; 5-10 grams in two doses on an empty stomach**
Dosage information from *Planetary Herbology by Michael Tierra, **A Handbook of Chinese Healing Herbs by Daniel Reid

Safety

Despite the above usage, the fruits are widely considered toxic (Foster & Chongxi, 1992) and can be emetic.
