Cayenne

Capsicum annuum, C. baccatum, C. chinensis, C. fastigiatum, C. frutescens, C. pubescens

Native To

South America, Central America, and Mexico.

Geographic Distribution

Grown in arid, tropical, and temperate climates from Mexico to South America. Introduced from South America to Hungary in 1585 (Hvass, 1960). Introduced to India in the 1500s (Kahate, 2007). Grown commercially in Africa, Asia, East Indies, Hungary, Central America, South America, India, Italy, Japan, Mexico, Southern United States, Spain, Zanzibar (Miloradovich, 1952).

Botanical Description

Small, tapering red slender peppers or chilis, two inches long or less, C. frutescens Linn. and Capsicum baccatum Linn are the varieties most often used in ground cayenne pepper (Miloradovich, 1952). The small peppers of these varieties taste very pungent but the scent is sweet when dried. Other cayenne varieties popular in gardening produce peppers up to 8 inches long. Fruit color may be green, yellow, violet, or red, but most often we associate cayenne with red fruits (Hvass, 1960). These are shrubby perennials or annuals in temperate climates, growing 1-6 feet tall. The flowers of the Capsicum genus are small, white, five-petaled, and star-shaped with a yellow center.

Key Constituents

Capsaicinoids including capsaicin and dihydrocapsaicin; carotenoids including capsanthin, alpha-carotine, violaxanthin; flavonoids including apiin and luteolin-7-O-glucoside; steroid saponins in a mixture referred to as capsicidine, and volatile oils (Thompson Healthcare Inc., 2007).

Sustainability

No known sustainability issues.

Harvesting Guidelines

Harvest fruit when red and firm. Fruit may be strung through the stem or calyx to dry if picked once these parts are mature (Miloradovich, 1952).

Uses

The Tupi, natives of a region that is now French Guiana, referred to peppers as quiínia, also spelled kyynha or kynnha. The word cayenne is derived from this Tupi term (Daniels, 2013).

Cayenne is said to have been cultivated by the Aztecs and Mayans thousands of years ago (Kahate, 2007). The Aztecs were of the region that is now Central Mexico, centered near present day Mexico City. The Mayans were of the Yucatan peninsula. There was much trade between the valley of Mexico and Yucatan while these two civilizations flourished (Graham, n.d.). Aztec, Mayan, and Inca cultures are known to have prized cayenne in cuisine and ceremony (Daniels, 2013). The word chili comes from an Aztec language called Nahuatl (Graham, n.d.).

In cayenne’s native region, the Pima Pajo use the smallest chilis for people who develop stomach problems from smoke and dust inhalation from working in mines. The Mayo make a liniment of cayenne leaves and alcohol for rheumatism. Tarahumara chew the peppers with other herbs for headache. In Mexican and southwest American culture, cayenne is sometimes used for pain relief (Kay, 1996). Additional traditional uses include applications for sore throats and cough, wounds, toothache, parasites, immune modulation, and to suppress appetite (Sanati, 2018).

Cayenne has also been integrated into the cuisine of many other warm regions, not only because it grows best in heat, but also because it stimulates sweating, ultimately cooling those who taste it (Freeman, 1991). Paprika, milder than fresh cayenne or hot cayenne powder, is made of the dried red fruits of the larger and sweeter varieties of Capsicum annuum.

In Ayurveda, cayenne is considered a strong stimulant of the circulatory and digestive system and a dispeller of heat and cold. However, it is not used indiscriminately as it may increase inflammation and increase pitta (Lad & Frawley, 1986). Tasting cayenne does stimulate salivation and digestive fluids of the stomach (Haas & Levin, 2006; Myers et al., 1987).

Cayenne has additionally been used in India for pain from arthritis and sciatica, for lowering body temperature in malaria, yellow fever, scarlet fever, and typhus, and for gastrointestinal afflictions such as dyspepsia, diarrhea, cholera, loss of appetite, and anorexia. Tablets of 1:1:1 cayenne, rhubarb, and ginger root are compounded with the goal of reducing desire for alcohol (Thompson Healthcare Inc., 2007).

Ingestion of capsaicin, the most researched constituent in cayenne, is said to stimulate blood flow to the brain, digestive organs, and skin (Haas & Levin, 2006). Cayenne has been used for conditions of poor peripheral circulation leading to coldness or insufficient circulation and function in the extremities, and for those prone to chilblains (Hoffmann, 2003).

Traditionally, cayenne has also been taken to ward off the common cold. It is a strong moving and potentially heating herb. The heating quality of cayenne and other chilli peppers has largely been attributed to capsaicinoids. However, by dispersing blood to the periphery and encouraging sweating, cayenne can be cooling when the environment or body temperature is hot.

Capsaicinoids are a class of compounds unique to the Capsicum genus. These compounds are pungent and oil soluble by a lipid-like quality and active in herbal preparations by an alkaloid-like aromatic component (Ganora, 2009). Capsaicinoids also have topical effects on perception of pain (Pengelly, 2004) and may contribute to the heating and dispersing actions of cayenne.

The capsaicinoid capsaicin is a well-researched topical anodyne with a paradoxical mechanism of action. Capsaicinoids stimulate receptors called vanilloid receptors, located on cutaneous sensory neurons, causing an overwhelming local release of the neurotransmitter “substance P” (in place of an intangibly technical name) in reference to its role as a gatekeeper that transmits the signals of local pain to the brain and modulates inflammation. Effectively, capsaicin applied topically stimulates a sudden release of substance P, temporarily depleting the neurotransmitter locally, and for a time, mitigating the sensation of pain (Pengelly, 2004).

Itching is a lower degree of pain. Cayenne has also been shown to reliably reduce itching related to specific medical treatments (Cho et al., 1997). Research also indicates that capsaicin acts on a receptor in the peripheral and central nervous systems, modulating the perception of pain and heat and regulation of body temperature (Ganora, 2009). It may also have some benefits in relieving chronic inflammation (Thompson Healthcare Inc, 2007).

Externally cayenne is used for a variety of issues involving pain and inflammation. Examples from research include osteoarthritis and rheumatoid arthritis pain, herpes zoster, shingles (Zostrix cream containing 0.025% to 0.075% capsaicin), and back pain (Frerick et al., 2003 as cited in Kuhn & Winston, 2008). In a study of 14 patients with osteoarthritis, tenderness and pain scores were significantly reduced at 4 weeks (McCarthy & McCarty, 1992). In a study of 70 patients with osteoarthritis and 31 patients with rheumatoid arthritis, a reduction in knee pain during use of capsaicin cream was observed (Deal et al., 1991). A review comparing five osteoarthritis studies concluded that topical use of capsaicin four times daily was moderately effective in reducing pain intensity up to 20 weeks (Laslett & Jones, 2014). In a study of postherpetic neuralgia (pain during or after herpes infection which can include shingles) in 12 patients over 60, topical application of capsaicin, 75% of patients reported substantial relief and only one patient reported a temporary adverse reaction, a burning sensation (Bernstein et al., 1997). Cayenne plasters were found to be more helpful than placebo in reducing lower back pain (Gagnier et al., 2006, and Gagnier et al., 2007).

Another area of research on the use of cayenne creams is for relief of diabetic neuropathy, a condition which can involve either pain or numbness as a side effect of chronically elevated blood sugar affecting nerves. In a 1991 multicenter study with 252 diabetic neuropathy patients, capsaicin or placebo cream was applied to painful areas four times a day for 8 weeks, with a statistically significant (58.4% versus 45.3%) decrease in pain observed (Anonym, 1991). A 1992 study with 22 patients with chronic severe diabetic neuropathy using similar methods and duration to the former study found 50% of patients who improved or were cured at follow up (Tandan, 1992).

Topical cayenne or capsaicin preparations have been studied for reducing pain or discomfort as side effects of surgeries. Capsaicin reduced postoperative neuropathy in cancer patients after mastectomy, amputation, or thoracotomy (Ellison et al., 1997). Plasters containing capsaicin have been used on an acupuncture point on the hand to reduce postoperative nausea and vomiting in abdominal hysterectomy patients (Kim et al., 2002).

Similar to the paradoxical action on pain externally, the effects of cayenne on painful conditions of the stomach in particular and gastrointestinal (GI) tract in general are complex. Conventionally patients with acid reflux and GI sensitivity were advised against eating spicy foods. However in some cases cayenne may be protective to the GI tract.

Rodriguez-Stanley et al.’s study in 2000 specifically acknowledges the complex physical effects of cayenne, analgesia and pain relief, mucosal protection, and injury. Patients with a history of heartburn ingested either a heartburn-inducing meal only or the same meal with a 5 mg cayenne capsule. Symptoms were the same or worse in the cayenne group, except for one set of parameters. The cayenne group had a quicker onset of symptoms but also quicker relief of symptoms. The researchers suggested a possible analgesic effect of cayenne, by depletion of the neurotransmitter substance P, as the timing of pain relief could not be explained by changes in stomach pH or change in gastric emptying. Additionally, the study reported, “[c]apsaicin appeared to exacerbate heartburn symptoms after initial post-meal esophageal contact.” However it also stated that “[a]t the 225-minute time point and thereafter, repeated exposure of the esophageal mucosa to capsaicin may have produced an analgesic effect since subjects rated heartburn as less severe than following the meal without capsaicin.” (Rodriguez-Stanley et al., 2000).

There are also studies which suggest the protective effects of cayenne on the healthy GI tract. In two small studies, with a total of 18 healthy volunteers, with normal endoscopies, each subject took 20 g chili orally with 200 mL water, compared to the placebo, water only, followed by a 600 mg dose of aspirin with water. An endoscopy 6 hours later revealed significantly less injury (1.5 versus 4 by a standardized assessment) to gastroduodenal mucosa in the chili group (Yeoh et al., 1995). Stimulating micro-circulation and mucosal secretion, by pungency and nerve stimulation, is one way that studies suggest cayenne may be protective to the lining of the stomach and colon (Abdel-Salam et al., 1997). Additionally, vitamin A, as beta carotene, an antioxidant, is highly concentrated in cayenne. Beta carotene helps protect the quality of the cells in the mucosal membranes of the sinus and digestive tract, improving functions of absorption, secretion, protection, and sensation (Daniels, 2013).

Cayenne may increase satiety. In a weight loss related study, approximately one gram of chili pepper was given to groups receiving 100% of the daily caloric requirements or 75% of their daily caloric requirement, and a control was used. When the patients on the reduced calorie diet had the cayenne supplement, desire to eat, satiety, and fullness was no different than the groups that received no reduction in their diet. However, the group that received a reduced calorie diet and no cayenne supplement felt less full or satiated and had a stronger desire to eat. The cayenne was given after the meals, and the study suggested that it may be helpful in preventing overeating (Janssens et al., 2014).

Cayenne may also support blood sugar balance. It is high in vitamin C, which may have a role in blood sugar regulation balance (Haas & Levin, 2006). However, favorable action of cayenne on blood sugar balance may exceed expectations of vitamin C content alone. In a 4 week study with 36 subjects, markers of metabolism were measured during a bland diet and a cayenne supplemented diet. Serum insulin, C-peptide, and glucose concentration indicated that regular consumption of cayenne may reduce hyperinsulinemia, which is typically usually caused by insulin resistance. These positive effects were greater in subjects with a higher body mass index (Ahuja et al., 2006).

By blood sugar balance, satiety, or effects on metabolism, C. annuum in various forms has been reputed to assist in healthy weight loss. In a study to measure the effect of paprika xanthophyll intake on abdominal fat in healthy overweight humans, 100 volunteers took either capsules containing 9.0 mg paprika xanthophylls, a type of plant pigment in C. annuum, or placebo daily for 12 weeks. Abdominal visceral fat area, subcutaneous fat area, total fat area, and body mass index were smaller in the xanthophyll group, and total cholesterol, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol were decreased in this group at the conclusion of the study, with no adverse effects (Kakutani et al., 2018). A review of clinical and animal studies over the last three decades concluded that C. annuum and capsaicin have demonstrated hypolipidemic, hypotensive, and blood sugar balancing effects, and an antiobesity effect in which its “[e]fficacy was partially the same as some antiobesity drugs” (Sanati et al., 2018).

Cayenne has many actions directly related to cardiovascular protection. Rich in flavonoids, cayenne is believed to be a valuable antioxidant, protecting cell health, particularly in the circulatory system, and reducing platelet adhesion (Lininger et al., 1998). In models of high fat diet, it has been shown to lower blood fat levels, possibly decreasing risk of atherosclerosis (Haas & Levin, 2006). Capsaicin taken in capsules, 4 mg daily, for 3 months significantly improved risk factors of coronary heart disease, specifically in individuals with low high density lipoprotein cholesterol levels (Qin et al., 2017). Another study, 4 weeks long, on metabolic and arterial function found that consuming cayenne may reduce resting heart rate and increase effective myocardial perfusion pressure time, which is a predictor of heart attack survival, in men (Ahuja et al., 2007).

Cayenne has been used by many cultures in a range of settings from field and kitchen preparations to surgical aftercare. It is applied when deep, warming stimulation may be helpful by changing the experience of pain or increasing circulation. However, temporary but painful adverse events do occur from irritation to the eyes, nose, and compromised mucosal membranes internally. Using low or moderate amounts internally,  and when possible using topical applications such as commercially available plasters which can be removed quickly and completely in case of discomfort, are cautious ways of approaching use of this pungent herb.

Adult Dose

Fresh Herb: Used as a food, to tolerance for taste.
Powder: 100-500 mg (Lad & Frawley, 1986). British Herbal Pharmacopeia: 30-120 mg (Hoffmann, 2003).

Tincture: 0.3-1 mL 3x/day (Hoffmann, 2003). 1:10, 90% (Kuhn & Winston, 2008). 5-15 drops 1:5, 95% alcohol (Moore, 1995).
Tea: Infuse 100-500 mg (Lad & Frawley, 1986). 1 tablespoon diluted in hot water, of an infusion prepared using ½ to 1 tsp per 1 cup water infused for 10 minutes (Hoffmann, 2003).
Capsules: Up to three 400-500 mg capsules per day (Kuhn & Winston, 2008). #0 size, 1-2 at a time (Moore, 1995).
Topical: Creams, if standardized are generally 0.025-0.075% capsaicin (Lininger et al., 1998).

Safety

Inhalation should be avoided when handling the powder or cutting the fruit, as capsaicin may cause immediate bronchoconstriction (Fuller et al., 1985). By extension, special caution should be exercised by people with asthma when handling the herb or using it topically.
Irritation of the skin and eyes and other mucosal membranes occurs when oil from the fresh or dried herb or topical preparations remain on the skin after handling. Preparations containing capsaicin should not be applied to or near open wounds or the eyes (Thompson Health Inc., 2007). Capsicum should be avoided on hypersensitive skin (Brinker, 2010). Testing topical products on a small area of skin on first application is recommended (Lininger et al., 1998). While processing hot peppers, gloves may be worn to reduce absorption into the skin. Capsaicin is not water soluble and therefore does not wash off easily, however, due to its lipophilic nature, milk or other solvents that contain oil or fat may be useful at relieving accidental exposure (Kuhn & Winston, 2008). Fortunately even though cayenne causes intense sensation, it does not typically produce blistering (Leung & Foster, 1996).
There is debate about the effects of cayenne on stomach ulcers and gastroesophageal reflux disease. People with ulcers were generally advised to avoid cayenne, with the expectation that it was irritant in nature and would increase acid secretion (Brinker, 2010; Meyers et al., 1987; Satyanarayana, 2006). However, other studies suggest that the most recognized active constituent of cayenne, capsaicin, inhibits acid secretion and stimulates alkali mucus secretions and blood flow to gastric mucosa, by stimulating nerves in the stomach that signal protection against injury (Satyanarayana, 2006).
Individual, meta, and epidemiological studies are discussed further in the main writeup, including an epidemiological study that suggests that populations with higher consumption of chilis have lower incidences of ulcers (Satyanarayana, 2006).
Cayenne is not recommended for people with hemorrhoids (Kuhn & Winston, 2008). Additionally, chili pepper consumption was found to increase symptoms or be harmful for patients with anal fissures (Gupta, 2007).
Most potential interactions noted for cayenne are only extrapolated from pharmacology, i.e., caution is noted when using therapeutic drugs which have similar effects to cayenne, or they have only been observed in in vitro or animal studies. The use of cayenne with anticoagulants, antiplatelet agents, thrombolytic agents, and low molecular weight heparins may increase risk of bleeding, though the clinical significance is unknown (Thompson Health Inc. 2007).
Some speculative interactions with less often used medicines have been explored in the following in vitro or animal studies. In an animal study, risk of theophylline overdose was increased with the use of cayenne (Brinker, 2010). Effects of barbiturates were increased with cayenne in an animal study (Brinker, 2010). According to additional studies, use of barbiturates and cayenne could either increase or decrease the effect of barbiturates (Thompson Health Inc., 2007). Additionally, in vitro research suggests that cayenne could increase metabolism of a pain reliever ethylmorphine (Brinker, 2010).
Despite its stimulating and moving nature, cayenne is generally considered safe during pregnancy and breastfeeding when used moderately, for example as a food (Kuhn & Winston, 2008). It is not recommended for use with young children (Kuhn & Winston, 2008).
Rarely, however notably, adverse events have been reported in men without known prior cardiovascular risk who suffered heart attacks shortly after beginning supplementation with cayenne capsules. In three cases, there were no other factors identified in healthy males ages 21, 25, and 41 who were using cayenne for either two days, five days, or an unspecified duration, to assist in weight loss (Akçay et al., 2017; Sayin et al., 2012; Sogut et al., 2012). Another male age 29 with no known risk factors suffered a heart attack after using a capsaicin patch for six days to reduce pain (Akcay et al., 2009). In another case, a 59-year-old male with high levels of thyroid stimulating hormone suffered a heart attack after abundant chili pepper consumption the day prior (Patane et al., 2008). All of these events were reported to possibly be linked to acute hypertension or vasospasm caused by capsaicin. One report indicated that capsaicinoids have been shown to “increase heart rate, and blood pressure, and dysrhythmic discharges with increased adrenaline” (Luo et al., 2011 as cited in Akçay et al., 2017). Another report related cayenne-related cardiac incidents to possible inhibition of potassium currents and coronary vasospasms from capsaicin, as has been demonstrated in animal models (Castle, 1992; Kehl, 1994; as cited in Sogut et al., 20012).
