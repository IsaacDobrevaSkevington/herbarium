Marjoram

Origanum majorana L. (syn. Majorana hortensis)

Native To

Temperate areas of western Asia, namely Cyprus and Turkey. Naturalized in antiquity to the Mediterranean region (USDA Agricultural Research Service/NPGS, 2018), to which it is now commonly considered native.

Geographic Distribution

Marjoram is widely cultivated throughout the Mediterranean region and in Asia, Europe, and North Africa (Bina & Rahimi, 2017). It is perennial in its native habitats, for example in Portugal, but usually does not otherwise over-winter in colder climates (Grieve, 1931).

Botanical Description

Marjoram is a perennial and sports the signature rectangular stalks of the mint family. The leaves are green, opposite, and oval in shape. The herb may grow up to a height of 30-60 centimeters (Bina & Rahimi, 2017). It is more delicate in appearance than wild marjoram, otherwise known as oregano (Origanum vulgare), with which it is very often confused.

Marjoram’s flowers may be pinkish, purplish, or white and they appear in clusters in bracts. These clusters give a knotted appearance, hence its common name “knotted marjoram.” Marjoram’s scent is pungent and slightly spicy. There are countless unclassified hybrid forms flourishing in gardens because Origanum is not only variable but also crosses easily in proximity (The Herb Society of America, 2005).

Key Constituents

Essential oil, monoterpenes, flavonoids, phenolic glycosides (Bina & Rahimi), tannins (Tierra, 1988).

Sustainability

No sustainability concerns.

Harvesting Guidelines

Harvest the aerial parts just as they come into flowering, drying them in a shaded location.

Uses

Marjoram is today commonly associated with the spicy taste of pizza seasonings or perhaps with a jar in the standard kitchen spice rack that is not often employed. The herb frequently gets confused with—or overlooked in favor of—its close relative oregano. Through a careful sifting of history, however, it becomes apparent that marjoram has an identity of its own, even if there is not a great deal of scientific research yet available to support marjoram’s strong traditional herbal uses. In general, however, a great many of marjoram’s indications since antiquity have not only been sustained throughout history but have also interconnected with some Eastern traditions.

Reading about marjoram in texts before the 18th century can be highly misleading because the word “marjoram” was at varying historical times used for both sweet marjoram (which is our marjoram) and wild marjoram (which we know as oregano, or O. vulgare). The botanical taxonomy of marjoram has also been a bit murky; Linnaeus first identified Origanum as one genus but throughout the following years the genus got reordered several times, under names such as Majorana, Amaracus, or Origanum (The Herb Society of America, 2005).

Marjoram’s current Latin binomial name Origanum indicates that marjoram belongs to the Origanum (oregano) plant genus. The word origanum can be traced back to ancient Greek ὀρίγανον (origanon) referring either to Greek oregano or to a sour or crabbed expression. The word may also be traced back to the Latin orīganum, which usually was applied in antiquity to wild marjoram, or what we know today as oregano (Lewis & Short, 1879). Origanum is often suggested to be derived from a combination of the ancient Greek ὀρός (mountain) and γάνος (brightness, joy, or pride) as both marjoram and various kinds of oregano flourish abundantly in mountainous terrain.

Marjoram’s second current botanical name, majorana, has a complex etymology that is also bound up with manuscript confusions, so we largely leave it aside here. Some modern translations of the Greek physician Dioscorides (c. 40-90 BCE), however, contain among his list of various cultural names for marjoram the idea that the Romans referred to what we know as marjoram as maiorana (Dioscorides, 2000). Meanwhile, a very early 17th century French edition of Matthiolus’ commentaries on Dioscorides remarks that while Toscans called marjoram “Persa” (perhaps because it first arrived in Italy from Persia), other Italians called it “Maiorana,” having borrowed the word from Latin (Matthiolus, 1605).

Marjoram has also long been identified with the plant widely called “amaracus” in antiquity (Latin: amaracus, from the ancient Greek: ἀμάρακον). The Roman polymath, Pliny the Elder (23-79 BCE), recorded that the most highly regarded type of amaracus in his day—the one with the best scent—came from Cyprus (Pliny, 1906). It is thought that our cultivated marjoram may in fact originate from wild populations of O. majorana in Cyprus (Karousou et al., 2012), an island which to this day still produces perfumes directly obtained from marjoram (Butler, 2015).
The word amaracus, which as noted above was also one of the former scientifically-bestowed names for marjoram’s genus, has a mythology of its own connected to perfumery. One version is preserved by Servius, a commentator on the ancient verses of Vergil:

“Amaracus was a slave charged with keeping the perfumes for a royal house (Amaracus hic puer regius unguentarius fuit). Having tripped and fallen while carrying the perfumes, he produced an even greater perfume by their accidental blending. As a result, the best perfumes are called amaracina. He himself was later transformed into the herb sampsucum, now also known as amaracus” (Butler, 2015, p. 75).

Pliny recorded many uses for amaracus, several of which have remained traditional applications for marjoram throughout history: marjoram was considered beneficial for menstrual problems (in pessary form, which Pliny noted was not useful for this indication in infusion form); for stomach cramping (as a boiled juice); for softening sinews and uterine tissue (in oil form, known as either sampuschinum or amaracinum); and for bruises or sprains (the leaves combined with honey for bruises and with wax for sprains) (Pliny, 1906). Amaracus was furthermore applied to scorpion stings along with salt and vinegar, used with polenta for eye issues, deemed useful for edema and strangury, and used in dry form to promote sneezing (Pliny, 1906).

Marjoram was sometimes referred to in antiquity also by the name sampsuchon and Pliny’s close contemporary, the Greek physician Dioscorides, recorded that the best sampsuchon was the Cyzicenian kind (in Asia Minor), followed by Cyprian and Egyptian, and that the herb was woven into head wreaths (Dioscorides, 2000). He mentions the same indications as Pliny but added that marjoram was used with wax for edema as well as dislocation, and that the leaves were mixed with other medications for fatigue and with other softening medications for restoring warmth (Dioscorides, 2000).

Marjoram also possesses a traditionally symbolic connection to joy and love, perhaps due to its scent. The ancient Roman poet Catullus (c. 94-54 BCE) referred to marjoram in a poem on a marriage theme (Carmina 61), invoking Hymen, the god of marriage, and calling upon him to “garland the temples with the sweet-smelling flowers of marjoram” (Catullus, 1893).

The herb also seems to have been especially linked to Venus or Aphrodite, the ancient goddesses of love in Roman and Greek mythology, respectively. In the Roman poet Vergil’s (c. 70-19 BCE) epic poem Aeneid, Venus takes the son of the warrior Aeneas away to Idalia’s grove, “where fragrant blossoms of soft-petalled marjoram embower in pleasant shade” (Vergil, Book I, c. 693, 1910).

Idalia’s grove was in fact on Cyprus, also the location of the best marjoram of antiquity according to Pliny and the second-best according to Dioscorides.

In the poetry of the Roman Lucretius (c. 99-55 BCE), namely De rerum natura, Book 4, marjoram is also associated with love:

“But the lover, shut out, weeping, often piles her threshold with flowers ad garlands, perfumes her haughty doorposts with amaracine [marjoram]… and, desolate, plants kisses on the doors” (Butler, 2015, p. 85).

Fast-forwarding centuries into the history of Europe, we still find an allusion to love. For St. Luke’s Day, marjoram was one of the ingredients in a preparation used to anoint oneself in order to dream of one’s future partner (Grieve, 1931).

In the Victorian language of flowers, marjoram, among other indications, sometimes stood for “blushes” (Kirkby, 2011). In some magical traditions, marjoram is used for love spells and added to food in order to strengthen love (Cunningham, 2012). One of the earliest English herbal sources to record marjoram in 1597, The Herball by John Gerard (1545-1612, but whose work largely translated that of Flemish Rembert Dodoens’ 1554 herbal work) notes the use of marjoram for individuals given to frequent sighing (Gerard, 1636)—of which act lovers may also be suspected.

Marjoram also appeared in several places in Shakespeare’s writings (The Herb Society of America, 2005). In some magical traditions it was and is placed in various places for protection or given to lift the spirits of depressed individuals (Cunningham, 2012).

Marjoram was eventually cultivated as an annual in gardens of northern climates, and much past literature in English from the UK is sparse, although the herb is still used in kitchens of those regions for eggs and meat dishes and considered to combine well with thyme and basil and to be of use in the bath (Barker, 2001). It has been conjectured that marjoram was introduced to the British Isles around the year 1573 (Stephenson & Churchill, 1834) and to France a bit earlier during the Middle Ages, perhaps as a consequence of the crusades (Mességué, 1988).

Indeed, if English herbalists John Gerard (1545-1612) and John Parkinson (1567-1650), the apothecary to King James I who also doubled as botanist to King Charles I (1567-1650), had not taken the time to include extensive entries on marjoram in their writings, much of which was then echoed by Culpeper (1616-1654), little traditional information may have trickled down to us to today in the English language.

In medieval households, marjoram appears to have been considered a necessary herb not just for in herbal infusion form but also in cooking, in washing hands, and in strewing the floors (The Herb Society of America, 2005). Marjoram was also apparently used in ale and beer before hops and in France was employed in making for hippocras (a traditional warm, spiced wine) (Freeman, 1943). By the early 17th century, Gerard remarked it was good added to broths and meats (Gerard, 1636). Marjoram does not have a strong parallel history in the Americas, where its close cousin oregano also did not become popular until post-WWII, when American soldiers came home with an acquired taste for Italian cuisine (The Herb Society of America, 2005).

The use of marjoram for its scent was also not neglected through the ages: at least as early as the Renaissance period in Europe, we find marjoram referred to as “sweet Marjerome” (Gerard, 1636; Parkinson, 1640). Marjoram’s scent was used for beautification: Parkinson remarked that “our daintiest women doe put it to still among their other sweet herbs, to make sweet washing water” (Parkinson, Chapter 6, 1640). And Culpeper, as Gerard before him, had also noted that marjoram was used in “all odoriferous ointments”…which included “powders, &c. that are for ornament or delight” (Culpeper, 1850, p.113).

A very early 17th century manuscript that is a French translation of Matthiolus’ commentaries on Dioscorides noted that the use of marjoram crossed social classes; it was popular for its fragrance even among poor women:

“Ladies enjoy [marjoram] so much, and are so curious about marjoram, for reason of its good fragrance: so that even the poorest women nourish it carefully and in wooden boxes, and in terracotta pots” (Matthiolus, 1605, p. 278).

John Parkinson, meanwhile, not only described several different types of marjoram but also delved deeply into the verbal confusion around its many names. He also described its close clusters of blossoms and explained that it was this knotted-up appearance of flowers that gave rise to its common name of “knotted Marjerom” (Parkinson, 1640).

It is also in the 17th century that we see clearly that marjoram may have retained its uses from antiquity as an emmenagogue, snuff for sneezing, diuretic, vulnerary for bruises and dislocations, stomachic, anticonvulsant and antispasmodic, and a helper for head and brain issues, all with an apparently new focus on liver and spleen issues as well respiratory health (Culpeper, 1850; Gerard, 1636; Matthiolus, 1605; Parkinson, 1640;).

By the mid-18th century, marjoram was still considered useful. The herbal work of botanist John Hill, first published in 1755, described marjoram as “a common garden plant, of no great beauty, but kept for the sake of its virtues and use” and shows that it was still employed for menstrual issues as well as for dizziness, headache, various nervous complaints, and for obstructions, with the additional note that the herb in powdered form was less efficacious than the fresh (Hill, 1812, pp. 216-7).

Nearly a century later, however, marjoram’s use in medicine appears to have fallen away and been replaced by its role in cuisine. It could be found described in the UK and Ireland as rarely used medicinally but still often in cooking or in snuffs (Stephenson & Churchill, 1834). In the mid-19th century, King’s American Dispensatory noted marjoram as being cultivated in American gardens for wide use in cookery with properties similar to oregano (Felter & Lloyd, 1905). Marjoram must have still had some importance, because before World War I, agents bought up the herb to northern Europe and exported it to Marseille and elsewhere (Grieve, 1931).

In terms of specific indications for restoring balance to the body, marjoram has a very strong traditional connection to the head and brain, which can be seen since classical antiquity and in English texts written as far back as the 17th century: as a type of snuff preparation, marjoram was thought to purge the brain and the herb in general was deemed a solid remedy for the brain and specific to head and brain complaints arising from cold causes (Culpeper, 1850; Gerard, 1636; Matthiolus, 1605; Parkinson, 1640).

The indication of marjoram for the head or brain was sustained; Italian folk remedy sources from the 19th and early 20th centuries also indicated that marjoram was prepared throughout the centuries in infusion form and applied externally as well alone or with other herbs for headache (Mazzei et al., 2018). Marjoram has also been said to have been traditionally used in France as a céphalique (an herb relating to the head) throughout the centuries, notably also for spasm and anxiety (Mességué, 1988).

By the 18th century, marjoram was used in France for both in epilepsy and insomnia (Mességué, 1988). At the same time, marjoram is also indicated by contemporary herbal practitioners for anxiety states in general, as well as insomnia (Holmes, 2006).

In addition to head pains, marjoram was also sometimes said to be a sovereign remedy against all nerve pains (Matthiolus, 1605). Indeed, marjoram is generally considered a nervous relaxant by herbal practitioners. Stress and tension are documented potential triggers of migraines and tension headaches and herbs used for them may also be herbs traditionally specific to epilepsy: one prevalent theory for migraine is that it is an episodic condition similar to epilepsy, in which waves of brain function suddenly change, translating brain cell excitability into attack or as an episode of meningeal inflammation (Bone & Mills, 2013). Hence marjoram’s use by some practitioners for both types of conditions.

The use of marjoram for nerve issues or spasms conditions related to the head does not represent a merely Western approach. In traditional Chinese medicine, marjoram may be used long-term for those individuals exhibiting constrained qi arising from overwork, stress, or chronic health conditions (Holmes, 2006). And in Azerbaijani folk medicine, bathing in a marjoram decoction is considered of assistance in nervousness and after a visit to some Middle Eastern bath-houses, marjoram might appear with a range of other fragrant herbs in a post-bath libation (Alakbarov, 2003).

Elsewhere in the East, the use of Origanum majorana also historically pertained to epilepsy. This use of marjoram appears to have been introduced by famous Persian physician Avicenna, or Ibn Sina (980-1037) and was apparently employed through inhalation (Sahranavard et al., 2014). Marjoram, which was known as marzanjoosh, appears in three out of five of the most important ancient Iranian herbals for epilepsy: the Canon of Avicenna; the Tuhfat al-Mu’minin by Hakim Mu’min (1669 BCE); and the Makhzan ul-Adwia, or Drug Treasure by Mohammad Hossein Aghili Alavi Khorasani (1772) (Sahranavard et al., 2014).

By the early 19th century in the West, we hear that marjoram is to be used fresh and in infusion form and indicated for headache as well as dizziness and nervous complaints (Hill, 1812) although “they talk idly who call it a remedy for apoplexies” (Hill, 1812, p. 217). It is not clear whether the apoplexy mentioned here is incapacity from stroke or cerebral hemorrhage or simply the state of speechlessness caused by severe emotions such as anger, but marjoram was recorded as being used for speechlessness and tongue paralysis in the 17th century (Parkinson, 1640; Gerard, 1636).

The 19th-century indication for dizziness is also of interest as it was commented in 1605 that marjoram was profitable for sea sickness and in distilled juice form removed pain from the ears and was deemed specific to tinnitus as well as deafness (Matthiolus, 1605). Meanwhile Parkinson and Culpeper remarked that the juice, dropped into the ears, eased pains and singing noises in them (Parkinson, 1640; Culpeper 1850). And herbalist Peter Holmes, seeking to integrate Western herbal therapeutics with traditional Chinese ones, has indicated marjoram for heart qi restraint (or Liver Yang rising), in which ringing ears are a symptom (Holmes, 2006).

Some herbal practitioners continue to use marjoram today for tension headache or migraine due to its nervous relaxant action. Indeed, like valerian root (Valeriana officinalis), marjoram helps with both spasm as well as seizures, being not merely spasmolytic but also anticonvulsant (Holmes, 2006). The essential oil of marjoram often appears in protocols for severe headaches rather than the infusion. It is not yet clear which phytochemicals may play key role in marjoram’s anticonvulsant activity, but triterpenoids, essential oils, flavonoids, or steroids are the prime suspects (Deshmane et al., 2007).

Marjoram also has primary uses for the digestive and respiratory systems. Its application to conditions of these two systems is mirrored in traditional Chinese medicine, wherein marjoram is strongly associated with the Lung and Stomach meridians (Tierra, 1988).

As with many plants in the mint family, marjoram possesses a carminative and digestive-stimulating agency, and it was traditionally used for loss of appetite in France (Mességué, 1988). The chemical Sabinene hydrate is largely linked to marjoram’s sweet flavor and can also occur commonly in hybrids with Origanum vulgare subspecies (The Herb Society of America, 2005).

As we have seen, marjoram’s indication for stomach cramping arises as far back as Rome of the first century BCE (Pliny, 1906). In the early 17th century it was used as a libation to stimulate the appetite and of use in medicine precisely because it helped digestion—but it was notably considered of aid for the stomach either internally or externally (Matthiolus, 1605). Some French shops even today offer marjoram for sale in tisane form.

Parkinson noted that marjoram was good “against all the paines and torments of the belly” (Parkinson, 1640); Culpeper, that the herb could assist flatulence (Culpeper, 1850); and somewhat earlier, Gerard had recorded an indication of marjoram for easing belly pains in general (Gerard, 1636). Again, this is not merely Western indications; in Azerbaijani folk medicine, bathing in a marjoram decoction is considered of assistance in flatulence (Alakbarov, 2003).

It is further interesting to note that herbal writers of the 17th century such as Matthiolus and Parkinson (and later Culpeper) indicated marjoram for the liver and spleen (Matthiolous, 1605; Parkinson, 1640; Culpeper, 1850); both of the traditional Chinese medicine organs of the same name are associated with marjoram. Today, Liver or Spleen might be associated with intestinal qi constraint or a Liver-Spleen disharmony, in which the marjoram is indicated for abdominal pains, colitis, irritable bowel syndrome, dyspepsia, colic, or distension (Holmes, 2006).

In terms of the respiratory system, meanwhile, marjoram has been historically used for breathing issues as far back as the middle ages; Gerard noted that marjoram “…draweth forth much baggage flegme” (Gerard, 1636, p. 665). Parkinson recorded that a decoction of marjoram could be of assistance in helping “all the diseases of the chest, which hinder the freenesse of breathing,” which Culpeper then echoed (Parkinson, 1640, chap. 6).

Marjoram, whose actions include antispasmodic and expectorant, has been traditionally used for asthma not just in England but also in Turkey (Bina & Rahimi, 2017). Marjoram might be indicated by some herbal practitioners today for asthma as well as whooping cough, wheezing, or an irritable cough (Holmes, 2006). Some initial research has concluded that taking 2 drops of marjoram oil per day successfully improves lung function in adults with asthma (Mohamed et al., 2008).

Marjoram is also a diuretic. There does not seem to be a widespread use of marjoram in the West today for or any scientific inquiry into its longstanding tradition—since ancient Rome—of efficacy in urinary issues, other than sometimes using essential oil for this body system. In Azerbaijani folk medicine, however, bathing in a marjoram decoction is considered to have a diuretic effect (Alakbarov, 2003).

Marjoram has long been used externally. We saw that in antiquity it was used as a perfume as well as a kind of vulnerary, applied externally to bruises or sprains (Pliny, 1906; Dioscorides, 2000). Gerard and Parkinson (and those writing after them, such as Culpeper) noted that this tradition was in part sustained:

“It [marjoram] is profitably put into those ointments and salves, that are made to warme and comfort the outward parts or members, the joynts also and sinews, for swellings also and places out of joynt…the oyle made thereof is very warming and comfortable to the joynts that are stiffe, and the sinewes that are hard, to molifie, supple and stretch them forth” (Parkinson, 1640, chap. 6).

“There is an excellent oyle to be drawne forth…good against the shrinking of sinews, crampes, cobyulsion, and all aches proceeding of a colde cause” (Gerard, 1636, p. 665).

Marjoram’s pairing with honey for external use goes back to Roman antiquity (Pliny, 1906), but Gerard added new details in having recommended using dried leaves of marjoram with honey to dissolve clotted or congealed blood as well as to remove “blacke and blew markes after stripes and bruises” (Gerard, 1636, p. 665). Today, herbalist Peter Holmes suggests that a very good liniment can be prepared from marjoram for stimulating or relaxing painful, cold, or tight muscles with a sun oil or essential oil as its base (Holmes, 2006).

The antibacterial action of its essential oil has been studied both in vivo and in vitro and determined as effective against Heliobacter pylori (Ohno et al., 2003). Other investigations have found marjoram to be of potential use in meat preservation, its essential oil having shown benefits against Staphylocococcus aureus (Marques et al., 2015).

As we have seen, marjoram has been used in concentrated oil preparations since antiquity and the essential oil in particular today is the form of the herb most in current use. In some aromatherapy practices marjoram essential oil is used for epilepsy, constipation, hypertension, immune support, skeletal muscle cramps, menstrual cramps and dysmenorrhea, cystic fibrosis, and depression (Martin, 2007).

The indication for bringing on menses found in earliest records of the 1st century BCE (Pliny, 1906) and which appear throughout early European and 19th-century American herbal writings has also been sustained in Iranian and Jordanian traditional medicine, where marjoram has been used not just for menstruation issues but also for, polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS)(Kolivand et al., 2017). This is one of the only ancient and sustained uses to have yet received any scientific support. Marjoram’s ability to regulate menstrual cycles has been recently investigated in the area of female reproductive health as pertains hormonal profiling of women with PCOS and found in a randomized pilot study to reduce levels of adrenal androgens while improving insulin sensitivity (Haj-Husein et al., 2016).

Although marjoram does not seem to be clearly indicated for tumors in traditional records of the West, it is now (as is the case with many other herbs) being investigated in light of cancer research. Most specifically, marjoram has been found to exert a marked antiproliferative effect. This has been explored in human cases of leukemia and liver carcinomas, where marjoram’s antioxidant action appears to play a notable role (Abdel-Massih et al., 2010; Fathy et al., 2016). At particularly high doses, marjoram seems to trigger massive apoptosis and has therefore been suggested as useful against the stubborn, triple negative p53 mutant type of breast cancer (Al Dhaheri et al., 2013).

Marjoram, while bearing many different names throughout history and frequently overlooked in favor of or conflated with oregano, has standing in its own right for its particular fragrance, revered since antiquity and still created today in Cyprus; for its connection with spasms related to the head and to the nervous system; its usefulness for respiratory, digestive, and urinary systems; its role in female reproductive health, and its promise for cancer research, among other indications. It is therefore worth revisiting marjoram with renewed eyes, whether in kitchen, practice, or scientific inquiry.

Adult Dose

Tincture: 1-4 mL, 1:3 strength, 45% alcohol*
Infusion: 6-10 grams
Essential Oil: 1-2 drops in gel cap with olive oil
Dosage information from *The Energetics of Western Herbs by Peter Holmes

Safety

Marjoram is an emmenagogue and is not used in pregnancy by some herbal practitioners due to its uterine stimulant action (Holmes, 2006), and it is speculatively contraindicated during early pregnancy (Brinker, 2010). Some herbal writers suggest that only small amounts should be used in tea for colic or flatulence because the oil is high in terpenes and polyphenols (Barker, 2001).
