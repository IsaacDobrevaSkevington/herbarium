Yarrow

Native To

Temperate and subarctic regions of the Northern Hemisphere in North America, Central America, Europe, the Middle East and Asia (Royal Botanic Gardens of Kew [RBGK], n.d.).

Geographic Distribution

In addition to its native range, yarrow has been introduced widely in other parts of the world, including South America, South Africa, Australia, and New Zealand (RBGK, n.d.).

Botanical Description

Donning a lacy bodice of leaves with a fragrance that possesses  deep, pungent, spicy notes and light, fresh, delicate floral scents, yarrow is an herbaceous perennial that many anticipate arriving in the spring. 

Despite its height being below eye level at 2-3 feet tall (Missouri Botanical Garden, n.d.), its signature double (sometimes triple) pinnately divided and lanceolate-shaped coarse leaves still manage to catch one’s attention and are the plant’s most identifiable and distinct embellishment (Madaus, 1938). These finely divided feathery and fern-like leaves grow in abundance on a simple, sturdy, erect stem, becoming smaller toward the top of the plant (Missouri Botanical Garden, n.d.). 

Dispersed along the stem are long, soft hairs that resemble the texture of wool; these hairs can also be found on the leaves, particularly when they are younger, but become hardly noticeable as they mature (Madaus, 1938). These leaves are admired and revered by many, with some proclaiming that “they are the most beautifully divided of those of any known plant” (Hill, 1812, p. 366). In fact, they inspired part of yarrow’s Latin name: mille means “thousand” and folium means “leaf,” thus the species name millefolium translates to “thousand leaf” (Madaus, 1938). 

Yarrow spreads by rhizomes, so each plant can have multiple flowering stems (Missouri Botanical Garden, n.d.). From summer to autumn, yarrow adorns itself with white flower heads, comprised of many small flowers, each one 0.4-0.6 mm wide (Madaus, 1938), that form flat clusters (corymbs) at the end of the stem (Missouri Botanical Garden, n.d.). Occasionally, the flowers of wild yarrow are pink; there are also many cultivars of varying colors including pinks and yellows (Foster, 1993), but these cultivars are often developed for ornamental purposes and are not typically used in herbalism (Soule, 2011).

Yarrow can be found in wide open spaces like fields, pastures, and occasionally in thin forests, and anywhere the ground has been disturbed, and is resistant to both hot and cold temperatures as well as drought; however, yarrow does not like extremely wet soil (Madaus, 1938).

Key Constituents

Volatile oil (e.g., α- and β-pinene, borneol, bornyl acetate, camphor, alpha-caryophyllene, 1,8 cineole [Hoffmann, 2003], thujone [Wood, 2009]), sesquiterpene lactones (e.g., achillicin, achillifolin, achillin, millifin, millifolide), flavonoids (e.g., apigenin, isorhamnetin, luteolin, rutin), tannins, phenolic acids (e.g., caffeic acid, salicylic acid), alkaloids (e.g., achiceine, achillein, moschatine, stachydrine, trigonelline), coumarins (Hoffmann, 2003), achilleic acid (aconitic acid), potassium, and calcium salts (Felter, 1922), vitamin C, bitters, and sterols (Wood, 2009).

Sustainability

Many look at yarrow as an intrusive weed (Missouri Botanical Garden, n.d.) given its ability to grow prolifically almost anywhere. Thus, there are no known sustainability issues for its use in herbalism.

Harvesting Guidelines

The plant is typically harvested when in flower (Moore, 2003; Wood, 1997). Many collect just the flowers, however, the top of the stems and the leaves also have potency and can be gathered with the flowers; some use the root occasionally as well. If collecting just the leaves, harvest young yarrow leaves in the spring before the plant flowers; leaves sometimes reappear in the fall and can also be harvested at that time (Wood, 1997). It is best to harvest this plant in the morning after the dew dries and before the sun’s heat evaporates the lighter volatile oils. Place the herb loosely in bags or hang in bunches to dry.

Uses

Transcending time, traversing geographies and cultures, and possessing a reputation of strength and elegance, yarrow is one of the oldest and most well-known plants in Western herbalism and has been used in a variety of cultures and herbal traditions. With a wide variety of applications, yarrow is considered an essential herb for home use and no first aid kit is complete without it since it is both antiseptic and styptic (staunches bleeding). This humble plant is a vulnerary, febrifuge, diaphoretic, aromatic bitter, and blood mover (Easley & Horne, 2016; Wood, 2007). 

Yarrow has been used from at least the time of the ancient Greeks and Egyptians (McIntyre, 1996), with the greatest of the Greek warriors championing one of the plant’s botanical classifications. Yarrow’s genus name Achillea hails from Achilles, the Greek hero who purportedly gained his renowned invulnerability when his mother dipped him in a bath of yarrow tea at birth (all but the heel by which she held him), and who used yarrow on the wounds of his friends (Berger, 1998). In fact, yarrow was used from the time of the ancient Greeks up until the American Civil War to staunch soldiers’ wounds and support the body’s natural process, hence its common names: woundwort, soldiers’ woundwort, staunch weed, nosebleed, and carpenter’s weed (Berger, 1998; McIntyre, 1996; Wood, 1997). 

The earliest documented mentions of the traditional uses of yarrow in Western herbalism include the works of Dioscorides (40-90 CE), a Greek physician, pharmacologist, and botanist, and Pliny the Elder (23-79 CE), a Roman statesman and naturalist (Applequist & Moerman, 2011). However, it’s important to note that oral lore pertaining to use of this plant could have dated back further in history than when yarrow first made its appearance in medical texts, as is the case for most plants that were used for their herbal actions (Applequist & Moerman, 2011).

Dioscorides (70/2000) considered this plant, which he referred to as achilleios or millefolium, exceptional for fistulas, ulcers, inflammation, and dysentery, and suggested its use to stop bleeding from wounds or from abnormally heavy or prolonged menstruation (menorrhagia). Pliny (77/1855) noted in Natural History that mentions of achilleos, millefolia, and sideritis were likely referencing the well-known plant yarrow, despite some of the names being used for other plant species that were also applicable for wounds. He also recorded uses of yarrow for bleeding including excessive menstruation, earaches, toothaches, and loose stools (Pliny, 77/1855).

The Muslim physician and philosopher Ibn-Sina (1025/1998), also known as Avicenna in the West, observed yarrow to be energetically cold and moistening, a blood thinner, and an aperient, clearing obstructions. Ibn-Sina thought yarrow’s steam to be a nasal decongestant, a sitz bath of the herb to regulate menses, and its internal use to open the uterus and aid in expelling fetal or placental tissue; yarrow was also respected in Islamic tradition as a kidney stone dissolver and headache aid (Ibn-Sina, 1025/1998).

In medieval herbal books, yarrow is touted for its application as a styptic (Madaus, 1938), and is mentioned as a common ingredient in formulas for inflammation, swelling, wounds including snake and dog bites, toothache, headache, heartburn, respiratory complaints, digestive cramping, difficulty urinating, and diarrhea (Applequist & Moerman, 2011). Furthermore, in a discovery that helps to set in stone its ethnobotanical lifespan, pollen grains of yarrow have been found in Neanderthal burial sites dated to be at least 50,000 years old (Shipley & Kindscher, 2016), and chemical constituents found in yarrow and chamomile were found in the dental calculus of Neanderthal remains from El Sidrón, Spain, thought to be at least 47,300 years old (Hardy et al., 2012). 

Yarrow enjoys a long history of use and versatility in its native territories throughout the Middle East. A variety of Achillea species grow wild in Iran, where they are known as “bumadaran” in the Persian language and used commonly to support issues of the gastrointestinal tract (Németh & Bernáth, 2008, as cited in Moradi et al., 2013). These Iranian species, of which there are nineteen, are also heralded as herbal supports in cases of headache, spasms, respiratory infections, liver issues, and occasional sleeplessness (Angourani et al., 2023). Yarrow’s contemporary uses are underscored in Persian traditions, where it is used to support wound health, pneumonia, rheumatism, and blood loss (Saeidnia et al., 2011). In Anatolia, yarrow represents one of the main economically important native plants, and Turkish tradition calls for tea made from Achillea species to support abdominal distress and gas (Honda et al., 1996). One Iranian study  found that a commonly used antimicrobial mouthwash called persica, containing a trio of toothache tree or Salvadora persica, yarrow, and spearmint (Mentha spicata), aids periodontal disease and reduces inflammation of the gums (Moeintaghavi et al., 2012). 

Jewish herbalists Cohen & Siegel (2021) note that yarrow was included in a formula for muscular strain and discomfort used in the Pale of Settlement in Russia, along with St. John’s wort (Hypericum perforatum), English oak (Quercus robur) bark, and an assortment of other herbs. Folk herbalists in Lachovitz used yarrow in conjunction with St. John’s wort to support headaches associated with dizziness or anemia, both by way of drinking an infusion internally or using it as a hair rinse (Cohen & Siegel, 2021). In a survey of two towns in the Hamedan province of Iran, both villages were found to use leaf and flower, and in some cases root, of Achillea biebersteinii and A. wilhelmsii for indigestion, backache, fever, and hypothermia, among other uses (Naghibi et al., 2014).

Many First Nations tribes of North America benefited from the use of yarrow. The Cree tribe uses a cold infusion of the whole plant for earaches and burns, and a hot preparation for stomach pain, fevers, and to encourage menstruation; just the leaves are used for diabetes, and the flowers or fresh root for teething pain (Young et al., 2015). The Woodland Cree dry the flowers to mask the scent of traps, as well as using it in a bath or as a poultice for arthritis, earning it the name “bone medicine” (Young et al., 2015). Yarrow is known as “white flower,” “bee plant,” “heart medicine,” and “head medicine” among different Cree regions, reflecting its importance and value amongst the tribe (Young et al., 2015). 

Russell Willier, a Cree healer, uses yarrow roots along with seneca root (Polygala senega) to address chest pains and heart issues (Young et al., 2015), as yarrow’s alkamines help reduce inflammation, including vascular inflammation (Muller-Jakic et al., 1994 & Dall’Acqua et al., 2011, as cited in Young et al., 2015). He also applies yarrow flowers to open sores, insect bites, and stings (Young et al., 2015).

The use of yarrow is also predominant among the Chumash people residing in the central to southern areas of the California coast; they primarily use yarrow for ailments such as arthritis, colds, headaches, and tooth pain, and also apply it externally to address issues like hemorrhaging, swelling, and wounds (Garcia & Adams, 2009). In fact, the Chumash tribe is known to have used yarrow for broken bones by “holding the [afflicted] area over steaming yarrow leaves, then binding the area with steamed buckskin strips” (Garcia & Adams, 2009, p. 13).

While some tribes primarily used yarrow leaves, Josephine Peters (2016), a Native healer and respected elder from the Karuk Tribe in northern California, highlights the use of the plant’s flowering tops. Peters notes that the flowers were prepared as a warm gargle to ease sore throats and as a tea to allay fevers, supporting some of its best-known uses.

Yarrow has a deeply rooted history of being adored, so the uses of this plant span far and wide. In fact, it was so favored that in Sweden it was periodically offered as a substitute for tobacco (Nicotiana spp.) or hops (Humulus lupulus) (Madaus, 1938). Historically in England, yarrow was predominantly used for hemorrhoids, as an emmenagogue to stimulate menstrual flow, and to address bedwetting (enuresis) in children (Madaus, 1938). In Italy, it is primarily used for gastrointestinal complaints (Guarrera, 2007, as cited in Boscaro et al., 2022). 

In 1938, Dr. Gerhard Madaus described that in Central European traditions, the young spring herb is traditionally used in an herb soup for Maundy Thursday, also known as Holy Thursday, observed on the Thursday before the Easter holiday to memorialize the last supper of Jesus Christ; furthermore, the leaves are used alongside onion skins to naturally dye Easter eggs in celebration of the Christian holiday.

Herbalists in Russia use yarrow for its muscle relaxing properties that are beneficial to the intestines by helping to reduce flatulence and abdominal bloating, as well as the urinary tract (Zevin et al.,1997). They suggest using yarrow juice or a decoction or extract preparation to ease gastritis and peptic ulcers and also to increase appetite as it promotes liver bile flow (Zevin et al.,1997). In this tradition, yarrow is also used to lower blood pressure, prevent kidney and gallstone formation, and address various skin conditions; a compress made with fresh yarrow juice and olive oil is suggested for skin infections like boils and skin tuberculosis, but can also be applied on stiff hands, sore nipples, and the scalp in cases of extreme hair loss (Zevin et al.,1997).

Yarrow has been used similarly for stomach issues and menstrual irregularities in Ukrainian folk tradition, and in Belarus it is known by the name “blood herb,” with an infusion of the flowers indicated for anemia and light or infrequent menstrual flow (Madaus, 1938). As German doctor and author Gerhard Madaus (1938) notes, the whole herb has traditionally been prepared as a cold infusion for stopping nosebleeds or freshly squeezed to make a juice that could be taken internally or applied topically for wounds.

The blood herb moniker is quite similar to herbalist Matthew Wood’s (2009) designation of the plant as “master of the blood” (p. 52) and is indicative of a long-known fact: yarrow can modulate one of the most vital components of the human body. Yarrow has the ability to regulate blood flow through the skin, capillaries, and venules, and can thicken or thin the blood to address various complaints such as bruising, wounds, hemorrhaging, and clotting (Wood, 2009). An herbal sitz bath containing yarrow can be used to help aid irritation from hemorrhoids, and to guard against them recurring (White, 2022). Wood (2007) states that yarrow can “decongest blood associated with inflammation, thin stagnant, congealed blood, tone the veins, [stimulate] the capillaries and arteries, and move the blood to or from the surface” (para. 1). 

As a cardiac tonic, yarrow eases hypertension by lowering blood pressure through dilation of peripheral cells (Hoffmann, 2003) and taking pressure off the heart by relaxing the arteries (Hedley, 1994; Hedley, 1995). Yarrow’s astringent action tones the blood vessels and circulatory system (Bennett, 2014), which may in turn support the plant’s diaphoretic (increasing circulation to the skin) or diuretic (increasing circulation to the kidneys) actions (Hedley, 1995).

Yarrow’s ability to regulate the flow of blood, along with its antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, astringent, and analgesic actions, make it ideal for avoiding infection, staunching blood flow, and easing pain. Herbalist Robin Rose Bennett (2014) uses yarrow for soothing swelling, especially when caused by poisonous bites and stings; she also uses yarrow on fresh wounds, and notes that it can be applied to both old and new bruises. To stop bleeding and disinfect a wound, one can apply a poultice of fresh yarrow leaves, a pinch of dried yarrow leaf powder, or a squirt of yarrow extract to a wound, ideally after cleansing (Bennett, 2014).

Since yarrow is renowned for its blood-regulating abilities, it has quite an extensive list of related indications, including its traditional use as an organ styptic (especially for bleeding from the nose, lungs, bladder, uterus, and/or rectum) (Madaus, 1938). However, it’s important to note that internal bleeding may be indicative of a serious health issue and should always be addressed with a qualified healthcare provider.

In yet another blood-related benefit, yarrow essential oil has been found to have an inhibitory effect against Babesia canis, a canine parasitic disease commonly transmitted through tick bites. Guz et al. (2021) conducted a study where B. canis parasites from dogs’ blood were exposed to yarrow essential oil in vitro, building on previous research that identified yarrow as one of the most effective essential oils against B. canis compared to eight other volatile oils, including clove (Eugenia caryophyllus) and pomelo (Citrus grandis). The study revealed that yarrow essential oil inhibited parasite growth by 29.6% at 0.01mg/ml and 73.6% at 0.1 mg/ml, possibly due to the sesquiterpene compounds, which accounted for 77.38% of the total oil (Guz et al., 2021).

However, yarrow’s anti-parasitic actions have also been attributed to additional constituents. The flavonoids apigenin, luteolin, and rutin have shown antiparasitic activity against Plasmodium falciparum by inhibiting enzymes involved in plasmodial type II fatty acid biosynthesis (as cited in Vitalini et al., 2011).

Yarrow’s volatile oils are responsible for its characteristic strong aroma and help to give the plant its bitter taste, which can be tempered by adding more pleasant-tasting herbs to a formula. Through its bitter and astringent constituents, yarrow stimulates the appetite, enhances absorption, and tones digestive organs (Wood, 2007). It is also antispasmodic, and helps ease gastrointestinal distress by relaxing the smooth muscles of the digestive tract (Castleman, 1995), and it has been used for digestive imbalances that are spastic in nature such as constipation, diarrhea, colic, dyspepsia, gastritis, enteritis, and hyperacidity (Madaus, 1938). Matthew Wood (2007) suggests a cold cup of yarrow tea to stimulate the stomach, digestion, and kidneys, as opposed to a hot tea for addressing fevers. As herbalist Richard Lawrence Hool (1922) declared of yarrow,

No remedy with which I am acquainted is more to be depended upon in chronic affections of the mucous surfaces of the internal organs. Its value in this respect is peculiarly apparent in chronic dysentery, diarrhea, and other diseases of the bowels (para. 11).

Furthermore, applying a yarrow compress or poultice over the liver area is indicated for liver problems, especially if caused by dietary changes, as it can stimulate liver function and enhance detoxification (Wenzel, 2014). Maintaining proper liver detoxification is crucial for hormonal balance and the elimination of excess hormones, such as estrogen; for this purpose, yarrow leaf and flower can be prepared as a tincture or infusion (White, 2022). 

The relaxing, antispasmodic nature of yarrow is also helpful for menstrual cramps, and yarrow can also help lessen heavy menstrual flows (Edwards, 2000). In fact, it is considered “one of our best agents for the relief of menorrhagia” (Felter & Lloyd, 1898, p. 20). A tincture of yarrow, known for its uterine tonic and stimulant properties, and shepherd’s purse (Capsella bursa-pastoris), an herb with hemostatic and styptic properties, can be taken as needed to address heavy menstrual bleeding (White, 2022). To ease premenstrual syndrome and menstrual cramps, a combination of yarrow, chasteberry (Vitex agnus-castus), and lady’s mantle (Alchemilla mollis) can be used in a vinegar extract (Wenzel, 2014) or tincture form. It is thought that flavonoids are responsible for yarrow’s antispasmodic activity here (Hoffmann, 2003). 

Yarrow has some application externally during pregnancy and postpartum. Yarrow may be used topically during pregnancy for varicosities and hemorrhoids, along with black tea (Camellia sinensis), witch hazel (Hamamelis virginiana), and white oak (Quercus alba) bark (Romm, 2018). Midwives greatly value yarrow extract or tea for helping slow and halt the bleeding of a postpartum hemorrhage and regulate the blood flow after delivery (Romm, 2018). Some herbalists and midwives suggest that yarrow leaf and flower powder may also be used on the cord stump of a newborn, helping astringe the tissues, stave off irritation or infection, and speed the natural process of scabbing over. Yarrow, honey, and breastmilk were found to be equally useful in aiding nipple fissures while breastfeeding (Firouzabadi, 2020). 

Yarrow can also support the bladder and help to address any bladder control problems (e.g., enuresis) (Madaus, 1938). Yarrow’s astringency tones the tissues of the urinary tract (Hardin, n.d.). Furthermore, yarrow’s antimicrobial activity is specifically indicated for urinary tract infections and cystitis because it also doubles as a diuretic (de la Forêt & Han, 2020; Hoffmann, 2003). Dr. Aviva Romm (2023) suggests yarrow in combination with uva ursi (Arctostaphylos uva-ursi) and marshmallow (Althaea officinalis) root for bladder infection. It is considered an excellent herb to add to a formula for strangury, bladder irritation and frequent urination, and cases of blood in the urine (hematuria) (Hool, 1922; Wood, 2009). Herbalist John Hill (1812) declared yarrow a gentle and useful herb for ureteral ulcers due to its diuretic action. Richard Lawrence Hool (1922) summarized yarrow’s benefits for the urinary tract system well when he said that “in almost all affections of the kidneys and bladder it may be beneficially employed, as it resolves mucous deposits and deterges and heals abraded mucous surfaces” (para. 26).

Hool (1922) notes that due to its diuretic and alterative properties, yarrow can also be an appropriate herb for dissolving uric acid crystals and other hard calculus deposits. This also makes yarrow a beneficial plant for addressing arthritic conditions, including gout and rheumatism. It can be combined with buchu (Agathosma betulina) leaf, goldenrod (Solidago spp.) aerial parts, pellitory of the wall (Parietaria judaica) aerial parts, sea holly (Eryngium maritimum) root, and/or uva ursi (Arctostaphylos uva-ursi) leaf in formulations for these concerns (Hool, 1922).

Yarrow is a great ally for colds and flu due to its antiviral action; it is ideal anytime there is a fever involved, as it is a renowned diaphoretic (Easley & Horne, 2016; Hardin, n.d.). Matthew Wood (2009) states that it is the “master of fever” (p. 52) due to this action and its ability to move blood to the skin’s surface, dissipating heat as needed and controlling the balance of fluids. By dilating the arteries to stimulate circulation and open pores, yarrow stimulates sweating and allows heat to move outward, thus breaking the fever. Yarrow is best taken as a hot tea for its diaphoretic action (Chevallier, 2007) and can be mixed with elder blossoms (Sambucus nigra), lemon balm (Melissa officinalis), and peppermint (Mentha x piperita) at the onset of a cold or the flu to help flush out the illness from the system and promote a faster return to health (Cummings & Holmes, 2017). 

Additionally, yarrow can address cough, bronchitis, or excess mucus in the lungs (catarrh) (Madaus, 1938). This anticatarrhal action is useful for sinusitis and upper respiratory congestion and is due to its volatile oils; these chemical compounds help promote the healthy flow and elimination of mucus, especially from the sinuses, and yarrow’s anti-inflammatory and astringent nature reduces swelling in sinus tissue, further allowing mucus to flow more easily (Bennett, 2014; Hoffmann, 2003).

Energetically, yarrow is considered both warming and cooling; its ability to move blood can help the body either release or reserve heat as needed (Wood, 2009). This is most likely due to the plant’s ability to move in whichever direction is needed by the body to normalize function and bring balance, a characteristic known in herbalism as amphoteric (Gladstar, 2012). It’s this amphoteric quality that makes yarrow indicated in so many varying conditions, from amenorrhea to hemorrhage. As Matthew Wood (2009) states, “Remedies with contradictory but complementary properties are often of great utility since they are able to normalize opposing conditions. This is true for yarrow” (p. 52). Despite its dual nature tendencies, most herbalists agree that yarrow is drying (Easley & Horne, 2016; Kress, 2012).  

While the focus of this monograph is on the use of yarrow flower and leaf, it is interesting to note a topical use of its essential oil that is particularly noteworthy due to the increasing prevalence of diagnosed Lyme disease infections. Research shows that fresh applications of yarrow essential oil exert a significant repelling effect on both nymph and adult deer ticks (Ixodes scapularis) and so it holds promise as a repellent in this way (Pickett, 2023).

Chinese yarrow (Achillea alpina), also referred to as Siberian yarrow or alpine yarrow, is the native species of yarrow in China and has a long history of use in Chinese folk medicine for various ailments, such as menopausal challenges, abdominal pain, acute intestinal inflammation, wound infection, and snake bites (Huang, 1999). In fact, modern Chinese medicine still employs this herb to address these same complaints (Li, 2009). A. millefolium is also utilized in Chinese medicine interchangeably with A. alpina; the dried herb is used to slow menstrual and hemorrhoidal bleeding, while the fresh herb is used to soothe wounds, sores, dog bites, and snake bites (Li, 2009). 

Yarrow is also mentioned in Shen Nong Ben Cao Jing or The Herbal Classic of the Divine Plowman, which is the oldest surviving Chinese materia medica (Huang, 1999; Nugent-Head, 2014). The book is a meticulous selection of 365 herbs, meant to correspond to the number of days in a year, rather than a comprehensive compilation of all herbs used during the Eastern Han Dynasty, and has had such a significant impact that it has been referenced in every materia medica that has come after it (Nugent-Head, 2014). 

The 365 herbs in the Shen Nong Ben Cao Jing are divided into three categories: 120 upper herbs that can be consumed daily to enhance longevity, vitality, and spirit, 120 middle herbs that are used to ward off and resist ailments, and 125 lower herbs that are taken in smaller amounts for shorter durations to address issues and “clear pathogens that have invaded the body” (Nugent-Head, 2014, p. 26). Yarrow is classified as an upper herb “prescribed in quantity and/or prescribed for a long time [that] will not injure the person [and used if one] desires to lighten the body, benefit qi and lengthen the lifespan” (Nugent-Head, 2014, p. 24).

Yarrow has even transcended the physical world, with the stalks being used with the I Ching, the legendary ancient Chinese divination system thought to be written around 1000 BCE and “one of the oldest books in the world” (O’Brien, 2007, para. 2). Traditionally, 50 yarrow stalks would be sorted out to develop yin or yang lines to form a hexagram that would then be interpreted as messages or prophecies (O’Brien, 2007).

Yarrow is a plant of legend and in the words of Richard Lawrence Hool (1922), “yarrow will not fail you” (para. 16) and “it is sometimes slow in its action, but mild, certain, and radical in its operation” (para. 11). Perhaps it is no coincidence then that yarrow grows prolifically in fields and gardens and makes itself so available to us.

Adult Dose

Tincture: 2-4 mL (1:5, 25%) 3x/day (Hoffmann, 2003). 

Tea: 1 cup (1-2 tsp dried aerial parts in 8 fl oz water) 3x/day; during fevers this tea can be drunk hourly (Hoffmann, 2003).

Safety

People who are sensitive to plants in the Asteraceae family may be sensitive to yarrow (Hoffmann, 2003); skin irritation or contact dermatitis may occur in highly sensitive individuals (Madaus, 1938). Yarrow is not recommended during pregnancy due to its emmenagogue action (Romm, 2018), and its safety has not been established for use during lactation (Gardner & McGuffin, 2013).
