Dong quai

Angelica sinensis (Oliv.) Diels

Native To

Endemic to China (Leon & Yu-Lin, 2017), the plant thrives in the cool, shady mountain woods found in the southern and western region of the country (Kuhn & Winston, 2005).

Geographic Distribution

Gansu province of northern China (Foster & Chongxi, 1992; Leon & Yu-Lin, 2017); Hubei, Ningxia, Shaanxi, Sichuan, and Yunnan provinces of China (Foster & Chongxi, 1992; Upton, 2003); Japan (Chevallier, 2000), Korea, Taiwan, United Kingdom, Lapland, and Iceland (Thorne Research, 2004).

Botanical Description

Like other plants in the Apiaceae family, A. sinensis has pinnate, slightly feathery leaves and an upright growth pattern, reaching up to 4 feet in height (Foster & Chongxi, 1992). Leaves grow from a single ribbed stem and have inflated sheaths which are especially notable on the basal and lower leaves (Leon & Yu-Lin, 2017). The leaflets are ovate in shape, with toothed margins and fine white hairs that can be seen mostly on the leaf veins and margins (Upton, 2003).

Dong quai typically blooms from June to July (Upton, 2003), and the inflorescences are umbellate with small white flowers (Leon & Yu-Lin, 2017). The fruits, known as schizocarps, are characteristic of the Apiaceae family but are distinguishable from other species only by subtle morphological differences (Foster & Chongxi, 1992). Dong quai has a sturdy taproot (Leon & Yu-Lin, 2017), and the central body of the root is short (¾-1¼ inch long) (Foster & Chongxi, 1992), squat, and divides into many twisted rootlets (Noe, 1998). The best quality roots are 4-10 inches long, plump, and have a yellowish-white color (Foster & Chongxi, 1992) once the bumpy and gnarled outside of the root is scraped off (Upton, 2003). The root then resembles carved ivory (Gladstar, 1993). The root crown has a swollen appearance and horizontal lenticels used for gas exchange can be clearly seen (Foster & Chongxi, 1992). Purple or pale green remains of stems and petioles are present in freshly harvested roots (Applequist, 2006). Upon the root being broken, the odor is aromatic and the fracture short and granular (Mills, 1985, as cited in Noe, 1998). When sliced, several layers of cork, narrow cortex, and a ring of phloem with profuse small, brown secretory cavities can be seen (Applequist, 2006).

Key Constituents

Alkylphthalides, ferulic acid, and polysaccharides (Upton, 2003); volatile oils (Braun & Cohen, 2010) such as ligustilide (Kuhn & Winston, 2005), cadinene, carvacrol, safrole, and isosafrole (Braun & Cohen, 2010); coumarins (Mills & Bone, 2005); psoralens (Kuhn & Winston, 2005); vitamin A  E, & B12, amino acids, carotenoids, folinic acid, phytosterols, calcium and magnesium (Thorne Research, 2004).

Sustainability

Wild populations in the ancient forests of Gansu are thought to be endangered or may no longer exist (Leon & Yu-Lin, 2017). Factors such as limited wild populations, dong quai’s popularity in Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) formulas, and global trends in herbal medicine require that many of the commercial supplies of dong quai come from cultivated plant populations in China. These are then exported primarily to manufacturers or herb distributors located around the world (Upton, 2003).

In the Gansu province, small-scale household cultivation is still common and oftentimes the main income source for farming families (Giacomelli et al., 2017). Production in Gansu accounts for 90% of the Chinese production of dong quai, resulting in more than 6,000 tons being harvested each year. Roots from this region are considered to be the highest quality medicinal material possible, thus categorized as daodi yaocai (Giacomelli et al., 2017). The classification of daodi is well-documented in TCM and dates back to the 13th Century, and is based on the belief that the unique soil, water, and environment in particular areas result in especially potent medicinal plants with increased therapeutic action (Zhao et al., 2012).

Harvesting Guidelines

Roots are typically harvested in the late autumn. The plants are dug up in their second or third year of growth (Upton, 2003); roots harvested before this age are considered inferior (Foster & Chongxi, 1992) due to their small size and low volatile oil content. Size determines the quality grade of the root, with the larger specimens being the most expensive. The highest-quality roots are those that are highly aromatic (Giacomelli et al., 2017), oily in appearance, pliable to the touch, and have a yellowish-white interior (Upton, 2003).

Since the roots are prone to rot, it is common practice amongst growers to stop watering the plants for the last few months before harvest to protect the root quality (Foster & Chongxi, 1992). Any rough handling or contact with water can cause the roots to bruise or turn black, so care is taken to keep them dry and handle them carefully (Foster & Chongxi, 1992).

Different regions process dong quai differently. In Gansu, once the mature roots are harvested they are dried under shade structures and smoked, while in Yunnan, the roots are intentionally dried directly in the sun (Foster & Chongxi, 1992). Other traditional preparations include slicing the root, adding rice wine at a 1:10 ratio, then gently roasting until dry (Foster & Chongxi, 1992).

Uses

Names can provide a glimpse into the history of a plant, allowing herbalists and other botanical enthusiasts perspective on the herbal actions, point of origin, or significant botanists that brought it to the attention of the Western European world. A. sinensis, with its centuries of use in Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM), has a rich story to tell. The genus Angelica is derived from the word angelus, meaning “angelic,” and may reference its “cordial and medicinal properties” (Foster & Chongxi, 1992, p. 66), as well as its sweet taste. Sinensis is derived from the Latin word Sina, meaning China, thus acknowledging the plant’s country of origin (Upton, 2003).  Synonyms include Angelica polymorpha var. sinensis Oliv. (Roskov et al., 2019).

Stories of dong quai abound in Chinese medical literature and provide linguistic context behind some of its common names. One legend involves a young man who loved his new wife but had to leave home and travel to the mountains to prove his bravery. After years of being gone and presumed dead, he returned to find his wife remarried. Both were heartbroken by the situation and, bereft, she became sick. She was only able to return to health after taking dong quai, which her former husband had gathered while away in the mountains (Foster & Chongxi, 1992). One direct translation of the Chinese name dang gui as “returned to the husband’s home” (Hsu et al., 1986) can be interpreted as paying homage to this story. Another translation, “proper order,” (Foster & Chongxi, 1992) acknowledges its balancing properties and the herb’s strength in nourishing the blood and regulating the body’s qi, key pillars of health in Chinese medicine (Upton, 2003).

While only officially included in the Pharmacopoeia of the People’s Republic of China in 1977 (Upton, 2003), dong quai has an extensive history of being used in herbalism. It first appeared almost 2000 years ago in The Divine Husbandman’s Classic of the Materia Medica (Shen Nong Ben Cao Jing, authors unknown) during the Han Dynasty (Foster & Chongxi, 1992). In this text, it was named as a remedy for vaginal infections and infertility, and its topical use was indicated for skin diseases, ulcers, boils, and wounds (Foster & Chongxi, 1992; Noe, 1998). It is included in more than 70 formulas in the Chinese Pharmacopoeia (The Pharmacopoeia Commission of the Ministry of Health of the People’s Republic of China, 2010) and in numerous significant formulas, including the classic Jiao Ai Tang formula, which translates as “ass-hide gelatin and mugwort decoction” (Upton, 2003, p. 2). This formula warms the uterus, nourishes the blood and regulates menstruation (Penner, 2017). A. sinensis is one of the main ingredients in the “Four Things Soup” (Shou Wu Chih) recipe, which is used to increase energy levels during the daytime while supporting deep, restful sleep at night (Noe, 1998). It is also a key ingredient in the “Four Substance Decoction” (Si Wu Tang) which is considered to be the most important blood-nourishing formula in Chinese medicine (Cheung et al.1983, as cited in Upton, 2003).

Within the lens of TCM, dang gui refers to the entire root, and this is divided into separate parts, each with their own name and distinct herbal properties: dang gui tou describes the root crown, dang gui shen the root body, and dang gui wei refers only to the “root tails” or rootlets (Upton, 2003). The root crown is used to stop bleeding, the root body to build and nourish the blood, while the tail is used to “quicken” the blood (Wei et al., 2008). The root crown can still be found in Asian markets but it is not typically found in American markets (Upton, 2003).

Dong quai is used to strengthen the heart, lung, and liver meridians, while harmonizing the blood (Braun & Cohen, 2010) and lubricating the bowel (Fang  et al., 2012). In formulas, it is used in China alongside astragalus (Astragalus membranaceus) in cases of nephrotic kidneys (Thorne Research, 2004). While its gynecological uses are well-known, it also has broader indications for supporting the cardiovascular (Romm, 2010), respiratory (Barnes et al., 2010), and gastrointestinal systems (Foster & Chongxi, 1992).

In modern day Chinese medicine, the plant is used for invigorating and replenishing the blood, moistening the intestines, and easing pain. It is employed in cases of menstrual disorders and as an emollient and laxative for chronic constipation, especially in older individuals (Fang et al., 2012). In Chinese and Japanese medicine, dong quai is always formulated with other herbs, while research has focused on in vitro or animal studies. This has made it somewhat difficult to isolate its pharmacodynamic mechanisms or determine the clinical significance of A. sinensis as a stand-alone herb (Braun & Cohen, 2010; Romm, 2010).

Wide use in TCM formulas for supporting reproductive imbalances has contributed to its broad acceptance in the West as primarily a tonic herb able to balance gynecological issues (Upton, 2003) with a steady and gentle hand. This contributes to its reputation as the “female ginseng” (Bartram, 1998). Traditionally, it has been used over a long-term to nourish and support the reproductive organs (Gladstar, 1993) and encourage normal functioning of the womb (Glenville, 2001). Dong quai has also traditionally been used to rebuild the blood after loss due to monthly menstruation and to ease pain and pelvic congestion often associated with the menses (Easley & Horne, 2016).

Due to its ability to stimulate circulation to the lower pelvis, A. sinensis has been indicated in cases of menstrual clotting, congealed blood (Noe, 1998), and other conditions arising from the “disordered female reproductive system” (Bartram, 1998, p. 154). Research has shown that it may support healthy prostaglandin levels, an important factor in cases of women presenting with painful periods (Glenville, 2001). Prostagladin may also play a role in migraines which coincide with menstruation. Prophylactic dosing of a formula which included A. sinensis alongside soy isoflavones and black cohosh (Actaea racemosa) significantly decreased the severity of migraine pain in a study involving 49 individuals over the course of 6 months (Burke et al., 2002).

Since dong quai supports healthy reproductive function and is especially useful when there are irregularities, it has been used by herbalists to ease the transition into menopause (Gladstar, 1993). Its ability to normalize and stabilize blood vessels may be helpful in reducing the number of hot flashes experienced during menopause especially when the traditional assessment is one that includes blood deficiency or statis (Hobbs & Keville, 2007). In one clinical study involving 50 pre- and post-menopausal women, a multi-herb formula containing dong quai was taken for 3 months and reduced hot flashes by 73% and night sweats by 69% (Rotem & Kaplan, 2009), although it’s impossible to identify the role of dong quai in particular in that result. In a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled clinical study involving 100 menopausal women in Hong Kong, a formula which included. A. membranaceus and A. sinensis significantly reduced mild hot flashes compared to placebo but, interestingly enough, not moderate to severe hot flashes (Haines et al., 2008).

While dong quai retains its reputation as one of the most useful tonic plants for menstrual issues (Braun & Cohen, 2010; Gladstar, 1993), oftentimes this overshadows its other uses as an herb to protect the liver, build the blood, ease pain, and increase circulation. Volatile oils act as antispasmodics in the body (Foster & Chongxi, 1992), and this is helpful when reducing pain, especially when related to muscle spasms. As a compress, it has been used specifically for pleurisy and bronchitis, while its bitter aromatic qualities lend well to its use as a carminative in cases of flatulent dyspepsia (Barnes et al., 2007). A decoction of the root has strong antibacterial qualities (Foster & Chongxi, 1992) and its high volatile oil content makes it an effective antifungal (Tabanca et al., 2008).

A range of chemical constituents are found in dong quai and contribute to this plant’s actions. Ligustilide and ferulic acid are often considered the primary biologically active constituents and are used in assessing the quality and identification of dong quai, however, this is problematic because neither of these chemicals are unique to Angelica sinensis, nor do they represent the complete activity of the plant (Upton, 2003).

The volatile oil content of the root is approximately 0.4-0.7%, and the majority consists of ligustilide but other constituents include cadinene, carvacrol, safrole, and isosafrole (Braun & Cohen, 2010). The distinct odor of the root has been linked to alkylphthalide (Sheu et al., 1987), one of the key volatile oils. Some of the volatile oil compounds have been linked to an antispasmodic effect on the uterus in vivo and in the respiratory system (Kuhn & Winston, 2005), but a uterine stimulant effect has been associated with the aqueous extract in vitro (Mills & Bone, 2000). The histamine-blocking actions of phthalides on cell receptors and their effect on calcium ion channels may explain the spasmolytic qualities of the herb, but more testing must be done to determine this with certainty (Romm, 2010).

A. acutiloba and A. gigas have been used as substitutes for A. sinensis due to their similar antispasmodic effects (Foster & Chongxi, 1992). Levisticum officinale (also known as European dong quai) is regarded as an additional substitute but is seen as inferior compared to A. sinensis (Zschocke et al., 1998).

Adult Dose

Tincture: 2 to 4 mL (1:5 in 70%) up to 3x/day (Easley & Horne, 2016; Kuhn & Winston, 2005)

Capsules: 500 to 600 mg capsules, up to 6 per day (Kuhn & Winston, 2005)

Topical: Apply as a compress for pleurisy and bronchitis (Barnes et al., 2010)

Decoction: 4.5 to 9 g per day (Braun & Cohen, 2010) or 30 g in 1 L of water simmered for 10-20 minutes, then consume 2-4 ounces 3x/day (Easley & Horne, 2016)

 

Safety

Dong quai’s main action focuses on the pelvis. This is supremely helpful in cases of abdominal stagnancy, as seen in amenorrhea. This characteristic is less desirable in cases of excessive motility, such as acute diarrhea. Because of its stimulating effect on the lower abdomen, its use is to be avoided when individuals are experiencing loose stools (Foster & Chongxi, 1992).

It is classed as pregnancy category C by Mills & Bone (2005), denoting that its use is contraindicated during the first trimester of pregnancy or in those prone to miscarriage (McGuffin et al., 1997). Consumption of the herb while nursing may lead to a rash on the newborn, so caution is advised (Romm, 2010).

Use during an individual’s menstrual cycle may stimulate heavy bleeding, so it is advised to avoid dong quai at least one week before menstruation begins (Gladstar, 1993; Kuhn & Winston, 2005). For the same reason, it is advised to discontinue its use at least 7 days prior to surgery due to possible interference with platelet aggregation (Gardner & McGuffin, 2013).

The outcomes of studies on the estrogenic qualities of A. sinensis are mixed: some results indicate that it has weak estrogen receptor binding capacity (Liu et al., 2001) while others show no estrogenic activity (Klein et al., 2003). Despite this confusion, caution is still recommended for those who have hormone-sensitive tumors (Braun & Cohen, 2010).

One case study has shown that concurrent use with antiplatelet drugs may potentiate the medication (Page & Lawrence, 1999), so its use should be avoided if an individual is taking warfarin or a similarly acting drug (Mills & Bone, 2005).
