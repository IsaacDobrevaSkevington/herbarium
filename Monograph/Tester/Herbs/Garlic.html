Native To

Central Asia: Iran, Kazakhstan, Kirgizstan, Tadzhikistan, Turkmenistan, and Uzbekistan (Royal Botanic Gardens Kew [RBGK], n.d.).

Geographic Distribution

Cultivated worldwide.

Botanical Description

Garlic has leaves that are long, narrow, lightly ribbed, and thin, like grass. The underground part is called the bulb, which is a compound of bulbettes called cloves held together by a thin, paper-like, covering. The flower occurs on the end of a cylindrical stalk, or scape, which rises directly from the bulb and terminates in a set of bulbils that open to form garlic’s large, round, umbel false flower cluster that is usually white or a light pink, depending on the cultivar variety and soil quality. Garlic is distinguishable from members of the Liliaceae family by its pungent, sulfurous smell. If a plant looks like garlic but does not smell like garlic, it is NOT garlic and can be potentially toxic.  

Key Constituents

Alliin, allicin,, enzymes (allinase), terpenes (citral, geraniol, linalool), B vitamins, minerals, flavonoids (quercetin), sulfur, selenium, ajoenes, vinyldithiins, and phosphorus (Batiha et al., 2020), diallyl trisulfide, diallyl disulfide, diallyl sulfide, allyl mercaptan (Rauf et al., 2022), tannins, phellandrene, β-phellandrene, S-allyl-mercapto cysteine, and β-phellandrene (El-Saadony et al., 2024). 

Sustainability

None known.

Harvesting Guidelines

Garlic is generally a cultivated herb. Whereas some of its Allium relatives are native across the northern hemisphere, Allium sativum is generally found in gardens in USDA zones 8-12. It is also easily found in grocery stores and farmer’s markets. Growing garlic in the garden is valued because of its ability to repel rabbits, moles, and many other pests that threaten the growth of brassicas and nightshades. Planting generally is done in the fall 1-2 weeks after the first killing frost, or about 4-6 weeks before the first hard freeze. The individual cloves are planted, pointed tip up, 1 ½-2 inches below the surface of the soil and are left over winter to freeze, which is important for garlic’s ability to grow in the spring. Plant each clove 4-6 inches apart. Add ½ inch of mulch and top the garden bed with straw or leaves over winter to prevent any frost damage to the cloves. In the spring you will see green shoots popping up. At this point, the garlic should receive about an inch of moisture a week. If it is a dry spring, just augment with water from the hose. By June, you will see the shoots of the garlic flower come up from the center of the leaves. These flower-bearing stems are called the scapes. In order to get a nice large bulb, harvest the scapes before they flower and use them in a delicious garlic pesto, sauté, steam, or other dish. Scapes have a more mild garlic flavor that does well in a variety of recipes where garlic or onion are typically used. In July, when the leaves start yellowing, stop watering the garlic and allow the cloves to firm up. When ⅔  of the leaves are yellow or brown, gently dig up the cloves, being careful not to break off the stalk and getting as much of the root as you can. A garden fork is best for this particular herb excavation. Remove the garlic from the sun and place in a well-ventilated area to cure. Hardneck garlic can be cured in a single layer on a screen, while softneck garlic stalks can be braided and hung to dry. After 3-4 weeks of drying the garlic is ready to enjoy.

Uses

An herb with over 7000 years of documented use, traces of garlic have been found in Paleolithic caves (Masé, 2013). Garlic is used throughout the entire northern hemisphere as a panacea, and engenders varied responses—those who come into contact with it are often either in love with it or are repulsed by it. In herbal lore, garlic has been said to repel vampires, keep evil spells at bay, and repulse bad energy (Masé, 2013). A person who was thought to have been prey to vampires was often cold and pale, indicating poor circulation and a stagnant nature. In practice, garlic indeed has quite the affinity for the cardiovascular system, encouraging and stimulating blood flow throughout the body, bringing a rosy pink tint to pallid skin and generally invigorating the person. Interestingly, garlic flower essence–an energetic preparation of the garlic flower infused in water–offers similar protection on an energetic level. According to Flower Essence Services (FES), garlic flower essence gives one “resilient and vital response to life, active resistance to adverse influences” and is used in cases where people are “fearful or weak due to parasitic influence, prone to low vitality; mediumistic” (Kaminski & Katz, 1994).

The Greeks and Egyptians alike used garlic to strengthen their workers, and garlic has also been found in tombs of both ancient kings and pharaohs. More recently, thousands of studies have been done on garlic and its primary constituent of scientific interest, allicin, and its effects on the cardiovascular system, infections, the respiratory system, cancer, blood sugar, and more. It is one of the most well-researched herbs today. 

Both cultivated and wild garlic and their many Allium relations are used in Indigenous cultures in the Americas. In Cherokee culture, cultivated Allium sativum is used as a carminative, an expectorant, a stimulant, and sometimes a mild cathartic (Hamel & Chiltoskey, 1975). Garlic is heralded among the Cherokee people for its utility as an aid for edema, scurvy, asthma, and various lung ailments. An obscure use mentioned by Hamel and Chiltoskey (1975) is by the Cherokee people “to remove deafness” (p. 35). Cherokee ethnobotanist and author J.T. Garrett (2003) notes that garlic is combined with Indian root or spikenard (Aralia racemosa) and used as a blood purifier, though its primary uses are to lower cholesterol, ward off infections, and support diabetes. Tracing garlic’s Doctrine of Signatures, Garrett (2003) recounts a Cherokee elder’s explanation of how garlic or “ground bulb” tastes like wet earth and combats microbes, yeast, and fungi that come from the ground. Garrett concludes, “I suspect that every culture of people on Mother Earth has valued garlic for a special medicinal purpose and for its abilities as an antibiotic” (p. 73).

Long-revered garlic, known for its pungent, though delicious aroma, has widely ranging effects on the cardiovascular, digestive, and respiratory systems as well as on the liver. Garlic is infamous for its intense fragrance, which may cling to one’s breath or bodily odor, even the following day after having eaten a garlic-rich meal the night before. Similarly, if garlic is diced and placed  on the bottom of the feet and covered up by socks overnight, the wearer’s breath in the morning will smell of garlic. This transference is a good indication that garlic has a systemic effect on the body, permeating the whole body with its powerful phytochemicals.

Interestingly, the full benefits of garlic can’t be taken in simply by swallowing a clove or two. When garlic is damaged by being cut or chewed on by insects or mammals, the plant interprets this action as if it’s encountered a predator. The sulfur-rich compounds in the cloves are activated by oxygen and an enzyme that converts the amino acid alliin to allicin and other isothiocyanates; these protective compounds hold the true beneficial power of garlic (Masé, 2013), including its hepatoprotective and renoprotective properties (Batiha et al., 2020; Kim et al., 2020). To harness those protective compounds for their benefit, chop or crush garlic and let it sit and oxidize for 3-5 minutes before it is taken internally or added to a meal. Heat deactivates this enzymatic process, so allowing the raw clove to sit for a few minutes before cooking allows it to potentiate.

The aromatic compounds in garlic work in the body by dilating blood vessels and opening and relaxing circulation. This action explains the many studies supporting the use of garlic in reducing blood pressure (Reinhart, 2008), which corresponds to the idea that garlic reduces tension in the cardiovascular system and allows blood to be pumped through the body more effectively. Studies show improvements when the equivalent of one clove of garlic is used daily for at least 4-6 weeks, which indicates that garlic as a part of the daily diet can support healthy blood pressure (Silagy et al., 1994). Importantly, in studies done on people who have average blood pressure, their measurements did not decrease, indicating that garlic would not be contraindicated with someone who has normal or low (hypotensive) blood pressure (Reinhart, 2008; Romm, 2017). Yale-trained MD and midwife Aviva Romm (2014) indicates garlic as a natural hypotensive to use during pregnancy. Of course, this usage should be discussed with a botanically knowledgeable practitioner before changing routines while pregnant.

Many studies have also been done on the ability of garlic to lower serum cholesterol levels. Interestingly enough, both human and murine studies have shown that daily garlic consumption (equivalent to 1-2 cloves of garlic a day) not only lowers serum cholesterol and triglyceride levels (Stevinson, 2000), but also increases the levels of high-density lipoproteins (HDL) also known as the “good cholesterol” (Hoffmann, 2003). This indicates the ability of garlic to improve the function of the liver, which is key in moderating healthy cholesterol and triglyceride levels in the body (Horton et al., 2002). Garlic improves antioxidant status and is a natural ACE (angiotensin-converting enzyme) inhibitor (Romm, 2017). 

Garlic, with its blood sugar regulating capabilities and lack of side effects, has shown to be very beneficial in addressing diabetes and its complexities (Jiang et al., 2024). It is its profound anti-inflammatory and antixodant qualities which decrease the buildup of harmful lipids and reduce platelet aggregation, benefitting the blood vessels and aiding in the management of various vascular system concerns, including high cholesterol, diabetes, and atherosclerosis (Lu et al., 2024).

Arteriosclerosis is the hardening of arteries due to age and other lifestyle choices like smoking and alcohol use. The arteries harden due to an increase of cholesterol plaque and natural calcification of the blood vessels over time. This leads to a lack of elasticity in the blood vessels, causing high blood pressure, and raising risk factors of thrombosis (blood clots), impotence, heart attack, and stroke. Studies have been done with garlic alone (Orekhov & Grunwald, 1997) and with statins (Budoff, 2006); both have shown that garlic has a positive effect on halting, and sometimes even reversing, arteriosclerosis.

Garlic has been shown in large doses to have anticoagulant effects in the blood (Bordia et al., 1998). The blood thinning effects can be reversed somewhat in an emergency (Tilgner, 2009). The human studies done on garlic shows that it operates as a blood thinner by inhibiting platelet aggregation, fibrinolytic actions, and reduced fibrinogen activity (Romm, 2017). The constituent mostly destroyed with heat, allicin, is associated with these anticoagulant activities. It is likely that all of garlic’s blood thinning compounds are primarily contained in raw garlic; cooked garlic, however, is not responsible for blood thinning action, since its consumption has not been correlated with increased bleeding (Romm, 2017).

Garlic is also an excellent herb for the first stages of a cold or flu. Garlic has 18 known antiviral and antibacterial substances (Gladstar, 1993) and notable antimicrobial activities throughout the body on viruses, bacteria, protozoa, worms, yeast, and fungi. Fire cider is a traditional tonic which has been reintroduced to modern herbalists by Rosemary Gladstar (2001) that includes many antimicrobial herbs, including garlic, and is used as a general immune stimulant and antimicrobial. It has been shown to be effective against gram-positive and gram-negative aerobic (not anaerobic) bacteria and against antibiotic resistant bacteria (Sivam, 2001).

The volatile oils in garlic are excreted through the lungs, and the antimicrobial nature of these volatile oils thus makes it particularly helpful in the case of respiratory infections like bronchitis, catarrh, colds, flu, and whooping cough (Hoffmann, 2003). It is an expectorant, helping the body to thin and efficiently remove phlegm that is clogging airways. Garlic has even been shown to work in conjunction with streptomycin against bacterial tuberculosis (Tilgner, 2009).

In the digestive system, garlic sparks the digestive fire and brings heat and vitality to the digestive process. This can be aggravating to people with a warm constitution. It has been shown to be effective against the bacterium H. pylori in conjunction with antibiotics, improving the protocol by over 30% over antibiotic use alone (Tilgner, 2009). H. pylori has been shown to cause stomach ulcers and is implicated in certain types of stomach cancers, indicating that garlic might be effective in protocols for certain digestive cancers (Hoffmann, 2003) in addition to its efficacy against other forms of cancer (Batiha et al., 2020; Rauf et al., 2022; Verma et al., 2023). Garlic is also traditionally used to kill off parasites in the intestines including pinworms, roundworms, hookworms, and tapeworms. Garlic enemas are used for children with severe pinworms (Tilgner, 2009).

Allicin has also been shown to be effective against amoebas like Entameoba histolytica and giardiasis. According to German MD Albert Schweitzer, the dose for supporting amoebic dysentery was equivalent to 3 cloves/day (10 g) (Tilgner, 2009).

Preliminary murine research suggests that an extract of garlic proteins (lectin) in vitro possesses significant immunomodulatory effects and may have the ability to reprogram immune responses, accelerate the production of immune cells, and demonstrate cytotoxic effects against lung cancer cells (Kiran et al., 2024). In murine trials and testing on human lung fibroblasts, garlic has been found to have antifibrotic activity; the use of garlic nanovesicles has been found to lower the mRNA markers of genetic markers determining inflammation, fibrosis, and extracellular matrix deposition in cases of bleomycin-induced idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (Santos-Álvarez et al., 2024).

Garlic, in particular its organosulfur constituents, has demonstrated a protective mechanism of action in Parkinson’s disease via its antioxidant qualities, benefits to mitochondria, and modulation of the neuroinflammatory response (Rakshit et al., 2023

Garlic can also be helpful in both the male and female reproductive systems. Rosemary Gladstar (1993) and Aviva Romm (2014) both suggest that whole, raw garlic clove suppositories inserted into the vagina are useful for yeast infections and vulvovaginitis. Garlic has shown efficacy in vitro against E. coli, Mycobacterium, Proteus, and Candida species (Romm, 2017). Matthew Wood (2008) indicates garlic is helpful for menstrual cramps, weakness during menstruation, amenorrhea, morning sickness, and hot flashes during menopause. Dr. Romm (2017) notes that garlic is a useful herb when addressing maternal breast candidiasis (thrush), as an alternative to harsher medications. Wood (2008) also notes that garlic may be helpful for male impotence, which is understandable because one of the leading causes of impotence is atherosclerosis.

Those who take garlic on a regular basis may notice lower and more stable blood sugar levels. More studies are still necessary to confirm this observation, but as we discussed above with lipid metabolism, if the liver is working more efficiently as a result of an herbal intervention, then blood sugar levels could also be positively impacted.

Used topically juiced or in an oil, garlic can help aid aches and pains due to sprains or overuse of muscles. Garlic oil is also traditionally used to resolve aches, pains, and infection in the ear (Tierra, 1998). While some suggest garlic oil be dropped inside the ear, others find that it works equally as effectively when rubbed alongside the eustachian tubes on the outside of the neck. Garlic oil or juice used topically on the foot is an effective aid for athlete’s foot, especially when combined with tea tree (Melaleuca alternifolia) essential oil.

According to extensive research, extract preparations of aged garlic are high in sulfur and saponins, offering notable immunomodulatory, anti-inflammatory, and antioxidant benefits (Verma et al., 2023). Another potent alternative form of garlic is black garlic, prepared by exposing raw garlic to high temperatures and high humidity and both aging and fermenting it; black garlic has been found to share many of the same anti-inflammatory, protective, antihypolipidemic, antiobesity, and immunomodulatory qualities as its raw form (Ahmed & Wang, 2021). All black garlic is aged, while not all aged garlic is black (fermented). 

Energetically, garlic is warming and tends to be drying when using the dried herb, but moistening when using fresh. 

As you can see, this simple kitchen herb lends itself to a whole host of uses—but don’t forget to love garlic as a daily addition to your diet! Garlic, in fact, is considered to be one of the foods most critical to a health-promoting diet (El-Saadony et al., 2024). Roast a head of garlic and use it as a delicious spread, chop garlic and add it to honey for a quick and effective cough and cold support, enjoy it in soups, stews, hummus, guacamole, vegetable roasts, and a myriad of other dishes. This is one food herb that can be added to just about anything for an extra kick of garlicky goodness.

Adult Dose

Dietary use is the most common way to incorporate garlic into the diet. Chopped fresh garlic incorporated into hummus, guacamole, soups, stews, and any other delicious dishes is best for daily use. 

If a person prefers to avoid the taste or smell of garlic in food they can use the following dosage instructions: 1 clove/day prophylactically, 3 cloves/day during acute infection, or 1 capsule of dried powdered garlic/day, or 3 capsules/day during active infection. 

Enteric-coated capsules: capsules with a dosage of standardized garlic powder (6 mg allicin yield) may be taken in a dosage of 600-900 mg/day (Hoffmann, 2003).

Liquid extract of aged garlic: 2400 mg/day (Sativi, 1999).

Safety

Because garlic is such a warming food, it aggravates pitta dosha and is generally irritating to people with a warm constitution. In high doses, it may upset the digestive system, causing gas, nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, and burning of the mouth. In very large doses it can kill off the healthy intestinal flora, so very large doses may need to be followed by a probiotic protocol. Interestingly, in normal and moderate doses garlic acts as a prebiotic, food for the good microflora in the gut. Contact dermatitis from prolonged topical exposure can be an issue (Romm, 2017). People with a known allergy to Allium plants should avoid garlic. The German Commission E reports no known contraindications of garlic. (American Botanical Council, 2000). Garlic should never be given to dogs or cats because of its toxicity to these animals.

Garlic has been shown in large (not dietary) doses to have anticoagulant effects in the blood (Bordia et al., 1998). Therefore, it should be avoided in high doses by those on anti-clotting medications or blood thinning drugs like aspirin and 2 weeks before and after surgical procedures. The blood thinning effects can be reversed somewhat in an emergency (Tilgner, 2009). 

Although some will caution the nursing mother/parent against use of garlic, the studies that have been done on garlic’s impact on lactation have yielded no adverse effects and, likewise, there are no documented negative effects in the many places where beneficial quantities of garlic are consumed daily; in fact, it is used as a galactagogue in India (Romm, 2017) and is listed as such in The Nursing Mother’s Herbal (2003). Usage during pregnancy should be discussed with a botanically knowledgeable practitioner.
