Raspberry Leaf

Rubus idaeus, Rubus spp.

Native To

Rubus idaeus is native to temperate regions of Europe and Asia (Royal Botanic Gardens of Kew [RBGK], n.d.a). Black raspberry (R. occidentalis) is native to the eastern and central United States and Canada (RBGK, n.d.c). Another species of black raspberry (R. leucodermis), sometimes called blackcap raspberry, is native to western North America, ranging from Alaska to Mexico (RBGK, n.d.b).

Geographic Distribution

In addition to its native range throughout temperate Eurasia, Rubus idaeus can be found in parts of the United States, Canada, Australia, New Zealand, and Iceland (RBGK, n.d.a). The North American native Rubus occidentalis has been reported in western Russia and Czechoslovakia (RBGK, n.d.c).

Botanical Description

A deciduous perennial shrub with biennial canes, Rubus idaeus grows to about 0.5-3 m in height and tends to form thickets (Tirmenstein, 1990). In wild plants, first-year canes often produce only leaves; in the second year they produce lateral branches (floricanes) from which grow leaves and flowers (Tirmenstein, 1990). The smooth, round stems are woody with bristles and/or curved prickles (Foster & Duke, 2000). The leaves are green on top and silvery grey/white beneath (Tirmenstein, 1990). They are pinnately compound with three to five leaflets; each coarsely toothed leaflet has a roughly oval shape with a rounded base and pointed tip (Brill & Dean, 1994). Five-petaled white flowers appear in spring and form red fruits in the summer. Commonly referred to as a berry, the fruit is a collection of drupelets around a cone-shaped core or receptacle; when the berry is picked, the fruit detaches from the receptacle, leaving a hollow center in the berry (Sycamore, 2021). This is a way to distinguish species of black raspberry (R. occidentalis, R. leucodermis) from blackberry (Rubus fruticosus); although the dark color of the fruits is similar, black raspberries are hollow, while the receptacle remains within blackberries (Sycamore, 2021). Rubus species tend to readily hybridize, often making it difficult to discern species, subspecies, and hybrids (Sycamore, 2021).

Key Constituents

Tannins (gallotannins, ellagic acid), flavonoids (kaempferol, kaempferol glycosides, quercetin, quercetin glycosides, hyperoside), phenolic acids (caffeic acid, chlorogenic acid), terpenoids (terpinolene, 3-oxo-α-ionol, a-amyrin, β-amyrin, squalene, cycloartenol), alkaloids (fragarine), volatile oils, vitamins (C, B, E), and minerals (calcium, iron, phosphorus, potassium, magnesium, manganese, zinc) (Easley & Horne, 2016; European Medicines Agency, 2013; Ferlemi & Lamari, 2016; Gladstar, 1993).

Sustainability

None known.

Harvesting Guidelines

Harvest a few top leaves from each stem (avoid stripping the plant of all leaves) before the blossoms open. You may wish to wear gloves and use clippers to avoid contact with the bristles. There are reports that wilted raspberry leaves may temporarily become toxic due to chemical changes in the plant during the drying process; as result, some sources advise foragers to only use raspberry leaves either fresh or thoroughly dried (Brill & Dean, 1994). This caution is likely due to the presence of cyanogenic glycosides, which are found in the seeds and wilting leaves of several members of the rose family including raspberry (Patel & Patel, 2016) and are converted to cyanide when chewed or digested (Bolarinwa et al., 2016). Symptoms of cyanide toxicity include gastrointestinal distress, dizziness, and headache (Bolarinwa et al., 2016). However, cyanogenic glycosides are also found in a number of common foods such as almonds and flax seeds (Bolarinwa et al., 2016), and they are rarely ingested by humans at amounts high enough to be toxic (Williams, 2018). It is unclear whether anyone has actually experienced ill effects from the use of wilted raspberry leaves, or if this is a theoretical concern. In any case, drying significantly reduces cyanogenic glycoside levels (Bolarinwa et al., 2016).

Uses

Raspberry leaf can be gathered from both cultivated and wild species (Tobyn et al., 2016), including red raspberry (Rubus idaeus), which is most commonly found in commerce and in modern herbal literature, as well as black raspberry species such as R. occidentalis and R. leucodermis, which are native to North America. Some herbal texts list R. strigosus as a commonly used wild species, but this is now considered a subspecies of R. idaeus, along with several other subspecies and varieties (RBGK, n.d.a).

According to 1st-century Greek physician Dioscorides (70/2000), raspberry’s abundant growth around Mount Ida inspired its species name, idaeus. The name also has connections with Greek mythology; a nymph called Ida was the nurse of the infant Zeus. According to the myth, raspberries were white until Ida pricked her finger on the thorns while gathering berries for Zeus; her blood gave the fruits their red color (Johnson et al., 2012). By the 4th century CE, Rubus idaeus was being cultivated and distributed throughout Europe by the Romans, and was brought to North America in the 1700s by the English (Johnson et al., 2012).

Scholars have debated whether Dioscorides and other authors of antiquity were truly referring to raspberry (R. idaeus) or an unknown variety of blackberry (Rubus fruticosus) (Tobyn et al., 2016). The two plants have long been recognized as having similar herbal properties and actions. While some authors maintain that there is little evidence of raspberry’s herbal use until the Renaissance period (Tobyn et al., 2016), others believe that raspberry has been used as an herb since ancient times and was likely cultivated by the Greeks and Romans for both its edible fruits and herbal properties (Johnson et al., 2012).

Dioscorides (70/2000) goes into some detail about blackberry, citing the use of its leaves as an astringent for diarrhea and excessive menstrual flow and to strengthen the gums. Of raspberry, he simply writes that the uses are the same as those of blackberry, only adding a couple of indications for eye inflammation and skin infections (Dioscorides, 70/2000). Modern herbalists still recognize many of the same indications for raspberry leaf; it is often used as an astringent and is considered to have an affinity for the uterus and intestines (Wood, 2009). The herb contains tannins, which give raspberry leaf tea or infusion an astringent taste similar to green or black tea and an astringent (toning) action on tissues of the body (Gladstar, 1993). Various parts of the raspberry plant such as the root, stem, leaf, and flower have been used in herbalism throughout history; today, most Western herbalists favor raspberry leaf, which is the part of the plant most commonly found in commerce.

Herbalists use raspberry leaf to bring balance to the entire digestive system; one of its most common and longstanding uses is for diarrhea. The Cherokee use the astringent leaves of both a wild species of blackberry (Rubus serratus, syn. R. villosus) and raspberry for this purpose; Cherokee author J.T. Garrett (2003) mentions this combination as a popular tea for children’s diarrhea. As a gentle herb, it is often one of the first to be called upon in cases of diarrhea in infants (Holmes, 1989). Raspberry leaf’s antispasmodic action in the gastrointestinal tract eases spasms and discomfort associated with diarrhea and colic; meanwhile, its astringent action not only benefits loose stool, but also helps promote healthy peristalsis in cases of mild constipation (Holmes, 1989). Herbalists Easley & Horne (2016) also note the herb’s antacid and antiemetic actions against nausea. Herbalist Matthew Wood (2009) indicates raspberry leaf for stomach flu, but mentions that it is a milder astringent than blackberry and is therefore less potent for intestinal issues (Wood, 2009).

Presently, there are no clinical studies on the efficacy of raspberry leaf for diarrhea. The herb has been found to have mild bacteriostatic activity against Vibrio cholerae, the bacterium that causes cholera; raspberry leaf also modulated the effects of cholera toxin in vitro by interfering with the intracellular toxin internationalization process, leading researchers to suggest that the herb may protect against rapid dehydration caused by cholera-induced diarrhea (Komiazyk et al., 2019). Interestingly, consuming raspberry leaf may also have a beneficial effect on the gut microbiome. A recent study by Yang et al. (2021) found that raspberry leaf extract led to an increased level of beneficial bacteria and a lower level of pathogenic bacteria in an in vitro digestion simulation.

Raspberry leaf’s astringency and anti-inflammatory properties are also used for oral issues, such as mouth ulcers, bleeding gums, sore throat, and inflammation; it can be used as a gargle for these purposes (Hoffmann, 2003). Matthew Wood (2009) makes use of the herb’s astringent properties in cases of excess mucus, stating, “It is my remedy of choice for allergies or influenza with copious free discharge of clear mucus from the upper respiratory tract” (p. 307). Harkening back to Dioscorides’ (70/2000) indication for eye inflammation, Wood (2009) also mentions raspberry leaf’s use as a mild astringent tonic for the eyes. The Cherokee peoples have also employed a wild species of raspberry, R. odorata, for eye soreness and inflammation (Garrett, 2003). The Cree name for Arctic raspberry (R. arcticus) is oskiskomin, meaning “eye berry;” a cooled decoction of the plant’s root has traditionally been used as eye drops for snow blindness (Young et al., 2015).

Raspberry also has a long history of use for balancing menstruation. The Cherokee have traditionally used the leaf as an antispasmodic for menstrual cramps and the root to address menstrual irregularities (Garrett, 2003). Raspberry leaf contains an alkaloid called fragarine, which in combination with the herb’s other beneficial constituents, gives tone to the uterus and other muscles of the pelvic region (Gladstar, 1993). According to herbalist Rosemary Gladstar (1993), “Raspberry leaf serves as both a uterine relaxant and stimulant, thus causing a regulating action in the uterus” (p. 254). The womb-strengthening abilities of fragarine help the uterus to contract more smoothly, reducing cramping during menstruation (Bennett, 2014). The astringency of raspberry is also used to lessen heavy menstrual bleeding (Gladstar, 2008); the Cree peoples have traditionally made a tea from raspberry root and stem for this purpose (Young et al., 2015).

While raspberry leaf receives the most attention for its tonic effect on the womb, it is considered a general reproductive, genitourinary, and endocrine tonic for people of all genders (Gladstar, 2017). The herb is thought to strengthen the endocrine system and balance hormones; it is used to establish rhythm and regulate menstrual cycles in young people as well as restore irregular menstrual cycles (Berger, 1998). It is sometimes used as a tonic for the urinary tract and is indicated for issues such as incontinence, irritable bladder, and pelvic floor dysfunction; it is also used to support prostate health (Young, 2019). J.T. Garrett (2003) mentions its current use among the Cherokee as a diuretic for kidney stones, urinary issues, and fluid retention. Some herbalists also use raspberry leaf to promote fertility in all genders (Weed, 1996).

Raspberry leaf is also widely used during pregnancy to strengthen the uterus and prepare the body for labor (Romm, 2018), a use which was originally introduced by Indigenous peoples of North America (Wood, 2009). The Cherokee have traditionally used raspberry root as a uterine tonic to reduce the likelihood of miscarriage and ease labor pains (Garrett, 2003), and the Cree have used decoctions of the leaf and root for various issues related to childbirth (Young et al., 2015).

Nineteenth-century American herbalist Samuel Thomson was the first to publish a written account of raspberry leaf’s use during pregnancy or labor (Tobyn et al., 2016). Thomson (1835) stated, “It is the best thing for women in travail [labor], of any article I know” (p. 58). Dr. Albert Isaiah Coffin (1853), a colleague of Thomson, used raspberry leaf during pregnancy to ease morning sickness and premature labor pains and during childbirth to promote the progress of labor. Herbalist and historian Maude Grieve (1931/1971) suggested that a warm raspberry leaf tea can be drunk freely during labor.

Today, raspberry leaf is one of the most commonly used herbs during pregnancy (Tobyn et al., 2016). It is considered an excellent tonic during pregnancy on two counts: its high vitamin and mineral content is richly nutritive for both parent and child, and its tonic properties help to strengthen the uterus in preparation for childbirth (Gladstar, 1993). It is also used to quell morning sickness and reduce the risk of miscarriage (Wood, 2009). However, there is some difference of opinion about raspberry leaf’s safety during the first trimester. Some herbalists maintain that it can be safely used throughout the entire 9 months of pregnancy (Gladstar, 1993), while others suggest its use primarily during the second and third trimesters (Romm, n.d.), and still others caution against its use during the first trimester due to its uterine-stimulant properties (Garrett, 2003; Mills & Bone, 2005). Please consult a midwife, doctor, or an experienced herbalist if you have questions about using raspberry leaf during pregnancy, and discontinue use in the case of early labor pains (Tobyn et al., 2016).

Raspberry leaf is used as a parturient to facilitate an easier labor (Hoffmann, 2003) and reduce the risk of excessive bleeding after birth (Romm, 2018). Its tonic effect on the womb is thought to bolster against postpartum hemorrhage caused by a relaxed or atonic uterus (Weed, n.d.). One survey of 500 nurse-midwives in the United States found that 63% of those who replied used red raspberry leaf to stimulate labor (McFarlin et al., 1999). While herbalist and midwife Aviva Romm, M.D. (2018) states that there is little evidence of raspberry leaf’s effectiveness in stimulating labor, there is research that supports its ability to improve labor outcomes and reduce the need for medical intervention.

One retrospective observational study by Parsons et al. (1999) compared those who consumed raspberry leaf in various forms (tea, tablet, or tincture) during pregnancy with those who did not. Findings suggested that consumption of the herb was associated with a decreased likelihood of pre- and post-term labor, as well as a decreased need for forceps or vacuum birth, artificial rupture of membranes, or caesarean delivery (Parsons et al., 1999). A clinical study by Simpson et al. (2001) compared labor outcomes among those who consumed 1.2 g of raspberry leaf extract in tablets twice a day beginning at 32 weeks of gestation with a placebo control group. The researchers found that consuming raspberry leaf did not shorten the duration of the first stage of labor, but the second stage of labor was over 9 minutes shorter, on average, in those who consumed raspberry leaf extract. Also, the raspberry leaf group was less likely to require a forceps delivery; the rate of this procedure was 19.3% among the raspberry leaf group compared to 30.4% of the control group. There were no adverse effects noted among those who took raspberry leaf or their babies (Simpson et al., 2001).

Following birth, a raspberry leaf infusion is used as a uterine tonic to help expel the placenta (Gladstar, 1993), restore the elasticity of the womb and uterine ligaments, and as a galactagogue to increase the flow of breastmilk (Berger, 1998). Although its galactagogue action has yet to be confirmed by clinical studies (Drugs and Lactation Database, 2021), herbalists commonly use raspberry leaf during lactation to ease breast discomfort and to enrich the milk with its nutritive properties (Wood, 2009). This use also has roots in Indigenous herbal practices; for example, the Cherokee have traditionally made use of raspberry root as a galactagogue (Garrett, 2003). However, herbalist Susun Weed (n.d.) notes that for some individuals, raspberry’s astringency may negatively impact the production of breastmilk.

Raspberry leaf is a rich source of vitamins A, B, C, and E and minerals such as calcium, iron, phosphorus, potassium, magnesium, selenium, and manganese (Gladstar, 1993; Pederson, 2010). Rosemary Gladstar (2008) suggests using raspberry leaf as a nutritive tonic for low energy levels and during recovery from illness. When used as a nutritive tonic, raspberry leaf is often prepared as an infusion (Gladstar, 2008). A longer steeping time of 4-8 hours leads to a higher concentration of minerals; according to Susun Weed (2015), a cup of raspberry leaf infusion contains about 200-250 mg of calcium, compared to 5 mg in a cup of raspberry leaf tea.

Raspberry leaf contains calcium in a form that is easily assimilated by the body; assimilation is further enhanced by the presence of phosphorus and vitamins A and C (Weed, n.d.). Calcium is vital for a healthy pregnancy (Weed, n.d.) and provides important nourishment for the musculoskeletal system, building and maintaining teeth and bones, as well as for the nervous, immune, and cardiovascular systems (University of Michigan, 2018). Raspberry leaf is particularly high in manganese, containing twice the amount of most herbs (Pederson, 2010); manganese contributes to antioxidant actions in the body and supports bone health, wound healing, blood sugar regulation, and metabolism of nutrients (Goodson, 2018). The herb is also a rich source of iron and is sometimes used to replenish iron-poor blood (Gladstar, 2008); it is often combined with nettle (Urtica dioica) leaf for this purpose (Gladstar, 2008). However, it’s important to note that raspberry leaf’s tannin content may interfere with the absorption of iron and other nutrients to some extent (Mills & Bone, 2005).

Raspberry leaf also has antioxidant properties, which are attributed to the herb’s phenolic content. Researchers suggest that raspberry leaf’s high antioxidant activity, combined with its rich nutrient content, has the potential to guard against a variety of degenerative diseases such as diabetes, cancer, and atherosclerosis (Staszowska-Karkut & Materska, 2020). An in vitro study found an extract of the leaves to have antioxidant effects against induced oxidative stress in human liver cells, leading the authors to suggest its possible use for hepatic issues related to oxidative stress; however, clinical research is needed to confirm this use (Costea et al., 2016). Researchers have also studied the effects of raspberry leaf extract against cancer cell lines in vitro and found cytotoxic activity against human leukemia (Skupień et al., 2006), laryngeal carcinoma, and colon adenocarcinoma cells (Durgo et al., 2012), although further studies are needed to confirm these effects in the human body.

Adult Dose

Tea: 8 fl oz (1-3 teaspoons dried herb in 1 cup water) 3x/day (Hoffmann, 2003).

Infusion: 4-8 fl oz (1 oz dried herb in 1 quart water) 3x/day (Easley & Horne, 2016).

Tincture: 2-4 mL (1:5, 40%) 3x/day (Hoffmann, 2003).

Safety

According to authors Mills & Bone (2005), raspberry leaf is contraindicated in anemia as it may interfere with the absorption of iron due to its high tannin content. Take raspberry leaf separately from metal ion supplements, thiamine medications, or alkaloid-containing medications or herbs. High doses of tannins may cause gastrointestinal irritation; use caution in inflamed or ulcerated conditions. While raspberry leaf is commonly used during pregnancy and is generally considered safe, some herbalists advise that its use is best confined to the second and third trimesters due to the plant’s reputation as a uterine stimulant. While this effect is considered doubtful except near the end of term (Mills & Bone, 2005), it is suggested to consult a healthcare professional if using raspberry leaf during pregnancy and discontinue use in the case of early labor pains (Tobyn et al., 2016).
