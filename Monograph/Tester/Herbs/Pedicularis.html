Native To

Pedicularis canadensis is native to eastern and central North America, stretching from Manitoba to Quebec in the north and extending south from Maine to Florida and westward to Mississippi, Louisiana, Texas, and as far as southwest Mexico (Royal Botanic Gardens Kew [RBGK], n.d.).

Geographic Distribution

Found in most of the eastern United States and Canada, Pedicularis canadensis grows from Manitoba to Quebec and New Brunswick, extending south through the Midwest, Great Lakes, New England, Mid-Atlantic, and southern regions from Texas to Florida to New Mexico and Mexico. It prefers to grow in the shady understory of forests where sunlight gets filtered through the canopy.

Botanical Description

P. canadensis is a perennial plant that emerges gradually and without announcing itself, its foliage appearing as a low-growing, pubescent, fern-like plant that can sometimes be seen sporting a deep red/carmine color with a slightly purplish hue (7Song, 2019), when it first emerges or basks in full sun. As a hemiparasitic plant, or water parasite (Williams & Boehm, 2017), P. canadensis has a taproot as well as short rhizomatic roots that spread to form dense colonies (Gleason & Cronquist, 1991). 

As one of pedicularis’s many distinctive features, its foliage consists of individual petiolate lanceolate to oblong simple leaves that can grow to nearly 8 inches (20 cm), although they are typically shorter (Ulaszek, n.d.). “The leaf blades are pinnately dissected, usually into lobes, but small leaves are just coarsely toothed” (Ulaszek, n.d., para. 1) with vertically undulating margins (Illinois Wildflowers, n.d.). These leaves form a dense basal rosette that produces a flowering stalk–sometimes several (Iowa Prairie Plants, n.d.). This hairy stalk can be green or tinged reddish-brown, features a few smaller alternating leaves along the stem, and grows 12 to 36 inches (30-90 cm) tall (TWC Staff, 2023).

At the apex of the stem is a solitary terminal indeterminate spike inflorescence gradually flowering from the base to the tip in March and April in its southernmost range and in May and June in its northernmost range (Ulaszek, n.d.). The hairy flowering spike can be up to 3.5 inches (9 cm) long, with each individual flower 0.5 to 1 inch (1 to 2.5 cm) in length (Minnesota Wildflowers: A Field Guide to the Flora of Minnesota, n.d.). 

The two to four petals (corolla) are fused and create a tubular shape with a longer upper lip that curves over the lower lip (galea), functioning as both a protective hood for the pollen and a guided runway for the long-tongued pollinators (e.g. bumblebees) who frequent it (Ulaszek, n.d.). While these petals are often a buttery yellow, they can also be a reddish-purple or a mixture of both (Ulaszek, n.d.), and the lower portion of the bloom can differ in color from the top, sometimes unfurling as white (Minnesota Wildflowers: A Field Guide to the Flora of Minnesota, n.d.). 

While the floral structure of P. canadensis is bilaterally symmetrical (Native Plant Trust: Go Botany, n.d.), it is complex and distinct. It stands out amongst the wide range of ecosystems it inhabits, spanning shady forest understories, rocky riparian woodlands, and sunlit meadows, as well as sandy oak savannas (Ulaszek, n.d.).

Each flower is supported by a leafy bract with lobed margins, toothed edges, and long marginal hairs. These bracts twist slightly so as to turn the flowers to one side (Minnesota Wildflowers: A Field Guide to the Flora of Minnesota, n.d.). These flowers, arranged in a spiral along the flowering stem (rachis), form stacked tiers where they arc outward and create a pinwheel-like pattern.

P. canadensis blooms are also hermaphroditic (Plants for a Future, n.d.), but fertilization still depends on pollen transfer (Ulaszek, n.d.). Upon pollination, the flowers turn into hairy, large (12 to 16 mm) fruits (Native Plant Trust: Go Botany, n.d.). The calyx persists and the inflorescence elongates into a capsule, resulting in spacing between the blooms that improves seed dispersal (Iowa Prairie Plants, n.d.). When ripe, these open into four segments (Iowa Prairie Plants, n.d.) and dehisce explosively; the empty seed heads may remain standing into mid-summer (Ulaszek, n.d.).

Key Constituents

Aerial Parts: Aucubins, cistanoside D, echinacoside, euphroside, forsythoside, geniposidic acid, iridolactone, leucosceptoside A, martynoside, mussaenoside, pediculariosides, plantarenaloside, shanzhiside methyl ester, verbascosides (Moore, 2003), and flavonoids (Frances, 2014).

Sustainability

Throughout most of its range, P. canadensis is confidently considered to be a secure, non-threatened species with no predicted extinction risk (RBGK, n.d.). See the Harvesting Guidelines section for an important discussion about wild harvesting this herb.

While P. canadensis is not federally listed in the US, it is always recommended to check local state or regional checklists for specific conservation status, as it may be rare in certain parts of its range.

It should be noted that P. canadensis should not be confused with P. furbishiae, which is currently listed as threatened under the U.S. Endangered Species Act and has been designated by the Committee on the Status of Endangered Wildlife in Canada as endangered (Roth et al., 2026). Globally, it has been indicated as being at a very high risk of extinction or elimination, ranging between critically imperiled and imperiled due to its narrow range; P. furbishiae, or Furbish’s louseworst, is only found along the ice-scoured banks of the riparian ecosystem that is the St. John river in northern Maine and adjacent New Brunswick (Roth et al., 2026).

Harvesting Guidelines

The aerial parts of Pedicularis canadensis are harvested in early summer when they bloom for about 3 weeks (Virginia Wildflowers, 2024).

While P. canadensis is not considered an endangered species or at threat of becoming endangered, it’s also not customarily cultivated so gathering this plant for herbal use is primarily done through wildcrafting (Saville, 2017). This poses a challenge when trying to generalize ethical harvesting guidelines as its popularity can differ across regions. For example, P. canadensis is more spread out around upstate New York and is thus best left alone, but amongst its southeastern, Rocky Mountain, and western ranges it’s much more common and able to be harvested in small quantities (7Song, 2019).

Since P. canadensis is less abundant in some areas, it’s important to notice the vigor of the plant colony before considering harvesting–including frequency of distribution, overall size, and general health–to ensure that several good-sized patches are left there to self-propagate (7Song, 2019). It’s recommended to reserve wildharvesting for very firmly established stands of the herb (Howell, 2025) as well as to encourage the growth of wild colonies by leaving behind plenty of untouched flowers and roots (Saville, 2017). Saville (2017) notes that “increased demand for this herb could cause concern for wild populations, especially those that are more easily accessible” (para. 12).

Renowned late herbalist and author Michael Moore (2003) summarizes harvesting of Pedicularis spp.: “Pick younger, peripheral plants in a stand, leave the parents, and support any program that tries to limit loss of wildlife habitat” (p. 51).

Uses

Pedicularis canadensis has proven difficult to neatly contain within the boundaries of botanical classification, a reality reflected in the extensive list of synonymous binomials this species has accumulated over time, including P. canadensis: forma albescens, forma bicolor, forma flava, forma praeclara, forma typa, subsp. fluviatilis, var. dobbsii, and var. gladiata (RBGK, n.d.; Wunderlin et al., 2025). 

The name P. canadensis refers to its individual species identity within its genera, combining the geographical distinction of Canada with the Latin suffix -ensis, meaning “belonging to” or “originating from.” Despite this specificity, the plant is more commonly referred to by its vernacular names, most notably lousewort–a name which is also applied to many other Pedicularis species. Lousewort is a common name based in folklore, as is, ironically, its scientific genus name. Pedicularis stems from the Latin word “pediculus” meaning “of lice,” referencing when 17th century cows developed a lice infestation after grazing in fields full of pedicularis (Sinadinos, 2025), resulting in a myth that farm animals that ate the herb attracted lice (Howell, 2025).

Another common name, wood betony, introduces an additional layer of ambiguity, as it’s also used for an entirely different plant: Stachys officinalis, also known as Betonica officinalis. Pedicularis and Stachys/Betonica officinalis differ in their family classifications as well as habitat. The moniker “wood betony” is a regionally dependent common name, referring to either Stachys spp. or Pedicularis spp. depending on context (Wood, 2009). For example, American wood betony (Pedicularis spp.), European wood betony (Stachys officinalis, Betonica officinalis), and Canadian wood betony (Pedicularis canadensis) denote different plants and genera (Swift & Midura, 2023). 

Stachys spp. and Pedicularis spp. also share slight similarities in both botanical features and beneficial actions (Wood, 2009)—most notably their use as muscle relaxants—but cannot be used interchangeably (Howell, 2025). Accurate identification is critical, as the two have different mechanisms of action: Stachys officinalis is more often associated with grounding and centering qualities, while Pedicularis spp. acts in a more generalized manner on skeletal muscle tension (Swift & Midura, 2023).

Furthermore, regional usage varies within the United States, with the moniker “wood betony” on the West Coast potentially referring to a different genus than when used on the East Coast (Swift & Midura, 2023). This overlap in common naming across unrelated plant families can lead to confusion regarding pedicularis in herbal literature (Moore, 2003); use precise binomial nomenclature to avoid misidentification.

Similar challenges in classification extend beyond naming conventions at the species level. The family Orobanchaceae contains both holoparasites and hemi-parasites and has been significantly reclassified and expanded in recent decades. Considered the largest family of parasitic plants, it contains nearly 100 genera and over 2000 species (Weakley & SE Flora Team, 2025), with the genus Pedicularis alone encompassing approximately 600 species (Saville, 2017) of which over half are endemic to China (Sinadinos, 2025). 

This reclassification not only reshaped how these plants are grouped, but also brought greater attention to a defining feature of the family–their parasitic and hemiparasitic strategies. Pedicularis is able to photosynthesize and make food, but does not rely on this alone, also taking some nourishment from the roots of other plants in close proximity, which in turn complicates efforts to fully understand its chemical composition (Natural Communities: Ecological Expertise Native Nursery, 2023; Ulaszek, n.d.). 

 Along its rhizomatic roots, P. candensis forms haustoria–small, specialized root structures initially established during the seedling stage–that attach to the roots of neighboring plants and access their xylem directly (Piehl, 1963). Pedicularis appears largely unconcerned with what it attaches to–haustorial connections have been found on living plants, dead root fragments, fungal tissue, and even small pebbles, and the number of possible hosts is likely extensive and unknown; it has been suggested “that connections may be made to essentially any vascular plant with subterranean parts in proximity to those of the parasite” (Piehl, 1963, p. 978). Current estimates suggest pedicularis may have at least 80 host species from over 35 families, with certain plants being targeted more often than others (Piehl, 1963), such as asters (Aster spp.), trees, especially oaks, and prairie grasses (Minnesota Wildflowers: A Field Guide to the Flora of Minnesota, n.d.; Ulaszek, n.d.).

In the case of Pedicularis spp., this hemiparasitic tendency makes it extremely difficult to determine the exact constituents produced by the plant itself versus what it is uptaking from its interwoven underground root neighbors. This variability complicates interpretations of its beneficial actions, as some traditional uses were influenced by compounds absorbed from host species. For example, its use as an expectorant or digestive aid has been linked to constituents such as aromatic compounds from conifers or tannins derived from oak species, respectively (Moore & Kamp, 1993). “Several of [Pedicularis spp.] can be found tapping into Thermopsis (mountain pea or false lupine), which contains anagyrine and N-methylcytisine (Caulophylline), active compounds that are mostly responsible for the effects of blue cohosh on the uterine muscles” (Moore & Kamp, 1993, p. 67). This could explain why Pedicularis spp., despite primarily acting on skeletal muscles, has also been reported as offering subtle relaxation to smooth muscle, particularly in regards to addressing menstrual cramping (Sinadinos, 2025).

Caution and careful observation should be used when identifying plants growing nearby when harvesting (Sinadinos, 2025), due to P. canadensis being a hemiparasite. “This is a big problem, for it seems difficult to figure out exactly what [constituents that Pedicularis spp.] contain and what is derived from the larger plants on which they are sometimes parasitic” (Moore & Kamp, 1993, p. 67). 

Due to these concerrns, it’s advised not to harvest P. canadensis when it’s growing nearby toxic, poisonous, or alkaloid-containing plants such as lupin (Lupinus spp.), ragwort (Senecio spp.), poison ivy, (Toxicodendron radicans), and mountain pea (Thermopsis spp.), as alkaloids, especially in high doses, can place strain on the liver and other organs (Sinadinos, 2025). Schneider and Stermitz (1990) showed that multiple pedicularis species are capable of acquiring secondary compounds from their host plants, including the hepatotoxic pyrrolizidine alkaloid senecionine from its host plant arrowleaf ragwort (Senecio triangularis), for example.

Pedicularis can be commonly found amongst oaks, known for their tannin content, or around pine and spruce trees, where the roots may gather more aromatic constituents that can stimulate urination or promote circulation (Sinadinos, 2025). Perhaps this is what accounts for pedicularis helping with erectile dysfunction (Frances, 2014). Herbalist Dara Seville (2017) notes that “finding pedicularis among aspen stands [Populus spp.] … is like harvesting two herbs in one as the aspen subtly shifts the energy and properties of the pedicularis increasing its anti-inflammatory pain-relieving nature” (para. 4). Flavonoid compounds present in Pedicularis spp. indeed contribute to its anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties, and there are some early indications of possible anticancer potential (Frances, 2014). Despite these varying constituent profiles, herbalist Matthew Wood (2009) considers the many different kinds of pedicularis found in North America to be interchangeable in use.

In herbal practice, Pedicularis spp. is most often used as a musculoskeletal relaxant, particularly for tension held within skeletal muscle (Frances, 2014)–the most common voluntary muscle type. Michael Moore (1993) claimed that it is among the most safe and effective of all muscle relaxant herbs. Its reputation for safety makes pedicularis suitable for both children and adults as well as extends into more specific applications, including its use during childbirth (Sinadinos, 2025). Christa Sinadinos (2025) describes its role in supporting muscular relaxation during labor without negative impacts or disrupting the natural rhythm of uterine contractions. She also suggests Pedicularis spp. for both internal and external use postpartum to help ease residual muscle tension and pain; refer to the Safety section of this monograph for further information on pedicularis use during pregnancy and postpartum.

In broader use, P. canadensis is indicated for individuals who have tense muscles from adrenaline, cortisol, and overstress or overuse (Moore & Kamp, 1993). This makes it useful for addressing muscle spasms, tension, and pain associated with both acute injury (Howell, 2025) as well as chronic pain–particularly in the neck and back (Howell, 2025)–and shoulder tension (7Song, 2019). P. canadensis is also indicated for sprains, strains, and tears of tendons or ligaments (Howell, 2025).

Pedicularis spp. supports those with tension headaches and trigeminal pain or tension (Sinadinos, 2025). Additionally, it is highly valued for supporting the release of muscular guarding, making it a herbal ally to use during therapeutic bodywork practices such as deep tissue massage, Hellerwork, or Rolfing (Sinadinos, 2025).

Pedicularis spp. also exhibits anti-inflammatory and antispasmodic properties, making it effective alone or in a formula for musculoskeletal spasm since inflammation often accompanies spasm (Frances, 2014). It helps aid muscle tightness and spasm that develop from overuse or strenuous, prolonged physical activity such as athletic training, hiking, and gardening. Paradoxically, it also addresses muscle tension that results from extended periods of immobility. Pedicularis has even been reported to help decrease muscle spasms in some people with multiple sclerosis (Sinadinos, 2025) as well as those with sciatica and spinal injuries.

P. canadensis supports inflammatory stomach pain and intestinal distress, especially when caused by diarrhea (Howell, 2025), and despite its antispasmodic qualities it’s noted to have minimal effect on general digestive cramping (Sinadinos, 2025), a complexity which may be related to the above discussion on variance in constituents depending on host species. It can also be used in blends to aid the upper respiratory system, specifically dry coughs, sore throats, and laryngitis (Howell, 2025), and while it has limited influence on respiratory spasms, it may help reduce the skeletal muscle tension associated with intense coughing spasms (Sinadinos, 2025).

7Song (2019) notes that Pedicularis spp. is particularly useful in formulas and he often prepares a blend of it with equal parts skullcap (Scutellaria spp.) aerial parts and black cohosh (Actaea racemosa) root, paying close attention to any potential side effects with usage of the latter.

A different relaxant formula might contain Pedicularis spp., skullcap aerial parts, mullein (Verbascum spp.) root, mugwort (Artemisia vulgaris) leaf, and/or catnip (Nepeta cataria) aerial parts (Swift & Midura, 2023). For specifically addressing tension at the base of the neck, pair pedicularis with skullcap aerial parts (Swift & Midura, 2023).

Deborah Frances, RN, ND, (2014) combines Pedicularis spp. with the following herbs, citing her clinical experience of their efficacy for musculoskeletal spasm: black cohosh (Actaea racemosa) root, guelder-rose (Viburnum opulus) bark, kava (Piper methysticum) root, valerian (Valeriana officinalis) root, skullcap (Scutellaria lateriflora) aerial parts, California poppy (Eschscholzia californica) whole plant, and/or passionflower (Passiflora incarnata) aerial parts. 7Song (2019) combines Pedicularis spp. with willow (Salix spp.) bark, and turmeric (Curcuma longa) root as an anti-inflammatory formulation.

Pedicularis spp. does not address nerve pain (Sinadinos, 2025) and is not a narcotic. Instead of masking discomfort from sore muscles or acute injuries, it works by relaxing muscle tension, which can reduce the need for stronger preparations for pain (7Song, 2019). 

For general pain, 7Song (2019) often blends stronger anodyne herbs with Pedicularis spp., such as hops (Humulus lupulus) strobile, valerian (Valeriana officinalis) root, and wild lettuce (Lactuca virosa) aerial parts. However, when formulating for cases of severe pain, 7Song (2019) cautions against combining Pedicularis spp. with anodyne herbs, so that large doses of skeletal muscle relaxant herbs can be taken without the risk of reducing cognitive function or being overly sedating. By separating out tincture formulations for relaxants and anodynes, the latter can be given at night to help with sleep and the former can help reduce pain levels during the day (7Song, 2019).

Pedicularis spp. can also produce a relaxed mental state by encouraging the body to let go of overall tension–from the temples and jaw down through the neck, shoulders, and into the lower back, thus signaling to the brain for the nervous system to relax (Swift & Midura, 2023), without sedating or impacting cognitive function (7Song, 2019).

Emotionally, Pedicularis spp. is indicated for people with constitutions that naturally carry a lot of deep tension but can’t necessarily identify where it came from or what caused it (Swift & Midura, 2023). It also serves “as a catalyst to encourage shifting in the depths of ourselves when we need to see things in a new light” (Saville, 2017, para. 5). P. canadensis has been used in herbal smoking blends for this relaxant effect and is suggested for those avoiding tobacco (Nicotiana tabacum) (Mackinnon, n.d.).

Pedicularis enjoys a varied history of use in Indigenous tradition. Hamel and Chiltoskey (1975) note that pedicularis is used in cough formulas along with other herbs as an anticatarrhal. In Mohegan tradition, an infusion of pedicularis leaves had a history of use as an abortifacient (Tantaquidgeon, 1972). In The Cherokee Herbal, a liquid extracted from pedicularis root is best known for aiding open wounds, cuts, and, when combined with Lobelia inflata aerial parts, sores (Garrett, 2003). J.T. Garrett (2003), Indigenous ethnobotanist and author, notes another Cherokee elder’s observations that pedicularis “relaxed the inners” and was thus useful for internal inflammation and swelling (p. 130) as well as bloody stool. Pedicularis roots are also decocted for use in stomachache or infused and taken for flux (bloody diarrhea) (Hamel & Chiltoskey, 1975) in addition to addressing sore throats, coughs, ulcers, and being used as an overall blood tonic (Sinadinos, 2025). An infusion of the stems and leaves is used for similar benefits, including as an expectorant and antitussive as well as for calming internal swelling (Sinadinos, 2025).

Additional insights from Cherokee herbal practice further extend Pedicularis spp.’s range of use. Cherokee herbalist Sondra Boyd highlights Pedicularis spp. for its role in addressing neurovascular conditions, including stroke and fainting, in which it eases tension within the blood vessels caused by particles or buildup that obstruct arterial flow; she further regards it as one of the few beneficial plants for use in cases of both major and minor seizure activity (Wood, 2009).

The Washoe people use a poultice of Pedicularis spp. for cuts, sores, and swellings, and also prepare the plant as a tea for use as a general tonic. The Ojibwa people associate the roots with aphrodisiac properties (Sinadinos, 2025), finely grating and incorporating them into food that was sometimes prepared with a desired love interest in mind (Foster & Duke, 2000) or using them as a means of restoring connection between separated partners (Sinadinos, 2025).

Pedicularis is occasionally eaten as a wild foraged edible. Canadian herbalist Terry Willard notes that the fresh root can be consumed either raw or cooked in a manner similar to carrots, while the stems have been prepared and eaten as a pot herb (Sinadinos, 2025). In a Master’s of Science thesis including many firsthand interviews with Cherokee tribe members, one informant recalls her mother preparing and cooking the leaves and stem of P. canadensis before feeding it to her family (Perry, 1974).

In grassland systems, pedicularis establishes relationships with tall, dominant prairie grasses (Natural Communities: Ecological Expertise Native Nursery, 2023), with its parasitic influence hindering their growth and reducing their competitive dominance. This allows less aggressive forbs and smaller bunch grasses to reclaim patches of ground (Ulaszek, n.d.). Pedicularis contributes to increased plant diversity across the habitats it occupies, playing an unexpected role in maintaining ecological balance (Natural Communities: Ecological Expertise Native Nursery, 2023), while also relying on these biodiverse systems for its own survival, as it tends to diminish when species diversity declines (Moore, 2003).

With its intricate web of ecological interactions–from promoting diverse plant species which in turn supports a wide variety of insects, pollinators, and other invertebrates that then serve as food sources for birds–P. canadensis plays a pivotal role in overall ecological balance, albeit in an anti-hero fashion (Natural Communities: Ecological Expertise Native Nursery, 2023).

Hemiparasitic P. canadensis follows in the true footsteps of the Orobanchaceae family, resisting being easily defined, evading a singular role, and blurring any line that dares to form a clear boundary around it. In doing so, it invites those who observe it to confront the limits of language used to describe the living world, subtly altering how we think of plants themselves. There is something quietly magical in this defiance, a quality captured by one student who, unable to recall its name, called it simply “that plant that sounds like a Harry Potter spell” (Saville, 2017, para. 5).

Adult Dose

Infusion: 1 tbsp dried or 2 tbsp fresh aerial parts steeped for 20 minutes in 8 fl oz of water, consume 4-8 fl oz up to 4x/day (Howell, 2025; Sinadinos, 2025).

Tincture: 20-120 drops (1-6 mL) (1:2 or 1:3, 70-95% if fresh; 1:5, 50-60% if dried) up to 4x/day (Sinadinos, 2025). ½ tsp (2.5 mL) (1:2, 50% if fresh; 1:5, 50% if dried), consumed every 30 minutes (Howell, 2025) for acute use.

Glycerite: 20-90 drops (1-4.5 mL) (1:2, 60% glycerin and 40% distilled water or 50% glycerin and 50% alcohol; fresh plant) up to 4x/day (Sinadinos, 2025).

Capsules: 2-4 “00” capsules of recently dried and ground herb, up to 3x/day (Sinadinos, 2025).

Topical: A glycerite preparation can be used externally as a liniment. For infused oil, use heat infusion for fresh herb or alcohol intermediary method for dried herb (Sinadinos, 2025).

Safety

Clinical herbalist 7Song (2019) revels in the overall safe use of Pedicularis spp. and notes that there are few downsides with administering it. Likewise, the late Michael Moore–who is credited with introducing Pedicularis spp. to Western herbalism (Gerran, 2021)–states that “by itself, [Pedicularis spp.] is one of our safest and most effective skeletal muscle relaxants” (Moore, 1993, p. 68).

With that being said, it’s important to note that pedicularis may occasionally cause feelings of vague disorientation and feeling dazed or spacey–which for those using the plant to help reduce pain may be considered an additional benefit (7Song, 2019). However, as a skeletal muscle relaxant, pedicularis isn’t sedating or negatively impacting mental function and is generally safe to dose throughout the day (7Song, 2019). Since the effects of pedicularis are fast-acting, it may be useful to experiment with a small dose to find out if these sensations of spaciness or any other unwanted side effects arise (7Song, 2019). 

Herbalist and author Christa Sinadinos (2025) states that higher doses of pedicularis could lead to nausea and vomiting, and strongly encourages one to pay close attention with dosing. Pedicularis is not to be used during pregnancy and breastfeeding (Sinadinos, 2025).
