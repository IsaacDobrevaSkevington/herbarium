Gotu kola

Centella asiatica

Native To

India, Australia, Asia, Africa, and the Pacific Islands

Geographic Distribution

Gotu kola grows wild throughout the southern hemisphere, including most of Africa, Southeast Asia, Australia, and the Pacific islands (Royal Botanic Gardens Kew [RBGK], n.d). Gotu kola is widely cultivated for commercial use in India, Sri Lanka, and in Hawaii and Texas in the US. It has been introduced to Colombia, the North island of New Zealand, and Florida and Oregon in the US (RBGK, n.d.).

Botanical Description

The herb creeps low to the ground, with small, rounded, bright green leaves no wider than 4 cm. The leaf has a slightly serrated border and joins the stem at a v-shaped slot. Gotu kola has very small, white, pink, or lavender flowers which are so small they may be easily missed underneath leaves. The plant also produces a small oval fruit. Gotu kola is a perennial herb in tropical climates and an annual in temperate ones.

Key Constituents

Leaf: Triterpene saponins (asiaticoside, madecassic acid, centellic acid, centoic acid, asiaticentoic acid, asiaticoside, madecassoside, centelloside, brahmoside, brahminoside, thankuniside, iso-thankuniside, brahmic acid, isobrahmic acid), glycosides (3-glucosilquercetin, 3-glucosylkaempferol, 7-glucosylkaempferol), flavonoids (quercetin, kaempferol), volatile oils (camphor, cineole), sesquiterpenes (beta-caryophyllene, beta-farnesene, germacrene D), fatty acids (oleic acid, linoleic acid, palmitic acid, stearic acid, lignoceric acid), and phytosterols (campesterol, sitosterol, stigmasterol); alkaloids (including hydrocotyline and vellarine),  and oligosaccharides (including centellose) (Walsh, 2014).

Sustainability

No known sustainability issues.

Harvesting Guidelines

Gotu kola can be found growing wild primarily in moist drainage areas. Cut the leaves off at soil level throughout the year in tropical climates and in summer and fall in temperate climates.

Uses

Gotu kola is among the herbs with a long documented history of use in both Ayurveda and Chinese medicine, with the earliest known mention in the Divine Husbandman’s Classic Materia Medica, which dates back to 2698 BCE (Walsh, 2014). It made its way into the Western materia medica by the 19th century (Wood, 2008). In the Himalayas and in Ayurvedic practice, it is considered to be one of the most sattvic and spiritual herbs, characterized as pure, clean, and wholesome, and for that reason has been used by yogis as a meditation aid. Gotu kola is thought to awaken and align the crown chakra, helping to balance the left and right hemispheres of the brain. Indeed, when the Doctrine of Signatures is considered, the gotu kola leaf looks very much like the cerebellum, bringing attention to the mind-strengthening, cognitive-supportive aspects of this herb. It is also one of the most important rejuvenation herbs in Ayurveda and a widely used tonic for the elderly, said to increase longevity and memory, and decrease senility and other signs of aging.

Energetically, gotu kola is cooling to neutral, and balancing to all constitutional types. Traditionally, it is said to equalize all doshas: vata, in which it calms the nervous system; pitta, for which it is rejuvenative; and kapha, in which it flushes excess (Frawley & Lad, 1986). Gotu kola is effective throughout the body’s systems, with an affinity for most tissues–particularly the blood, marrow, and nerves and the nervous, circulatory, and digestive systems  (Frawley & Lad, 1986). It is also slightly drying, and helps reduce adipose tissue that has retained toxins (Tierra, 1980); along with its effects on the nervous system, this makes it helpful for addressing drug withdrawal, particularly when used in combination with oats (Wood, 2008). Gotu kola is specifically indicated for addressing tissue states of relaxation, atrophy, and depression (Wood, 2008), which reflects its cooling, nourishing aspects.

While some sources describe gotu kola as odorless and tasteless, gotu kola is actually bitter, sweet, and slightly astringent. In Ayurveda, which includes more complex descriptions of taste, action, and post-digestive effect, it is considered bitter, cooling, and pungent (Walsh, 2014).

Gotu kola has a wide range of actions as an anti-inflammatory, nervine tonic, rejuvenative, vulnerary, alterative, diuretic, antipyretic, cardioprotective, immunostimulant, anticonvulsant, nootropic, and thyroid stimulant. It is also widely regarded as an adaptogen.  While the mechanism is not known, the herb improves vascular tone and protects against (and reduces signs of) cardiovascular disease (Lininger et al., 2000); this vascular support may be linked to gotu kola’s usefulness in increasing circulation to the brain and extremities. In Chinese medicine, where organ system actions are viewed through a different lens than in Western herbalism, gotu kola is said to work through the Liver, Spleen, and Kidney channels.

Traditional indications for gotu kola in Ayurvedic medicine include as a rejuvenating rasayana for overall vitality, enhanced cognition, and skin regeneration and to support imbalances that are reflected in the head and brain. These include nervous disorders, epilepsy, and reduced cognition as well as premature aging and hair loss. Its cooling energetics are employed in intermittent fevers (such as cholera or malaria). As an alterative, gotu kola is traditionally used for leprosy, eczema, psoriasis, and other chronic skin diseases. These indications are mirrored by Western use for these same skin ailments, as well as assisting in the repair of surgical wounds (Mills & Bone, 2005). Similarly, gotu kola is indicated in Western herbalism for mental fatigue and senility, reduced vitality, and high blood pressure (Wood, 2008).

Modern use of gotu kola reflects a rich base of evidence gathered from clinical trials on multiple body systems and tissues, primarily looking at its effects on connective tissue and the nervous, integumentary, cardiovascular, immune, and musculoskeletal systems. Much clinical research has been done on gotu kola and its constituents, which include triterpene saponins as a main group that contributes to its beneficial actions. Gotu kola leaf’s triterpene saponins are responsible for its ability to support collagen synthesis, acclerate wound repair, reduce scarring, improve skin firmness, aid connective tissues, and stimulate venous health.

A major mechanism that contributes to gotu kola’s anti-anxiety effects are its modulation of gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA), an inhibitory neurotransmitter that induces feelings of relaxation and calm (Pizzorno & Murray, 1999). In one study (Bradwejn et al., 2000), subjects’ startle effect, a marker of chronic anxiety, was greatly improved in most cases with a regular dose of 12 g daily. Gotu kola’s antiaging effects on the brain are a result of increased neuronal branching and improvement in free radical scavenging activity and the resulting reduction in oxidative stress and damage, which also makes it helpful for improving cognition and memory (Caldecott, 2006;Khalsa, 2008, as cited in Walsh, 2014). This, along with GABA modulation, suggests that gotu kola is a useful herb for ADHD in both children and adults.

Both in vitro research and post-mortem studies indicate gotu kola’s effectiveness in the processing of β-amyloid protein plaque, the accumulation of which is a key indicator of Alzheimer’s disease. Gotu kola and some isolated constituents (asiatic acid, for example) reduce plaque production, stop apoptosis of brain cells, and reduce free radical concentrations (Yance, 2013). Gotu kola also inhibits acetylcholinesterase (AcH), which contributes both to the pathology of Alzheimer’s disease as well as cell signal transmission that contributes to seizure (Nasir et al., 2012). While this is not yet tested on human subjects, the implication is that gotu kola could be an effective anticonvulsant in epilepsy.

Gotu kola is specifically indicated for soothing wounds, burns, keloids, and other skin traumas, both externally and internally. Referred to as “the herb of longevity,” gotu kola enjoys a long history of use maintaining supple, resilient skin, especially during the aging process and during menopause when skin’s natural collagen and elasticity decline. As a vulnerary, it stimulates collagen production in skin cells, improves granulation tissue formation, and increases the tensile strength of the skin, making it an excellent post-surgical support herb (Pizzorno & Murray, 1999; Bian et al., 2013).  At the same time, it reduces scarring in many differentf conditions. In particular, studies on keloid formation have found that extracts of gotu kola inhibit the overproduction of scar tissue by reducing the inflammatory phase of scar formation, while at the same time modulating certain growth factors (Pizzorno & Murray, 1999; Bian et al., 2013). 

Gotu kola’s tissue-building actions are also seen in its use for diabetic and syphilitic ulcers, bed sores, and ulcers and lesions in the digestive tract caused by tissue-destroying disorders including Crohn’s disease, gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD), and ulcerative colitis. Interestingly, it is helpful in cases of weak nails and hair, particularly in hypothyroidism, where it exerts a thyroid stimulating effect (Wood, 2008) as well as integumentary support. Gotu kola is also useful in immune conditions affecting the skin, such as scleroderma, eczema, and allergic reactions, as it inhibits mast cell degranulation, reduces itching and swelling, and calms proinflammatory signaling pathways (Walsh, 2014).

Beyond supporting the skin and brain, gotu kola supports immunity as an antiproliferative and chemoprotective during cancer; it also has been shown to reduce radiation-induced damage to DNA (Babykutty et al., 2008; Hausen, 1993). In vitro and in vivo studies show that gotu kola inhibits the proliferation of cancerous human cells of the breast, colon, and skin in a dose-dependent manner and inhibits the production of substances (including NF-kappa B, TNF-alpha, and a number of prostaglandins) that cause inflammation in the body system (Babykutty et al., 2008; Hausen, 1993).

Because it is both anti-inflammatory and promotes circulation of nutrients and oxygen to the extremeties, gotu kola is well-suited to balance disorders of the musculoskeletal system, including osteoarthritis and rheumatoid arthritis, joint injuries, soft tissue injuries to ligaments and tendons, and tendonitis. In one animal study on arthritis, the triterpene madecassoside, which modulates humoral and cell-mediated immunity, was shown to halt joint destruction by stopping joints from being infiltrated by inflammatory substances and lowering immunoglobulin (IgG) levels (Punturee et al., 2005, as cited in Walsh, 2014).

Gotu kola is used for many cardiovascular conditions, particularly where venous hypertension and insufficiency are present. These include edema, varicose veins, pain, cramping, hemorrhoids, ischemic reperfusion injury, and general fatigue. The triterpenes in gotu kola modulate fluid balance in the vasculature, decreasing swelling and feelings of heaviness and supporting micro-circulation at the capillary level. These qualities make it an effective aid in cases of microangiopathy, a common side effect of diabetes in which the capillary walls become thicker, weaker, and more likely to leak proteins while also slowing blood flow. In the same way that it supports skin health, gotu kola works to repair the lining of the vascular walls and stabilizes plaque due to lipid accumulation, making it especially useful in reducing the risk of thrombosis and atherosclerosis. Several studies have demonstrated gotu kola’s effectiveness in addressing varicose veins in which the metabolism of specific polysaccharides in vascular walls becomes altered; gotu kola helps to regulate the metabolic process, thus stabilizing the tissue (Cesarone et al., 1994; Arpaia et al., 1990, as cited in Walsh, 2014).

Adult Dose

Infusion: 1-8 g dried leaf/day divided into 1-3 doses (Mills & Bone, 2005).

Tincture: 1.5-3 mL (1:2, 30%) 1-3x/day (Winston & Kuhn, 2008).

Fresh juice: 25 mL or 3-10 g of powder, 2-3x/day (Caldecott, 2006)

Traditionally, gotu kola is prepared as a nerve tonic by decocting in milk, and as a rejuvenative it is best prepared in ghee; it can be combined with basil and black pepper for fever (Frawley & Lad, 1986).

Safety

Gotu kola is generally accepted as a gentle herb, contraindicated only where there is a known allergy (in which case it may result in contact dermatitis). While Brinker (1998) suggests that it can theoretically act as an emmenagogue, most sources show that the herb is safe in pregnancy, breastfeeding, and for children’s use. Gotu kola is also consumed as a raw or cooked vegetable in many areas, attesting to its safety. However, as with other saponin-rich herbs, there are some people for whom gotu kola may be inappropriate or should be dosed at low levels. These include those with fat malabsorption issues, deficiency of vitamins A, D, E, or K, some upper GI irritation, or cholestasis (Mills & Bone, 2005). Symptoms of gotu kola overdose include headache, dizziness, stupor, and bloody stools (Bastyr University, 2003). There is some concern about the potential for adulteration, as gotu kola shares a common name (brahmi) with another herb, Bacopa monnieri, so purchasing from trusted sources is key. It is also important to use gotu kola sourced from areas that are well-tested for purity, as it can grow in polluted ditches and waste areas, particularly in India (Wood, 2008).
