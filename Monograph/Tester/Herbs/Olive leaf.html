Native To

The cultivated European olive (Olea europaea subs. europaea) originated in the eastern areas of the Mediterranean basin (Bartolini & Petruccelli, 2002) in Asia Minor (Centre for Agriculture and Bioscience International [CABI], 2019) and is now considered native to southern Europe, temperate Asia, and Africa (CABI, 2019). It is often conflated with the African subspecies (Olea europaea subsp. cuspidata), which originated in Africa (CABI, 2019).

Botanical Description

Olive trees are evergreen and may live for more than 1,000 years (ISSG, 2010). The leaves have very short stalks, are arranged oppositely, and are lanceolate, ovate, and narrow in shape. The mature leaves are leathery and waxy in texture, green on the upper side, and silvery-whitish below.

The leaves will grow for 2-3 years before abscising (falling off the tree) (ISSG, 2010). Stomata located in trichomes on the undersides of the leaves help retain water for drought resistance (ISSG, 2010) and protect against ultraviolet radiation (Kiritsakis & Shahidi, 2010).

Olea europaea subs. europaea, the cultivated olive, is closely related to the wild olive (Olea europaea subs. oleaster or sylvestris), which has smaller leaves and fruit, shrubbier growth, and which has been hybridized with the cultivated olive (CABI, 2019).

 

Key Constituents

Secoiridoid monoterpenes (e.g., oleuropein), triterpenoids, flavonoids, hydroxytyrosol (Hashmi et al., 2015)

Sustainability

An olive epidemic manifested in late 2013, resulting at first in the demise of trees in Puglia, Italy (Butler, 2013), where ultimately over 230,000 hectares were impacted (European Parliament, 2019). The disease, caused by the pathogen Xylella fastidiosa, had by 2019 spread to Spain, Germany, and France, leaving in its wake widespread destruction of trees, over 1,000,000,000 euros in damage, and a 60% decrease in harvest (European Parliament, 2019). The cure remains unknown; climate change will only increase the bacterial spread, and research programs and containment measures remain underway (European Parliament, 2019).

Olive trees’ thick canopies can crowd out native species, resulting in altered ecosystems via loss of native biodiversity; because the olive is so long-lived, the seeds of native species in the soil become depleted, limiting  their regeneration after removal of olive trees (Invasive Species Specialist Group [ISSG], 2010). Olive trees may pose a fire hazard in dry environments because they are highly flammable due to their high oil content (ISSG, 2010).

Olive is declared an environmental weed in Australia (although the African subspecies seems a more frequent problem) and is considered  invasive in New Zealand and Hawaii (CABI, 2019).

 

Harvesting Guidelines

To obtain the highest levels of active phytochemicals, particularly oleuropein, harvest in the autumn and avoid hot days (Şahin et al., 2017). Leaves can gather dust; wash gently and dry for a few hours if tincturing fresh.

Uses

Archaeological records show that Olea europaea became established in Greece prior to 3500 BCE and was further developed in Africa during the Roman Empire (Bartolini & Petruccelli, 2002). While sixty or so species are distributed across the majority of Africa, South Asia, Central America, South Australia and New Zealand, as well as warm European zones, olive is considered typical of the Mediterranean areas (Lambardi & Rugini, 2003) where it is widely cultivated.

Olive appears in both cultivated and wild forms—the latter is considered to have originated in Asia Minor, Nubia, and possibly Italy, but now grows spontaneously in the Mediterranean climate (Bartolini & Petruccelli, 2002). While both cultivated olive and wild have been theorized to derive from distinctly different botanical specimens or from hybridizations, grasping the diffusion of different species beyond these conjectures is difficult (Bartolini & Petruccelli, 2002).

The European olive was introduced to the Americas perhaps as early as 1560, when three specimens were conveyed to Peru, one of which was stolen and used to create plantations in Chile (Sturtevant, 1919). The tree was first introduced to South Carolina, California, and Florida in the early to mid-18th century (Sturtevant, 1919).

The venerable olive tree has been known and used by human beings even before the development of literature. While its leafy boughs were employed in many ancient cultural contexts—the echoes of which survive today—very early herbal indications for the leaf along with their historical lineage have not yet been fully surveyed. Both an emergency application of olive leaf during a mid-19th century epidemic and the rising popularity of olive leaf extract (OLE) around the turn into the 21st century has effectively ignited scientific study and brought the multifaceted leaf back into use.

Olive and its leaves were widely used in antiquity in cultural contexts. While the geographical origination and development of cultivated olives is a complex subject, the olive tree is known to have been cultivated in ancient Egypt: its boughs appear in temple art and funerary archaeology (Bartolini & Petruccelli, 2002; Kiritsakis & Shahidi, 2017; Lucas & Harris, 1962).

The olive tree is ever associated with ancient Greece, where it was sacred to Athena and played a pivotal role in the founding myth of the city of Athens. Leaves in the form of wreaths were awarded to Olympic victors and brides also wore garlands. Indeed, every life stage seemed associated with its leaves, from newborn babies who received a small branch, to elders who carried boughs at the Olympic games, to the dead who were crowned with wreaths (Bartolini & Petruccelli, 2002) or laid to rest on pyres or couches of leaves (Lawson, 1910). The importance of the olive in ancient Greece is underscored by the fact that to harm a tree resulted in the death penalty (Bartolini & Petruccelli, 2002).

A branch of olive was also a well-known symbol of peace in Greece, where it also signified prosperity: its leafy branches were given as offerings to deities, rulers, and victors. The Romans strengthened its symbolism of peace, sending their envoys with olive branches to be used as peace tokens. In the Old Testament, a bough of olive was carried in the beak of a dove after the flood, and it is perhaps the most often mentioned botanical item in the Bible. Olive also appears in the Quran and is a symbol of peace in the Arab world (El-Shamy, 1995). Even today, the olive boughs may be found in governmental iconography throughout the world.

The olive leaf was also used in ancient magical practices. At least two manuscript fragments from Greco-Roman Egypt demonstrate that an olive leaf was used to write spells upon or worn as an amulet, but what is also interesting about these spells is that they were intended to ward off fevers (Betz, 1992)—an herbal use through which olive leaf would, many centuries later, become reintroduced to modern society.

Olive leaf appears later in other European magical practices connected to Christianity. There were seven olive trees in the Garden of Gethsemane, planted during Christ’s lifetime, and it was believed that wearing their leaves near to one’s chest would ward off harm (Daniels & Stevens, 1903/2003). Olive leaf also had a role to play in love magic practiced by women at some undefined historical point: standing beneath an olive tree, back towards the trunk, a woman would pick an olive leaf and toss it over her right shoulder while reciting, “Go forth, little messenger, like Noah’s faithful dove, and bring to me an olive-leaf of love” (Daniels & Stevens, 1903/2003, p. 824). If the leaf fell to the ground, it meant misfortune in love, but if caught and carried by the wind, it portended that she would be a content wife within a year (Daniels & Stevens, 1903/2003).

Olea comes directly from the Latin noun olea, meaning either “an olive” or an “olive tree.” Its second name, europaea, was given by Linnaeus in 1764 (Bartolini & Petruccelli, 2002), likely to indicate its favored southern European habitat. Its constituent oleuropein, while found in all parts of the tree, is considered the primary constituent in olive leaf, which also grants the leaf its bitter taste (Barbaro et al., 2014). It is this chemical that has received the vast majority of scientific exploration and is deemed responsible for many of the leaf’s actions, often in liquid or powdered extract form. Olive leaf extract (OLE) became a popular supplement around 1997 along with the focus on the heart-healthy benefits of the Mediterranean diet and has dominated scientific inquiries.

Olive leaf is astringent, anti-inflammatory, antimicrobial, antioxidant, and febrifugal; all of these actions contribute to its longest-standing historical indication, which has been kept extant from deep antiquity through the Renaissance: as a styptic and anti-infective for wounds. A section of the Hippocratic Corpus that dates to around the mid-5th century BCE indicated the leaf—sometimes in poultices or boiled vinegars—for sores or ulcers and for heavy menstruation (Hippocrates, 1567).

Celsus, a Roman encyclopedist (ca. 25 BCE-50 CE) who is still known for his work De Medicina, also mentioned olive leaf: in describing how herbs were injected into a wound with a syringe before a plaster was applied to further arrest and clean the wound, he noted that a decoction of olive leaves was suitable for such a plaster, especially for cankerous wounds (Celsus, 47/1961).

There are also a few extant records during the medieval period: in his medical tract, Peter of Spain (1205-1277) recorded using olive leaf wine as a poultice for itchy male genitals (Brozyna, 2005). In a medieval medical handbook of the 13th century, John the Physician’s Therapeutics, olive leaf plays a vulnerary and anti-infectious role: a juice derived from olive leaf is mixed with honey and dripped into the ear as a way to address pus (Zipser, 2009). Olive leaves were also made into a decoction to be kept in the mouth for several hours to combat bleeding lesions, or the “succulent” leaves of the olive tree chewed to soothe an inflamed tongue (Zipser, 2009, p. 85).

During the medieval period, olive leaves were also burned in fumigation, but apparently not for infectious conditions. In Salerno, the “Salernitan women”—a group of female physicians who studied at the Schola Medica Salernitana, one of the first medical schools to enroll female students—burned olive leaves for children with cerebral issues or paralysis (Green, 2001). Unrelated scientific study is now starting to be conducted on neurodegenerative syndromes and olive leaf’s neuroprotective abilities (Hashmi et al., 2015).

The vulnerary and anti-infectious usage of olive leaf also appears in early Eastern practice. According to al-Kindī (an Arab Muslim polymath, philosopher, and physician of 801-873 CE), olive leaves were used to tend gums and as an ingredient in toothpaste (Lev & Amar, 2008). Its use as a mouth cleanser is found today in the Moroccan pharmacopoeia (Bellakhdar et al., 1991). In Iran and Iraq, the leaves have traditionally been used for cough, as a constricting herb, disinfectant, diuretic, and stimulant, while Yemenite Jews have used them for colds, jaundice, and wounds (Lev & Amar, 2008).

Some Eastern texts without a clear date mention that the leaf was used for constriction and as a disinfectant, carminative, and febrifuge in England (Lev & Amar, 2008), which is curious because there is not readily available evidence of all these uses there. Olive leaf was certainly in Western medicinal use in 1877 (Rudolphy, 1877) but was known long before that: it was planted in the Oxford Botanic Garden as early as 1648 (Ellacombe, 1878/2019) and was recognized by herbalist John Parkinson as growing in England although it does not flourish there (Ellacombe, 2019; Parkinson, 1640). Its use in herbalism may be traced further back: it appears wild olive went by the name “oleaster” or “oleaster sylvestris” and the 10th century Anglo-Saxon Leech Book has a prescription using “Oleaster, that is wild Olive tree” rind, or bark (as cited in Ellacombe, 1878/2019, p. 185).

The first herbal published in English during the Renaissance traced the use of olive leaf back to Galen and went into depth regarding several uses for wounds, although much of these may be copied directly from antiquity (Turner, 1551) as they were again by Parkinson, who also remarked that wild olive leaves were more “cooling and binding” than the cultivated, especially their juices mixed with vinegar (Parkinson, 1640, p. 1440). The 1695 edition of Culpeper’s London Dispensary notes olive leaves in a list of “herbs and leaves” and also makes the following entry in a section called The Physicians Library (apparently launched by a contemporary, not Culpeper himself): “Oleæ folia. Olive leaves: they are hard to come by here” (Culpeper, 1695, p. 34).

Perhaps olive leaf was known but not necessarily widely used in practice nor readily available in northern Europe up to the early 19th century. A smattering of mid-19th-century folk records in southern Africa reveal active use of olive leaf (Olea europaea subsp. africana), where it functioned as a styptic and vulnerary, and there is a very early 20th-century Cape Dutch use of cold poultices of leaves in eye injuries and leaf extract in an eye lotion for humans and animals (Long et al., 2010).

The ancient use for wounds may even have partially survived into present-day Italy; ethnobotanical data from southern Italy has revealed that olive leaf infusion may be applied externally for ingrown nails or to restore the epithelium (De Feo & Senatore, 1993). Yet despite sustained historical records, it appears that only one in vivo animal study (Koca et al., 2011), has attempted to back up olive leaf’s long-documented reputation as a vulnerary.

Olive leaf had its first major historical reappearance in the mid-19th century for a different indication: it garnered a reputation as a febrifuge and anti-infectious herb in scientific circles when the British pharmacologist and botanist David Hanbury undertook a voyage to the Mediterranean and the Middle East with the intention of collecting and studying various plants. He then published a brief article on olive’s febrifugal qualities in which there is a rare wealth of information, and which made olive leaf known to the world as a botanical with potential.

The reason for olive leaf’s reappearance in this context is because Hanbury took the time to recount in his article a letter from his friend, Sidney Maltass of Smyrna, who in 1843 was on the island of Mytilene when it was overrun by an often-fatal infection hallmarked by fever. The druggists had run out of quinine and Maltass tried to think of a substitute for helping the ill:

“…knowing that biberine and salicine were often used for fever and ague, I turned over in my mind all the bitters that I could think of which might prove effectual. Many were poisonous, and I rejected them, then thought of olive leaves, and after several trials made on myself, I commenced administering doses of a decoction of the leaves, say two handfuls boiled in a quart of water til evaporation had reduced it to a point. This I gave in doses of a wine-glassful every three or four hours. Obstinate cases of the fever gave way before it; and for many years I have found it more effective than quinine.

I have recommended it to our medical men, and although at first they ridiculed the idea, they all use it now. It is a most valuable remedy for the poor in an olive-growing country” (Hanbury, 1854, pp. 353-354).

In fact, the use of olive leaf for fever predates Maltass’s “discovery” during the epidemic. Hanbury went on to say that Maltass’s experience was corroborated by French sources and trials by Spanish doctors (Hanbury, 1854). He referred also to a Dr. Pallas, who in 1828 noted olive leaf’s use among Spanish physicians, and its use as a substitute for cinchona (Cinchona officinalis) bark among the French Officiers de Santé during the Peninsular War of 1808-1813 (Hanbury, 1854). Pallas also noted a bitter principle to which he ascribed olive’s febrifugal qualities, and which would later be called oleuropein, the star of OLE today.

It was noted around the time of Hanbury’s article that with so many fevers in the East, a leaf extract should be investigated as an alternative to expensive quinine (Pereira, 1855/2014). Olive leaf was also recognized in the early 20th century by Maud Grieve (1931/1971) (possibly via awareness of Hanbury) as having “valuable febrifugal qualities” (p. 599) and she also noted its use as a decoction for fevers in the Levant. As an infusion of the leaf was mentioned for fever in Gastaldo’s Official Compendium of the Italian Flora in 1974 (Hashmi et al., 2015), it perhaps sustained a folk use for fever in Italy, but as of today any further research on its febrifugal qualities appears altogether absent.

In Africa, olive leaf decoctions have traditionally been used in other types of infections, specifically for sore throats, colds, and diphtheria (Long et al., 2010). In Tunisian folk herbalism, olive leaves are used for several types of inflammatory conditions and bacterial infections as well as cough, gingivitis, otitis, and jaundice (Haloui et al., 2010).

Hanbury’s publication, however, ultimately led to pharmaceutical studies being undertaken in the late 1960s by The Upjohn Company, which concluded that the leaf inhibited all bacteria, fungus, protozoan, and yeast against which it was tested (Ritchason, 1999). A great many scientific studies have now validated the antiviral and antimicrobial agencies of olive leaf (Hashmi et al., 2015). While oleuropein is largely credited with these actions, the molecular mechanisms remain somewhat mysterious (Barbaro et al., 2014).

There is a belief that OLE can kill viruses because oleuropein interacts with the protein coat of the virus and not with its genetic material while supporting the immune system (Ritchason, 1999). An uncontrolled study by Dr. Robert Lyons at a clinic in Budapest on 500 people from ages 12-50 with a range of conditions apparently showed an astounding recovery rate from infections with use of OLE (Ritchason, 1999) and the elenolic acid within oleuropein may also be credited with inhibiting viruses (Ritchason, 1999).

Olive leaf constituents have indeed been tested against several Gram positive and Gram negative bacteria (Hashmi et al., 2015; Lee & Lee, 2010) as well as fungi, and extracts have demonstrated a marked combined antifungal and antibacterial activity even at low concentrations (Pereira et al., 2007). In one study, an aqueous extract did not demonstrate effective antibacterial agency while acetone and ethyl alcohol extracts did; oleuropein was again credited with the effects (Korukluoglu et al., 2010). The effects have also been noticed with respect to the leaves’ caffeic acid, and combined constituents have a higher antimicrobial effect than those in isolation (Lee & Lee, 2010).

Meanwhile, the anti-inflammatory olive leaf possesses a folk connection to bone health and therefore to addressing forms of arthritis. In Mediterranean folk medicine, the leaf is used for gout, a practice that incited scientific study, again with a focus on the agency of oleuropein and how it prevents the buildup of uric acid (Flemmig et al., 2011). Olive leaf also contains hydroxytyrosol, a powerful antioxidant and anti-inflammatory phenolic compound found in leaves after hydrolysis of oleuropein. This constituent has been studied with success in a double-bind, placebo-controlled trial in which patients with gonarthrosis reported significant improvement of pain after 4 weeks treatment with OLE containing 22% hydroxytyrosol (Takeda et al., 2013). Oleuropein and hydroxytyrosol were found to substantially impact both formation and maintenance of bone in animal studies, leading researchers to suggest olive leaf may be of use in osteoporosis; however, clinical studies are needed to confirm this (Hagiwara et al., 2013).

The leaf has been used in folk medicine contexts for diabetes as well (Hashmi et al., 2015). In the Beni Mellal region of Morocco, a recent ethnobotanical survey of herbalists, folk practitioners, and traditional healers demonstrated that olive leaf is in fact the most commonly employed botanical for this purpose (Mrabti et al., 2019); the same is true in the Souk Ahras province of Algeria (Amel, 2013), and it is also frequently used in Palestine (Ali-Shtayeh et al., 2012). OLE is now a popular go-to for diabetes, and many studies confirm that leaf constituents aid in lowering serum glucose and insulin levels (Hashmi et al., 2015) and may inhibit oxidative stress connected to the complications of diabetes (Hashmi et al., 2015; Jemai et al., 2009).

While most scientific studies to date have been on rats (Hashmi et al., 2015), there is at least one foundational human study, which was in fact catalyzed by folk usage: a New Zealand human trial using 46 overweight, middle-aged males at risk for metabolic syndrome, gave the participants olive leaf polyphenols for 12 weeks (de Bock et al., 2013). The oleuropein and hydroxytyrosol in olive leaf extract demonstrated an ability to improve insulin sensitivity and pancreatic b-cell secretory capacity (de Bock et al., 2013), suggesting further research and use for type 2 diabetes.

Another major area of olive leaf’s influence is cardiovascular wellbeing. As early as 1976, olive leaf was used in a traditional way in Europe for hypertension and atherosclerosis (European Medicines Agency, 2011), but it is unclear from where this use arose. Water decoctions have apparently been used in Greek folk methods for high blood pressure for some time (Lawrendiadis, 1961) and it is traditionally used in Algeria for hypertension in decoction or infusion form, sometimes in oil maceration (Amel, 2013) as well as in Morocco (Mrabti et al., 2019).

Many such folk methods have inspired animal studies that demonstrated lowered blood pressure. Olive products in general form a substantial part of what is commonly referred to as the Mediterranean diet, the practitioners of which have notably lower rates of cardiovascular disease. The leaf’s hypotensive reputation was confirmed when it was shown to have coronary vasodilatory activity in an animal study (Zarzuelo et al., 1991) and later a new secoiridoid was identified: oleacein, which is a potent angiotensin converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitor (Pengelly, 2004). The leaf’s constituents oleuropein, hydroxytyrosol, and tyrosol have also since been linked to anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, anti-ischemic, anti-atherosclerotic, and hypolipidemic effects upon the myocardium, which has been studied in both in vitro and in vivo studies (Efentakis et al., 2015; Marković et al., 2019).

Human clinical trials have followed: in one study, hypertensive patients who had aqueous OLE for 3 months (after 15 days on placebo) experienced significant decreases in blood pressure sans side effects (Cherif et al., 1996). A Swiss study tested an extract on borderline hypertensive human subjects, namely 40 identical twin pairs divided into two groups, one receiving 500 mg of extract daily and the other 1,000 mg (Perrinjaquet-Moccetti et al., 2008). In 8 weeks’ time, those with higher dosage demonstrated lower blood pressure and LDL cholesterol (Perrinjaquet-Moccetti et al., 2008). The lowering of blood pressure by OLE if given in a high enough dosage was later considered scientifically confirmed(Susalit et al., 2011). A recent study in a randomized, double-blind, controlled, crossover trial looking at the impact of OLE on metabolic and vascular measures also confirmed the leaf’s hypotensive and lipid-lowering effects (Lockyer et al., 2017).

Olive leaf—specifically oleuropein but also with other flavonoids and phenolics—also exerts an antitumor effect on many different types of cancer lines (Barbaro et al., 2014; Hashmi et al., 2015; Ruzzolini et al., 2018) and is subsequently receiving much scientific attention in the cancer field. A triterpenoid called maslinic acid found in both fruit and leaves may also contribute to this effect;  it is a known antitumor agent and has shown antiproliferative effects against colon cancer cells (Reyes-Zurita et al., 2009).

Olive leaf’s newly emergent profile along with its somewhat neglected historical indications suggest that it is a remarkably diverse and powerful botanical about which there remains much to realize.

Adult Dose

Infusion: 2 teaspoons dried leaf per 8 fl oz water steeped for 1 hour) 2-3x/day (Winston, 2003).

Tincture: 1.5-2 mL (1:5, 60%) 3x/day (Winston & Kuhn, 2008).

Safety

There appear to be no known toxicities in either long or short-term usage of the leaf (Wood, 2008), although research in this area is lacking (European Medicines Agency, 2011). Taking olive leaf with meals, however, may avoid gastrointestinal discomfort (Winston & Kuhn, 2008). Due to the fact that olive leaf lowers blood sugar levels, it may be considered contraindicated for individuals with hypoglycemia (Gonzalez et al., 1992; Winston & Kuhn, 2008). Participants using olive leaf extract (OLE) in a study reported adverse effects that, while mild, included coughing, vertigo, muscle discomfort, headache, fatigue, malaise, myalgia, and muscle cramps (Susalit et al., 2011).
