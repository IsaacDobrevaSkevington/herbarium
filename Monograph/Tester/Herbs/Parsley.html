Parsley

Native To

Parsley’s true nativity is debated (eastern Mediterranean; Algeria or Lebanon; or Sardinia, after Linnaeus) but from about the early 20th century it has been generally described as native to Southern Europe or the Mediterranean.

Geographic Distribution

Parsley is widely distributed throughout the Mediterranean, various European countries, and Western Asia (Nirumand et al., 2018). It is also widely naturalized as a culinary herb in many European countries and elsewhere in the world (Nirumand et al., 2018).

Botanical Description

Parsley flourishes in rocky terrains and wastelands. It is biennial in temperate regions, with its flower stem appearing in the second year. The plant may grow up to 30 cm or 1 foot in height (Chevallier, 2016).

The herb is mildly aromatic and features many hollow stalks growing from one root. The deeply divided leaves are of a vibrant green hue and may be either curly or flat depending on variety. The root is whitish and resembles a parsnip. The flowers, which are small, yellow, and appear on compound umbels, yield tiny ovoid seeds.

The leaves of flat-leaf parsley may be visually confused with those of other herbs in the Apiaceae family: cilantro/coriander (Coriandrum sativum) as well as celery leaf (Apium graveolens). Parsley should also be carefully distinguished from cow parsley/wild chervil (Anthriscus sylvestris), which in turn is often mistaken for the deadly hemlock (Conium maculatum).

Parsley attracts and hosts wildlife, notably swallowtail butterflies, who use the herb for their larvae. It has also been planted around onions to deter the onion fly and near roses for greenfly, as well as with tomatoes and asparagus to entice bees (Watts, 2007).

Key Constituents

Leaf: flavonoids (apigenin), ascorbic acid;

Aerial parts and seed: volatile oils containing apiol and myristicin;

Leaf and root: coumarins (Farzaei et al., 2013).

 

Sustainability

Due to the fact that parsley is both readily available as a cultivated plant and is also rarely ever found in the wild nowadays (Chevallier, 2016), there are no current sustainability issues.

 

Harvesting Guidelines

Harvest the leaves at any point during the growing season, taking from the outer stalks first.

Gather the seeds just as they begin to naturally fall to the ground.

One may dig the roots either in the autumn of its first year or in the spring of its second year of growth. Remove the leaves and then either cut and dry the roots or store them in slightly damp sand. If using the root for culinary purposes, however, do not remove the leaves until ready to cook.

Uses

Many folks may still regard parsley as the bit of green stuff languishing on a restaurant plate—decorative yet usually untouched—although it is increasingly acknowledged as one of the most nutritious components of a meal. An ancient botanical, parsley is particularly known for its impact on the urogenital, digestive, and female reproductive systems, but in fact possesses a wide range of possible actions that cannot be fully covered here. At the same time, remarkably little human research has been undertaken on parsley, even while animal studies are slowly proliferating.

Parsley’s Latin genus name Petroselinum is roughly equivalent to “rock celery”: the word comes directly from the ancient Greek πετροσέλινον, which sometimes appears to be synonymous with our parsley in historical records. Petro– is from πέτρος, meaning “stone” and –selinum from σέλινον, meaning “celery.” Due to a plethora of ancient words used to describe celery and parsley, there has been rampant confusion, stemming from antiquity onwards, in differentiating between the two plants (for an in-depth historical overview of this issue, refer to Tobyn et al., 2011).

The ancient Greeks used a plant called selinon, but beyond the debate as to whether that was parsley or celery, there was also a grappling—often found in Renaissance herbals—as to which selinon was which (there is more than one kind). This confusion was a problem even in ancient days: for example, Dioscorides (70/2000), the Greek physician and botanist of the first century CE, remarked that oreoselinon was mixed with diuretics, antidotes, and heating medicines, but that it should be distinguished from petroselinon, which grew on rocks. Meanwhile, the much earlier Greek botanical writer Theophrastus (c. 371-287 BCE) had apparently identified parsley as oreoselinon and differentiated between curly and flat-leaf varieties (as cited in D’Andrea, 1982).

Selinon often fits a historical profile of parsley. It was used not only to crown athletes but also to adorn the tombs of the dead, and the plant was so associated with death among some Greeks that it seems not to have been used for cooking. The ancient Greek myth of Opheltes seems to account for a marked association of selinon with both athletes and death (for more on this, refer to Bravo, 2018).

Selinon seems to have been generally associated in ancient times with Hades and Persephone, king and queen of the underworld; vases depict the deities with branches of the herb. Parsley would later become associated with St. Peter, possibly after the saint took over the ancient role of Charon, ferryman of the underworld, in his position of guarding the gates of heaven (Grieve, 1931; Storl, 2016). Parsley has also been associated with Persephone in contemporary Wicca.

The Romans, on the other hand, seemed to have enjoyed parsley at the table. There is also some evidence that proto-Celtic peoples also knew parsley prior to Roman conquest; it has been found in archeobotanical evidence from at least one 600-400 BCE site (Wagner et al., 2020). It is often bandied about that the Romans wore garlands of parsley to stave off inebriation. The Romans did incorporate a parsley-like plant known as apium at banquets, particularly when wine and drinking bouts were involved, as several of the poems of the Roman poet Horace also show (Lewis & Short, 1879).

The Roman polymath Pliny the Elder of the first century CE is often the source on parsley for later herbalists. He described what might be parsley under the name apium (note that he mentions petroselinum elsewhere and as a type of parsley), which some philologists have assumed is synonymous with selinon and that it could denote either parsley or celery, and thus the identity of apium has remained controversial in scholarly circles (Andrews, 1949). The English Renaissance herbalist Parkinson, however, who was himself mired in ancient source material and often found himself sorting through it, refers to parsley as both selinum or apium—which he acknowledges at the same time were words that also referred to many different sorts of herbs.

Pliny (77/1906) wrote that apium was greatly esteemed and went so far as to say that of all things dug out of the earth, it had the greatest diversity. He noted the plant’s culinary uses: it was found in countryside milk drinks and especially in sauces . It was also used to revive sickly stock fish (Pliny, 77/1906), an observation that the author of the first English herbal in the early 16th century, Turner (1551), resurrected by noting that if parsley were thrown into fishponds, the fish would be healed.

Pliny (77/1906) also remarked that apium could be either female or male and that Greek writers such as Chrysippus and Dionysius had said that the female version should not be used because it was consecrated in funeral feasts for the dead. He also claimed it would hurt eyesight, that its stalk passed on worms creating barrenness, and that children who partook of breastmilk in an apium-eating mother would become epileptic (Pliny, 77/1906). (One wonders if the worms were actually the larvae of the swallowtail butterfly. Meanwhile, the reference to epilepsy has been variously rejected or espoused by herbal writers ever since.)

In terms of post-antiquity folklore, there are too many examples to record here. The great majority of them, however, have to do with growing parsley while counteracting evil, which is perhaps passed down from its aforementioned connection with the underworld. In England, the seeding of parsley was preferably done on Good Friday or another holy day (and in Ireland, on St. Patrick’s Day) to avoid the interference of the devil (Watts, 2007). Parsley was said by some Pennsylvania Dutch to grow better if one first urinated in the hole in which it was to be planted, and some said it took either a wicked man or a good man to plant it (Watts, 2007). In Maryland, there was a practice of sowing parsley by blowing the seeds from the pages of a Bible (Watts, 2007).

In 16th-century France, meanwhile, parsley was said to shatter glass or to give that power to a woman who touched it (Watts, 2007). There are also a handful of examples of parsley’s connection with love rituals (Storl, 2016). Parsley seems to have been historically associated with virility and heroism in the male sphere but with death and witchcraft in the feminine, espoused by the following adage: “Parsley helps a man mount his horse, but it helps a woman end up under the ground” (Storl, 2016, p. 312).

There is also some indication that parsley functioned as an aphrodisiac (Wood, 2008), which is also noted in the Persian folklore record (Hamedi et al., 2017). Parsley has also played a symbolic role in other Eastern traditions. Karpas, one of six foods on the Seder plate of Hebrew Passover, is often represented by parsley: the herb is dipped in saltwater and functions variously as a symbol of springtime, the prosperity of the Israelites in Egypt, slavery, and the coat of Joseph (Moffic, 2020).

It has often been repeated—without clear evidence—that Catherine de Medici introduced the culinary use of parsley to France in the 16th century (Wheaton, 1983). The herb does appear in de la Varenne’s cookbook of the 17th century and afterwards it appears to be commonly used in cuisine, but that could have been an even earlier occurrence. Herbalist Gerard (1545-1612) mentions that parsley leaves are quite pleasant in sauces and broths and impart a pleasing taste (Gerard, 1636), while Parkinson (1567-1650), largely regarded as the last great English herbalist, reported that it was much used in meats and broths (Parkinson, 1640). Parsley has long been used in the bouquet garni of France and is known as a breath freshener, particularly to offset the effects of garlic. There are also two main cultivars known well in the culinary sphere: curly leaf parsley and flat-leaf parsley ((Petroselinum crispum var. neapolitanum), otherwise known as Italian parsley.

While the roots, leaves, and seeds of parsley have long been used, the seed has quite often been considered the strongest part of the plant (Gerard, 1636; Wood, 2008). The leaf and root are usually infused and drunk, but the seed is quite a different creature and is used for its aromatic oils, which are often extracted. Twelfth-century German Benedictine abbess and herbalist Hildegard von Bingen (1155/2001) regarded parsley as something that “generates seriousness in the mind,” (p. 68 ) as a remedy more warm than cold, rather robust, and better taken raw.

One of parsley’s primary historical herbal indications is for bladder and kidney health, especially when it comes to stones, and the herb remains perhaps best known for this purpose today. Herbalist Matthew Wood (2008) has remarked that parsley’s natural rocky habitat, alluded to in its very name (petro– means stone), is a signature for its ability to resolve kidney stones. Parsley’s long-standing reputation for breaking up deposits in the bladder or kidneys appears to go back as far as Roman antiquity; Pliny the Elder (77/1906) remarked that the seed of apium was a diuretic and that the seed or root in wine broke bladder stones. Apium was applied topically with egg white or boiled in water and drunk for kidney issues (Pliny, 77/1906). The root and seed of a type of parsley called oreoselinon, taken as a libation in wine, were also used as a diuretic (Dioscorides, 70/2000).

Jumping forward in time, Hildegard von Bingen (1155/2001) suggested a preparation  for stones that combined two parts parsley with one part saxifrage (Saxifraga spp.), strained through cloth and drunk in a sauna. The same combination of herbs was cooked in water and also poured over the sauna stones; she deemed that this ritual, done often, would make a person better (von Bingen, 1155/2001).

By the Renaissance period, parsley was clearly commonplace for herbalists. In some editions of herbalist Culpeper (1653/1789), parsley is described as so commonly known that it did not even need a description. Both Culpeper and Parkinson (1640) confirmed that it was used to break stones and gave a recipe from Tragus (otherwise known as Hieronymus Bock, the German physician, botanist, and minister, c. 1498-1554) for kidney stones that, while far more complicated and with many more herb ingredients, also mentions saxifrage like Hildegard.

There are even a few sporadic reports from the New World that some indigenous tribes have made use of parsley for this body system: Cherokee tribes have used parsley in infusion of leaves and roots for kidneys, dropsy, and bladder issues (Hamel & Chiltoskey, 1975; Native American Ethnobotany Database, 2003) and European settlers of Appalachia also employed it as a diuretic for kidney inflammation (Garrett, 2003). The Mi’kmaq have traditionally used parsley as a urinary aid in instances of cold being present in the bladder (Chandler et al., 1979; Native American Ethnobotany Database, 2003).

In Chinese medicine, parsley is associated with the Bladder meridian (Tierra, 1988) and regulates bladder qi and harmonizes urination (Holmes, 2006). Even homeopathy connects parsley to the urinary system: Samuel Hahnemann, the father of the discipline, noted parsley as a specific for slime and mucus in the urine due to gonorrhea (as cited in Wood, 2008).

It is conjectured that parsley’s high magnesium and chlorophyll content inhibits the dehydration of calcium oxalate and eases hyperoxaluria (Kuźma et al., 2014; Nirumand et al., 2018). In rat models, parsley has been found to be effective in regulating the urinary pH to a level in which calcium oxalate crystals are kept as dispersed particles to be eliminated (Al-Yousofy et al., 2017; Nirumand et al., 2018; Saeidi et al., 2012).

Some 20th-century practitioners have meanwhile suggested parsley as a tonic for the adrenal glands (Wood, 2008). Parsley and its extracts have also been used on the market as complementary or alternative preparations for renal diseases (Nirumand et al., 2018), often in popular supplement forms. Some scientific assessments warn nephrologists against this, however, as only animal models have been used and have found adverse effects (Vamenta-Morris et al., 2014) and there is also variation in some effects of diuresis depending on the type of preparation in rat models (Kreydiyyeh & Usta, 2002).

Parsley has also been historically used as a digestive aid, largely through a carminative action, but is also deemed relaxant and spasmolytic by herbalists and also some animal studies. This does not seem to be a notable indication in antiquity, at least in extant records. Its use in this fashion goes back at least as far as Hildegard von Bingen (1155/2001), who wrote that those with a sick stomach should take parsley with fennel (Foeniculum vulgare) and soapwort (Saponaria officinalis) and make a relish to which was added butter, beef fat, and dried salt—it was then eaten often in cooked form. If one ate garlic and suffered stomach pains, eating parsley would also reduce the discomfort (von Bingen, 1155/2001).

In the same way that children have been given distilled water of dill (Anethum graveolens) (Grieve, 1931), parsley was used for children with trapped winds. Renaissance herbalists Culpeper (1653/1789) and Parkinson (1640) also remarked that nurses very commonly gave distilled water of parsley to children with trapped winds and that it was also very useful this way for adults.

More recently, parsley has been used as a carminative and for gastrointestinal problems in Peru and Iran (Farzaei et al., 2013). The Cherokee have also used it for a range of digestive issues, among them indigestion, diarrhea, gas, and colic (Garrett, 2003). Parsley has also been used in Italy as an anal suppository for constipated children and babies (Jashemski, 1999). According to herbalist Matthew Wood (2008), the slight bitterness of parsley increases digestion. Scientific inquiry, albeit so far just in animal models, is also starting to suggest parsley’s efficacy against gastric injury due to stress (Akıncı et al., 2017).

Parsley also pertains to the liver not only in Western herbal history but also in Eastern. In ancient Rome, the seed of apium was given in white wine to persons afflicted with jaundice (Pliny, 77/1906). Centuries later, Culpeper (1653/1789) and Parkinson (1640) would repeat this indication in the aforementioned recipe copied from Tragus, who deemed it excellent for jaundice, whereby the condition was eased through diuretic action.

In Chinese medicine, parsley is associated with the liver and Liver meridian as well as the stomach (Tierra, 1988) (Note, however, that Petroselinum should not be confused with plants known as Chinese parsley, namely Coriandrum sativum). Parsley seed possesses a reputation as a hypotensive for Wood element types and is used for bilious migraines (Holmes, 2006). At least one scholar of Asian wellness modalities has noted that parsley decoctions are used in India for jaundice and liver issues to remove toxins from the blood (Edde, 1998). Additionally, likely due to its potent mixture of vitamins and minerals, herbal and folklore records have indicated parsley as antianemic (Barker, 2001; Hamedi et al., 2017; Holmes, 2006).

Parsley may at one time have been seen as a spring alterative or liver tonic; at least one Renaissance record notes that in the month of May it was eaten with sage, butter, and salt in England to maintain the health of the body (Parkinson, 1640). Parsley’s blood-cleansing action and liver affinity might also have something to do with the fact that it was used for snakebite in very early English times (Cockayne, 1864/2012). Gerard (1636) also mentioned that parsley seed “resists poisons, and are therefore mixed with treacles” (p. 1014) and that both root and seed (but the seed being the stronger of the two) was boiled and imbibed in ale to “cast forth strong venom or poison” (p. 1014). Animal studies have begun to validate folkloric uses of parsley for liver health (Farzaei et al., 2013).

Parsley has also been strongly associated with various gynecological conditions since antiquity. Wood (2008) noted that in 1849 French herbalists introduced the use of parsley for menstrual irregularity, but this indication may in fact be traced back to Roman times; Pliny noted that apium promoted menstruation. It was also used to expel the afterbirth (Pliny, 77/1906), both indications which are repeated by the Renaissance herbalists. Dioscorides (70/2000) also indicated that the aforementioned oreoselinon was used to increase menstrual flow.

The Eclectics would later use parsley to bring down the menses and ascribed this action to the apiol content, remarking that it could also reduce pain. It was preferred in a liquid form, and the Eclectics noted that the preparations often did not succeed in creating emmenagogic effects due the uncertain quality of apiol-rich preparations (Felter, 1922). Parsley is still used by herbalists for dysmenorrhea and amenorrhea (Barker, 2001; Wood, 2008) and Wood (2008) remarks that one can use the seed tinctured in vinegar for severe cases of dysmenorrhea. Parsley has also been used by the Cherokee for gynecological purposes: an infusion of leaves has been used for women with scant menstrual flow (Hamel & Chiltoskey, 1975). Its anticoagulant and antiplatelet aspects combined with its estrogenic qualities and spasmolytic actions appear to work together to address imbalances in menstruation (Farzaei et al., 2013).

Parsley also has a long history of being used externally for wounds and infections and is considered by modern researchers to be antimicrobial. We know that the herb apium was used in Roman antiquity for sores and wounds. A decoction of the seed was used to promote the resolution of bruises and mouth ulcers (Pliny, 77/1906). Pliny (77/1906) also recorded that apium was applied to the eyes in various mixtures: as just the beaten plant material, a bread or polenta mixture, and in honey, which was sometimes fomented with a warm decoction of the herb for eye discharge and also applied to other parts of the body. The indication for eyes gets repeated through the centuries, and in 1976 it was noted by Dr. J.V. Cerney that parsley was effective for weak eyes and other eye conditions (as cited in Wood, 2008). Parsley has also been used for insect bites (Grieve, 1931; Wood, 2008). At the same time, it has been put to use both for external parasites such as lice (Holmes, 2006) as well as used against nematodes in the garden.

Apiol was at first obtained from parsley in 1849 by two doctors in Brittany and subsequently put to use for ague and malaria (Grieve, 1931). This is interesting as the oils of many plants of the parsley family are diaphoretic (Elpel, 2010), but the historical record is not strong in this regard for parsley itself. Hildegard von Bingen (1155/2001), however, indicated parsley for mild fevers, and Persian records also indicate the herb as an antipyretic (Hamedi et al., 2017).

Parsley’s essential oil has since been deemed a useful antimicrobial agent by at least one study that set out to determine its antibacterial and antifungal activities in light of a demand for natural products. The essential oil was tested against eight fungi and seven bacteria and was found to have both bacteriostatic and bactericidal activity against all the bacteria and fungistatic activity against all fungi. Its fungicidal activity was found to be higher than the conventional antifungal medication, ketoconazole (Linde et al., 2016).

Parsley has a lesser-known but long-standing and clear connection to arthritis, rheumatism, and musculoskeletal issues. Imbibing parsley tea or chewing the leaves has been said to ward off or address rheumatism in folkloric records (Watts, 2007). In ancient Rome, Pliny (77/1906) noted that the root juice of apium, when mixed with wine, helped lumbago pains. In The Old English Herbarium (by Pseudo-Apuleius, dating back to the 4th century CE), parsley is suggested for sore sinews, being pounded up and laid on the affected parts (as cited in Cockayne, 1864/2012). Centuries later Hildegard von Bingen (1155/2001) followed suit by advising that those who had gout (from too much alcohol) combine parsley with four times the amount of rue (Ruta graveolens) and roast in a dish with olive oil or goat tallow, then place the preparation warm over the affected parts of the body and tie a cloth over them. In the Persian folklore record, parsley is also indicated as antiarthritic (Hamedi et al., 2017).

Some herbals remark that parsley seeds can be used as a substitute for celery (Apium graveolens) seeds in addressing gout, rheumatism, and arthritis due to its ability to flush waste from inflamed joints and kidneys (Chevallier, 2016). Very recent mice model studies have shown that aqueous extracts of parsley can reduce the effects of hyperuricemia—which causes gout and inflammatory arthritis. This appears to be due to the herb’s anti-inflammatory action: parsley is very high in polyphenols and is antioxidant (Soliman et al., 2020).

Myristicin and apiol, largely found in the volatile oil from the seeds but also in parsley root and leaf, are deemed responsible for its antioxidant action (Farzaei et al., 2013). And it is perhaps due in part to parsley’s antioxidant action (along with its vitamin C and quercetin content) that a less prominent historical indication of parsley for coughs, bronchitis, asthma, and other lung issues exists. Many herbalists also regard parsley as spasmolytic or relaxant, particularly the seed (Holmes, 2006). Although these actions are generally connected to the digestive or urinary systems, Hildegard von Bingen (1155/2001) also mentioned it in a recipe for paralysis, which may be another expression for muscular cramping.

As with a great many herbs, parsley has come under recent scrutiny in cancer studies, and we may look forward to its evolution in these and other realms of medical research. Its affinity for addressing tumor growth, particularly in breast tissue, also proves a possibly ancient one: the leaves of apium were used in cataplasm (poultice) form to disperse hard breast tumors (Pliny, 77/1906). Recent scientific inquiry has also been investigating a connection between parsley and breast cancer. A study of five in vivo and in vitro cases considered how apigenin’s anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and anticarcinogenic properties could be therapeutic against breast cancer (Nabavi et al., 2015). A couple of years later, the phytoestrogens in parsley root extract were examined in relation to cytotoxicity, metabolic activity, and performance of DNA synthesis in both benign and malignant breast cells and found to exert antiproliferative effects (Schröder et al., 2017).

Energetically, parsley root is warming and moistening, seed is drying, and leaf is neutral.

Adult Dose

Tincture: 2-5 mL root (1:2, 40%); 1-4 mL seed (1:2, 40%) (Holmes, 2006); 1-2 mL dried herb (1:5, 40%) 3x/day (Hoffmann, 2003).

Infusion: 4-10 g seed; 6-14 g dried root in short decoction or long cold infusion (Holmes, 2006); 4 fl oz (½ teaspoon dried herb in 8 fl oz water) 3x/day (Winston, 2003).

Safety

Not for use in pregnancy; as with other members of the carrot family, parsley can contribute to uterine contractions and the seed is used as an abortifacient (Wood, 2008). Usually both seed and root are contraindicated in pregnancy due to their emmenagogic and uterine stimulant nature (Brinker, 2010) but some herbalists advocate avoiding the leaves as well (Barker, 2001).

Herbalist David Winston (2003) suggests using parsley for infant colic through breastmilk, but parsley is also known to dry milk, hence others remark that the herb is to be generally avoided in breastfeeding (Barker, 2001).

If the oleoresin of the seed is distilled and ingested, it can apparently cause a range of serious effects, such as paralysis, dizziness, fainting, and fatty degeneration of the kidneys and liver (Grieve, 1931). Parsley should not be used in cases of allergic hypersensitivity (Brinker, 2010); parsley can cause allergic reactions (Hoffmann, 2003). The essential oil is a photosensitizer (Hoffmann, 2003), for which effect its furocoumarins seem to be responsible (Farzaei et al., 2013).

Parsley seed and root are furthermore contraindicated in kidney inflammation, as the volatile oil constituents can cause epithelial irritations. It should not be used in instances of edema linked to kidney or heart disorders due to poor excretion of urinary salt (Brinker, 2010).
