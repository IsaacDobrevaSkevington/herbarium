Native To

It is considered native to Africa, specifically including the following countries: Angola, Benin, Burundi, Cameroon, Central African Republic, Ethiopia, Gabon, Gambia, Ghana, Guinea, Guinea-Bissau, Kenya, Madagascar, Mozambique, Niger, Senegal, Sierra Leone, Somalia, South Africa, Sudan, Tanzania, Togo, Uganda, Zambia, and Zimbabwe. It is also likely native to India in the state of Andhra Pradesh (Rojas-Sandoval, 2022) and south-central and southeast China (Royal Botanic Gardens Kew, n.d.).

Geographic Distribution

Cultivation of this plant in tropical regions has led to its widespread geographic distribution. It can be found in other countries in Africa, Australia, humid and sub-humid parts of Asia, the West Indies, and certain Pacific islands. This plant is also now located in southern parts of the United States, Mexico, Central America, and many countries of South America and the Middle East (Rojas-Sandoval, 2022).

Botanical Description

Butterfly pea is a perennial, leguminous herb which grows as a creeper plant spreading out horizontally and low to the ground. The Latin genus of this plant comes from its appearance resembling that of female genitalia and the species name originates from its ternatin content, which is responsible for its natural vibrant color. This plant likes to grow in full sunlight or partially shaded areas and prefers a wide soil pH type, ranging from 5.5-8.9 (Jeyaraj et al., 2020; Gupta et al., 2010). Its flower and corolla are considered papilionaceous or butterfly-shaped, giving it its common name (Biyoshi & Geetha, 2012).

Its flowers are zygomorphic, resembling a pea shape, and come in groups of fives (Oguis et al., 2019). Their petals can range in color from light to dark blue, white, and purple (Jeyaraj et al., 2020). Each calyx is tubular and contains five sepals, which stay connected for two thirds of their length. Each corolla of the plant contains five free petals with one large standard petal with a large round banner, two smaller wing petals, and two white keel petals (Oguis et al., 2019; Biyoshi & Geetha, 2012), which help to protect the floral organs for germination. The keel petals help to protect the floral organs for germination.  The pistil of the plant is long with a curved end at the distal aspect and contains a single carpel; the ovary contains a single compartment in which 10 ovules are arranged. The stamens each contain 10 filaments which can be separated into one group with nine connected together and one free (Oguis et al., 2019; Biyoshi & Geetha, 2012). Each of the 10 filaments contain white anthers with pollen and can be further divided into four lobes (Pullaiah, 2000).

The pinnate leaves of butterfly pea each contain 5-7 leaflets which are both elliptic and oblong in shape. The leaves are around 2-3.2 cm in width and 2.5-5 cm in length (Jeyaraj et al., 2020). 

Butterfly pea seeds are around 4.5-7 mm in length and 3-4 mm in width (Jeyaraj et al., 2020).  Seeds of this plant are oval in shape with color ranging between black and yellow brown.  The root of C. ternatea is considered to contain an elaborate and deep taproot system. Multiple, thin roots jut horizontally away from the taproot. The extensive nature of the root system allows it to survive up to 8 months in drought, giving this plant its durability. The roots also contain nodules that contain nitrogen-fixing bacteria, contributing to the plant’s ability to convert nitrogen gas into a more usable form (Oguis et al., 2019).

Roots: alkaloids, ternatins, flavonoids (Gupta et al., 2010) including quercetin and myricetin glycosides (Kazuma et al., 2003), saponins, starch, tannins, carbohydrates, taraxerol and taraxerone, resin, proteins (Gupta et al., 2010)

Sustainability

This plant is hardy and typically grows well with other plants, though it may be difficult to propagate due to its low seed germination rate (Oguis et al., 2019). This challenge can often be overcome by storing seeds for 6 months, which increases germination to 15-20%, or by chemical scarification in which the seeds are soaked in either boiling water or sulfuric acid for at least 10 minutes which increases germination to 100% (Mullick & Chatterji, 1967; Makasana et al., 2016).

Harvesting Guidelines

Butterfly pea blooms in the early summer through the fall, and in tropical climates, may bloom throughout the entire year; harvesting of butterfly pea flowers is best done when they have freshly bloomed or shortly after their bloom, when the petals have just begun to dry. For those seeking to use C. ternatea for livestock food, the recommended time to harvest this plant is at 45 days of growth, as this is when the dry matter content seems to be at peak levels (Mahala et al., 2012).  According to ruminant models, at between 50 and 70 days of growth makes the best time to harvest for slow digestion of carbohydrates from this plant, being the fibrous parts, as opposed to fast digestion of the plant, the non-fibrous parts, though leaves are not as affected by the aging process (Abreu et al., 2014). Pruning butterfly pea every 42 days also helps increase dry matter content (Colina et al., 1997). Cultivating this herb with irrigation helps improve the quality of its nutritional content (Abreu et al., 2014).

Uses

Butterfly pea is an ornate, bold-colored plant with diverse herbal benefits and much cultural significance. Interestingly, though often referred to as blue pea or thought of as having the bold blue color, the plant containing the white flower is considered to have stronger effects (Mukherjee et al., 2008). In several Asian cultures, this plant symbolizes transformation and change as well as beauty. In ayurvedic medicine, this plant is highly regarded. Some traditions include utilizing it in spiritual events as it is thought to help bridge a connection to a higher power. In transformation rituals, many make use of its color changing properties by first focusing on where they are in life, what they have learned, what they are grateful for, and what they’d like to change and then squeezing citrus juice, such as from a lemon or lime, into the butterfly pea drink, thus changing its color with the pH change. The transformation aspect of this plant is also highlighted by its name, as butterflies are often symbols of metamorphosis. This plant is considered to possess energetics for protection, purification, and emotional health, all of which speak to its scientific properties of antioxidative, alterative, and mood supportive qualities. 

Given their vibrant blue color, the flowers of this plant have traditionally been used as a dye; this aspect of the plant can be attributed to its ternatin anthocyanin content (Saito et. al., 1985). Utilization of a natural alternative to synthetic dyes may be beneficial as synthetic dyes can have negative impacts on health as well as increased hyperactivity in children (Chusak et al., 2018; McCann et al., 2007). Some also note that butterfly pea leaves may also be used as a natural dye color to elicit green (Mukherjee et al., 2008). This plant is not only a natural alternative to dye but given that its color will change as the food quality diminishes over time, butterfly pea flowers serve as good criteria for detecting spoilage in foods that contain anthocyanin (Hasanah et al., 2023).  

Many cultures in India have been using butterfly pea traditionally for years, according to researcher and pharmaceutical chemist Dr. Girish Kumar Gupta (2010). In Bangladesh, the leaves and roots are used as a support for urinary tract infections by boiling the plant in water and drinking the decoction for 7 days (Gupta et al., 2010). In the central Indian state of Chhattisgarh the seeds of this plant can be crushed and taken with water to be used as a purgative for an upset stomach, while in the southwestern state of Karnataka, the juice of the root is used to help bring down a high fever. Those in Assam, northeastern India, may use the juice of the leaves as a poultice and topical application to help aid a headache or swelling of glands. People in the coastal region of Kodiakkarai use a paste derived from the plant’s flowers to address infections of the eyes or headaches. In southern India in Tamil-nadu, this plant is commonly used for its anti-viral properties (Gupta et al., 2010).  

Culturally, this plant can be found in South Asian dishes, often used as a dye for desserts and savory foods, such as in the production of blue rice. To make this unique dish, dried butterfly pea petals are extracted with water; the extract is then incorporated into fresh water which is then set to boil with rice added to cook (Maneechot et al., 2023). In Malaysian cuisine, this is called nasi kerabu and is paired with an herbal lemonade also prepared with the butterfly pea flower extract (Yim, 2010). In Malay and Peranakan culture, this herb may be incorporated in pulut inti (a dessert coconut rice), kuih tekan (glutinous rice cake), pulut tai tai (glutinous rice cake), and nyonya zhang (rice dumplings) (Yim, 2010). In Thailand, butterfly pea is made into a blue drink served alongside lime juice and pandan syrup. Those in Burma enjoy this plant simply dipped in batter and fried (Yim, 2010). In India and the Philippines, butterfly pea is often eaten just as is, for its nutritious content as a raw vegetable. Butterfly pea flowers can also be incorporated as a garnish in salads (Yim, 2010).

The longevity tonic effects of C. ternatea have been harnessed by many cultures throughout the years. Many Asian countries have been using this plant for the preservation of youthful skin through supporting a healthy complexion and fighting oxidative skin damage over time. This was supported in an in vitro study which demonstrated that the antioxidative nature of this herb fights ultraviolet induced mitochondrial deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) damage in human skin cells (Zakaria et al., 2018). In a study evaluating the effect of several ayurvedic herbal extracts on skin health, C. ternatea demonstrated ability to inhibit collagenase, the enzyme that breaks collagen down,  thereby keeping skin integrity intact and contributing to firmer skin texture. Moreover, C. ternatea demonstrated the ability to inhibit lipoxygenase, an enzyme that modulates inflammation in the epidermis and dermis; one study also found that this herb significantly decreases the intracellular level of free radicals (Zagórska-Dziok et al., 2021). This finding, along with the herb’s anti-inflammatory, antioxidative, and antimicrobial effects which may help maintain a proper skin microbiome, may help contribute to the restorative effects of this herb (Zagórska-Dziok et al., 2021).

Butterfly pea is considered a nootropic with various positive effects on cognitive function, at least in animal models (Oguis et al., 2019).  In Ayurveda, C. ternatea is a classic for easing stress, lightening depression, and gently sedating, demonstrating some of its mood-boosting effects (Mukherjee et al., 2008). It is one of the herbs included in the ancient ayurvedic formula shankhpushpi which acts as a brain tonic, nervine, and laxative. Not only does C. ternatea support the central nervous system and enhance mood, but it is considered to support memory function and intellect as well (Gupta et al., 2010). In a study of the effect of methanolic extract of C. ternatea on the central nervous system, this herb was found to reduce the intensity of behavior mediated by serotonin and acetylcholine. Though this was a murine study, anxiolytic, antidepressant, and antistress properties were demonstrated (Jain et al., 2003).

Butterfly pea has antimicrobial, antiparasitic, antifungal, insecticidal, larvicidal, and anthelmintic properties. Extract of the leaves and roots has been shown to demonstrate toxicity against the larvae, eggs, or live organisms of nematodes, roundworms, and earthworms (Hasan & Jain, 1985; Kumari & Devi, 2013; Gilding et al., 2015; Khadatkar et al., 2008; Salhan et al., 2011). Its insecticidal properties are active against multiple weevil species and the corn earworm (Kelemu et al., 2004; Poth et al., 2011a; Mensah et al., 2015). C. ternatea flower and leaf demonstrates antibacterial properties against multiple organisms including but not limited to Staphylococcus aureus, Streptococcus pyogenes, and Xanthomonas axonopodis (Shahid et al., 2009; Anthika et al., 2015; Kelemu et al., 2004). Butterfly pea leaf demonstrates broad spectrum antifungal effects against those in the Aspergillus, Candida, and Cryptococcus families, to name a few (Oguis et al., 2019).

This plant has diverse immune supportive properties including an ability to resolve fever, address inflammation, and ameliorate pain (More & Hake, 2019) and topically, these anti-inflammatory properties have been harnessed by many cultures. In ayurvedic medicine, the leaves are used as a poultice for inflammation of the eyes, while in the Philippines, the seeds are used as a poultice for arthralgia and swollen joints (Burkill, 2002). Ayurveda also uses this herb to support joint pain as well as for infections. It should be noted that for this purpose, as well as for its antidotal effect to animal stings and bites, the roots and leaves are used as opposed to the flower..  

The immunological benefits of butterfly pea continue into the oncology field. In vitro studies of both petroleum ether and ethanolic extract of the flower demonstrated dose-dependent cell toxicity (More & Hake, 2019).  Much of the anticancer activity of this herb is thought to be due to its ability to inhibit nuclear factor kappa beta, thereby helping to stave off metastasis. This antimetastatic activity was demonstrated in a 96% ethanolic extract of butterfly pea leaf against the MCF-7 HER2-positive breast cancer cell line in vitro (Asysyifa et al., 2020). Further studies evaluating crude methanol extract of this plant on human breast cancer cell lines also found C. ternatea to exhibit strong cytotoxicity, specifically against MDA-MB-231 (Akter et al., 2013). It should be noted that the MCF-7 cell line is the most commonly studied cell line in the world with an overexpression of human epidermal growth factor receptor while MDA-MB-231 comes from cells with triple negative breast cancer (Lee et al., 2015; Chavez et al., 2011). Other anticancer effects of this herb credit its taraxerol and taraxerone content of the root, which is known not solely for its actions as an anti-tumor agent but also for its ability to modulate inflammation and its role in addressing Alzheimer’s disease (Swain et al., 2012; Takasaki et al., 1999; Biswas et al., 2009; Ngo & Li, 2013).

Butterfly pea demonstrates notable anti-diabetic effects. A randomized crossover trial evaluated the efficacy of C. ternatea on plasma glycemic response 30 minutes after ingestion of sugary beverages. The results of this study indicated that postprandially, plasma glucose and insulin levels remained suppressed when the beverages were ingested with C. ternatea flower extract and that C. ternatea ingested alone did not change plasma glucose or insulin concentrations in a fasting state. These findings indicate that butterfly pea may be helpful in supporting insulin sensitivity and glucose pathways without a deleterious effect of hypoglycemia (Chusak et al., 2018). Moreover, the petals of this plant may be helpful in addressing diabetic complications. An in vitro study evaluated the effect of C. ternatea flower petal extracts on advanced glycation end products (Chayaratanasin et al., 2015). These glycation end products have been correlated with the worsening of age-related diseases. The results of the study indicated that this plant has strong antiglycation and antioxidant properties based on its phenolic, flavonoid, and anthocyanin content; these help to scavenge free radicals, reduce levels of fructosamine, reduce protein oxidation, and inhibit the formation of advanced glycation end products in a dose-dependent manner (Chayaratanasin et al., 2015). Based on this, C. ternatea may pose as a good adjunctive for both lessening the likelihood of developing diabetes and addressing later stage diabetic complications.

More than just acting on blood sugar regulation, butterfly pea possesses other beneficial actions on metabolic and digestive health. A study investigating the effect of C. ternatea extract on alloxan-induced diabetic rats discovered both antihyperglycemic and antihyperlipidemic effects from this plant. Moreover, the team found that C. ternatea inhibits the process of lipid cell production and accumulation in vitro, which suggests implications for combatting metabolic syndrome (Chayaratanasin et al., 2019). Though also a murine study, scientists found that C. ternatea improved cardiovascular dysfunction and oxidative stress in hypertensive rats with nitric oxide-deficiency. This, paired with C. ternatea’s anti-platelet-aggregation properties suggest that this herb may be helpful in metabolic health and supporting cardiovascular wellness (Maneesai et al., 2021).   

C. ternatea demonstrates promise in supporting urogenital health, though further research is needed. Ethanolic extract of the dried whole root demonstrates diuretic activity, a finding supported by both intravenous and oral administration (Gupta et al., 2010). Because of its strong antimicrobial effects, this herb may help support urinary tract infections (Sathyanarayana et al., 2024). Studies show that it may work on the kidneys as well. In an in vitro murine study, C. ternatea forestalled kidney injury and dysfunction (Saengnak et al., 2021). Regarding the reproductive system, this herb has been found to be benign to the male sex organs. In fact, in another murine in vitro study, C. ternatea demonstrated protective effects against testicular damage, primarily due to its antioxidant activity (Iamsaard et al., 2014). Similar results were found for female reproductive health and fertility. In yet another murine study, C. ternatea extract was protective against damage induced by bisphenol-A (BPA) on reproductive performance and demonstrated no negative effects on maternal body weight (Goh et al., 2022). 

This plant has a broad range of functions, including its use in agriculture. Butterfly pea can be applied as a fodder for feeding cattle and other livestock (Oguis et al., 2019). This flower is so palatable that it is often a preferred choice for livestock as compared to other crops. Butterfly pea is a quite nutritious legume in composition, making it a nourishing choice for farm animals.  Its botanical characteristics, including having a slender stem with large leaves, paired with its safety profile, make it an ideal choice as a source for haymaking as well as for cultivating barren fields into crops (Gomez & Kalamani, 2003). This plant also serves as a great option for grazing rotation, cover cropping with nitrogen fixation, green manure, or as an ornamental garden plant (Mukherjee et al., 2008).

C. ternatea poses multiple options as a beneficial herb, food choice for humans or livestock, or even dye plant. Its neuroprotective properties are numerous with implications for mood balancing, memory support, boosting cognitive well being and combating Alzheimer’s disease. This herb is cardioprotective, with implications of supporting those with hypertension and improving metabolism through glucose optimization, insulin sensitization, and modulation of lipid accumulation. As a natural antioxidant, it supports skin health and fights the oxidative damage of aging throughout the body. It supports optimal microbiota from diverse bacterial species to fungi and parasites. In regards to immunity, this herb may be used orally or topically to combat inflammation, including oncological, and help decrease pain. While more research is needed to elucidate its efficacy on human health, its safety profile and long use in ayurvedic and other traditions is promising.

Adult Dose

Infusion: add (1 tsp or 4g of dried butterfly pea flowers to 1 cup of hot water.  Let steep for 10-15 minutes. Strain dried flowers out of tea. Drink hot or over ice, cold. Note: due to the acidity content, adding citrus fruits can change the color of the tea.

Syrup: warm ½ cup of water over low heat. Add 3 tablespoons of butterfly pea flower or 1 tablespoon of butterfly pea flower powder. Bring to a boil, then stir in up to ½ cup of raw cane sugar. Allow to steep for 20 minutes. Strain the liquid.

Poultice/topical: gather butterfly pea leaves or seed. Make sure to clean off any dirt or insects and to wash the plant part. Mash until made into a paste, adding lukewarm water as needed. This may be done with a pestle. Once paste is complete, spread onto a muslin cloth, ensuring there is enough paste to cover the entire affected area. Avoid open wounds. Leave on for up to 20-30 minutes, then rinse off. Repeat as needed.

Safety

This plant is generally considered safe to consume for animals and people in moderation. Aqueous extract of the flower petals has demonstrated no cytotoxicity in human fibroblast cells and instead has demonstrated protective effects in human erythrocytes (Vidana Gamage et al., 2021). Mild gastrointestinal distress can occur in some individuals who ingest butterfly pea with symptoms such as nausea, diarrhea, and stomach pain due primarily to the oil content; it should be noted that these are anecdotal reports in nature, often involving animal studies as opposed to human trials, and this herb is often utilized for addressing gastrointestinal system complaints and as an antiulcer agent (Kovitvadhi et al., 2024; More & Hake, 2019).
