Rehmannia

Rehmannia glutinosa; syn. Chirita chanetii, Rehmannia chinensis, Gerardia glutinosa, Digitalis glutinosa

Native To

This herb is considered native to Eastern Asia including China, Korea, and Japan (Park et al., 2004).

Geographic Distribution

This plant is native to and distributed throughout many provinces of China including Henan, Shandong, Shanxi, Shaanxi, Hebei, Lioning, Jiangsu, Zhejiang, Hunan, Hubei, and Sichuan. It is also distributed in several inner states of Mongolia (Zhang et al., 2008). R. glutinosa can be found in Korea and Vietnam (Park et al., 2004; Thanh Loan & Thi Le Thu, 2023). While its current distribution is mainly found where it is cultivated, it can be found growing wild in mountainous slopes and trails between sea level and 1100 meters in temperate conditions (Zhang et al., 2008).

Botanical Description

Rehmannia is a herbaceous, rhizomatous perennial that grows upwards of 30 cm (Royal Horticultural Society, n.d.), blooming from late spring to early summer. Botanists describe the flower calyx as bell-shaped with the corolla approximately 4.5 cm in length (Li et al., 2022). R. glutinosa produces both reddish-brown and pale pink two-lipped flowers with small clusters near the stem and a reddish or yellowish purple hue located in the anther or throat of the petals (Royal Horticultural Society, n.d.; Li et al., 2022).

The leaves of R. glutinosa grow in basal rosettes. The resinous nature of the plant extends to the leaves which grow to about 11 cm and contain fine, soft hairs (Missouri Botanical Garden, n.d.; Royal Horticultural Society, n.d.). The structure of the leaves ranges from ovate to oblong with a concave vein form (Li et al., 2022).

The roots and root tubers of R. glutinosa also contain the viscous property of this plant (Zhang et al., 2008). The majority of the beneficial properties of rehmannia come from the large roots and rhizome which is fleshy, yellow when fresh, and stout in nature (Missouri Botanical Garden, n.d.; Li et al., 2022).

Key Constituents

Aerial parts: (+)-(7S, 8S, 8’S)-9-O-[β-D-glucopyranoyl] asarininone, 2α,3β,19α,23-tetrahydroxy-olean-12-en-28-oic acid, 7,3′-dihydroxyl-5′-methoxyisoflavone, aeginetic acid, catalpol, corchori fatty acid B, pinellic acid (Lei et al., 2015).

Rhizome: catalpol, D-mannitol, sucrose (Kitagawa et al., 1971). 

Root (tuber): adenosine, amino acids, alkaloids, lecithin, organic acids, phosphoric acid, sterols, trace elements, vitamin A (Shen-Nong Limited, 2002), beta-sitosterol, daucosterol, fatty acids, palmitic acid, sulfur cyclic compounds (Ni et al., 1992), glycosides including iridoid glycosides such as 6-O-E-feruloyl ajugol, acetoside, aucubin, catalpol, cistanosides A and F, echinacoside, glutinoside, jioglutoside A-B, jionosides A1 and B1, leonoride, mellitoside, purpureaside C, rehmaglutins A-D, rehmannioside A-D (Zhang et al., 2020; Shen-Nong Limited, 2002). 

Whole plant: amino acids, dihydrocatalpol, flavonoids, inorganic ions, lignans, phenol glycoside ionine, terpenoids (Zhang et al., 2008), chromones (Zhang et al., 2021), cyclopentanoid monoterpenes (Zhou et al., 2022), iridoid glycosides (catalpol, aucuboside, ajugol, geniposide) (Zhang et al., 2020; Jia et al., 2023; Zhou et al., 2019), iridoids, unsaturated fatty acids (Jia et al., 2023), phenylethanol glycosides (aceteoside), tyrosine decarboxylase (Li et al., 2022), polysaccharides (Huang et al., 2024), saccharides including polysaccharides, oligosaccharides (stachyose), monosaccharide (Jia et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2008), rehmannioside A (Yang et al., 2022).

Sustainability

Rehmannia poses sustainability issues for several reasons. Replanting proves difficult due to its nature of accumulating metabolites that inhibit growth. The allelochemicals produced by R. glutinosa lead to degradation of soil richness and disturbance of a harmonious microbial soil environment. These metabolites include phenolic acid which, as it accumulates, creates an environment for pathogens such as fungi and root-knot nematodes to invade the root; these factors can impede the ability of R. glutinosa to be replanted for 8-10 years (Gan et al., 2025). 

To combat this, scientists recommend understanding and implementing specific crop rotation patterns and the introduction of rhizobacteria that upregulate plant growth. Further recommendations include soil disinfection techniques to omit pathogens, mulching, flooding, adding organic agents to the soil, and fumigation for eradication of pests. It should be noted that attempts to mitigate the continuous cropping obstacles encountered with R. glutinosa, such as pesticide use and chemical fertilizer, may result in other sustainability/environmental concerns such as increased pollution (Gan et al., 2025).

Harvesting Guidelines

Cultivation of rehmannia is typically conducted using rootstock as opposed to growing from seeds (Kim et al., 2020). If seed propagation is conducted, then it is recommended to sow in the autumn or spring, and preferably in a greenhouse to help control the environment given hardships with R. glutinosa seed starting (Brickell, 1990). Once seedlings are large enough to warrant their own containers, they are transplanted outside, typically around late spring or early summer. To obtain cuttings, choose the winter for roots and the late spring or early summer for basal cuttings with shoots at least 8-10 cm above ground (Huxley, 1992). 

Pruning is not required for rehmannia, and it grows best in well-drained soil and an acidic or neutral pH (Royal Horticultural Society, n.d.). Rehmannia requires a moderate amount of water and grows best when exposed to full sun in plant hardiness zones 7-10 (Missouri Botanical Garden, n.d.). Aside from some pest susceptibility, mainly slugs and snails, R. glutinosa remains mostly disease-free (Royal Horticultural Society, n.d.).

Uses

Rehmannia glutinosa is named after the German physician and botanist Joseph Rehmann, hence the genus, and its resinous nature, hence the species (Zhang et al., 2008). Its common name, Chinese foxglove, derives from its similarity to foxglove, a plant from the Digitalis genus which often appears with dramatic racemes of colorful, tubular flowers of similar shape to rehmannia and to the fingers of gloves (Missouri Botanical Garden, n.d.).

R. glutinosa remains one of the most common herbs in Chinese medicine. Considered to have first been recorded in its tuber root form from the Shennong’s Classic of Materia Medica, this herb is often used for conditions involving yin and/or Blood disorders (Li et al., 2022).

Understanding the core concepts of Chinese medicine, such as yin/yang and the five elements, helps to shed light on the role rehmannia plays as a balancing herb. R. rehmannia can be found in the Clear Heat and Tonify (strengthen) yin categories (Bensky et al., 2004) for supporting disharmony based on bodily patterns. In the Korean Herbal Apothecary, rehmannia is classed as a Water herb for supporting the Bladder and Kidney and aiding a Lesser yang constitution type (Yoon, 2023). It is typically used in synergistic herbal formulas with other botanicals, and may be paired with Chinese medicine therapies such as acupuncture, cupping, tai chi, and qi gong.

Sheng di huang, the pharmaceutical name of which is rehmannia radix, or R. glutinosa rhizome, is in the Clear Heat category of Chinese herbs. Sheng di huang is also considered to enrich yin and cool the Blood. In its unprepared form, this herb is considered sweet, bitter, and cold and enters the Heart, Kidney, and Liver meridians. Indications for this herb would be heat signs such as thirst, a red tongue, fever, sore throat, constipation, and hemorrhage or hematemesis which may indicate Heat in the Blood, though again it should be noted that Chinese medicine uses patterns to base herbal formulas upon as opposed to conditions or symptoms (Bensky et al., 2004; Jia et al., 2023). This speaks to rehmannia’s herbal action as a hemostatic agent (Zhang et al., 2008). Rehmannia is also useful in regulating the menstrual cycle, especially one characterized by heavy bleeding (Chevallier, 2000). 

Shu di huang, the pharmaceutical name of which is rehmanniae radix preparata, or prepared R. glutinosa root, exists in the Tonify Yin category of Chinese herbs. In its prepared form, this herb has been cooked before use, which changes its energetics to slightly warm and sweet. The root tubers are steamed in rice wine until black and saturated with moisture and then dried for nine cycles in the sun; this version of rehmannia also enters the Heart, Kidney, and Liver meridians and in addition to its main action, is able to Augment the Marrow, Generate Essence, and Tonify Blood. Augmenting the Marrow refers to the ability to nourish the bone Marrow and all it supports such as Kidney function, brain health, and bone strength, and though not all Chinese medicine actions translate directly to a plant’s Western function, preliminary research indicates that R. glutinosa regulates bone marrow activity (Liu et al., 2023). Acupuncturist and herbalist Michael Tierra (n.d.) also notes that prepared rehmannia has the ability to stimulate the formation of red blood cells as well as immune cells in the bone marrow. It is thought to have the ability to both improve bone density and forestall bone loss, and has been demonstrated to do the same in animal studies (Cabrera, 2023). Indications for the prepared form of rehmannia, which contains less catalpol, the herb’s active ingredient, than the raw form (Wang et al., 1997, as cited in Zhang et al., 2008), include Blood Deficiency symptoms such as palpitations and insomnia, yin, notably Kidney yin, Deficiency symptoms such as dizziness, back pain, and tinnitus, and symptoms of Essence Deficiency such as hair graying, premature aging, and cognitive decline (Bensky et al., 2004).

While used in many Blood and Kidney Tonifying Chinese medicine formulas, rehmannia has also become an important blood builder for many Western herbalists and has long been touted for its positive effects on the metabolic system. Not only is rehmannia an antioxidant but a preliminary study from Ren et al. (2017) shows evidence that it can improve insulin resistance and support mitochondrial wellbeing. R. glutinosa as an aqueous extract demonstrates reversal of glucose dysregulation and oxidative stress and resolution of insulin resistance, and improvements from oxidative stress which may be partly due to its ability to reverse oxidative stress (Ren et al., 2017). Rehmannia is an antiobesity type herb, and a study found that Korean women who took rehmannia for 2 weeks experienced a significant decrease of waist circumference; this may partly be due to its effect on the microbiome, given the evaluation of the fecal samples post-intervention as compared to pre-intervention (Han et al., 2015). Another study by Li et al. (2024) investigated rats and the effect of catalpol on those with induced type 2 diabetes mellitus. Catalpol demonstrated potential both as a hypoglycemic agent and for ameliorating lipid profiles in those with type 2 diabetes, and may do so partly via immunomodulation of the AMPK (adenosine 5’-monophosphate-activated protein kinase) signaling mechanism (Li et al., 2024). A Chinese formula called Rehmannia Six Formula (Liu Wei Di Huang Wan), of which R. glutinosa is the chief herb, is commonly used for those with diabetes; moreover, its additional actions as a neuroprotective and nephroprotective agent help support common complications of diabetes (Poon et al., 2011; Dai et al., 2018). 

R. glutinosa is valued for its profound benefits on mental and emotional wellbeing and ability to regulate the stress response. Herbalist Chanchal Cabrera (2023) refers to rehmannia as an adaptogen with a trophorestorative effect on the adrenals, supporting both their structure and function and optimizing stress hormone levels, particularly in the context of chronic degenerative diseases and neuropathy, while James Duke (2002) classifies rehmannia as an adrenal stimulant. As an ethanolic extract, rehmannia has been found to act as an antidepressant in preliminary studies. Specifically when looking at chronic, unpredictable mild stress (CUMS), R. glutinosa ethanolic extract was found to inhibit depression-like behavior in affected rats; the study also found that rehmannia extract has mood and attention benefits through upregulating certain neurotransmitter pathways and acting on gene expression and proteins such as brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) (Wang et al., 2024). 

Chinese herbs are often combined for synergistic benefit. As an anxiolytic, R. glutinosa blends well with Lilii bulbus for their combined antidepressant and anti-inflammatory effects which have been found to be effective in addressing anxiety and depression, as well as other non-cognitive based conditions such as hypertension, cancer, and menopausal symptoms (Wang et al., 2024). A similar effect is observed when rehmannia is combined with Platycladus orientalis, a blend which demonstrated actions on nitric oxide synthase 3, epidermal growth factor, estrogen receptor 1, and mitogen-activated protein kinase 1, among others (Liu et al., 2021). 

R. glutinosa also supports the body as a neuroprotective agent including its antiapoptotic effects due to its catalpol content. A study investigating its use for assisting those with spinal cord injuries found that the catalpol protected neurons and reduced stress in the endoplasmic reticulum, supporting rehmannia’s role in neurodegenerative diseases (Huang et al., 2022). Using a steam preparation of the root of R. glutinosa elicits neuroprotective effects on both learning and memory. In a preliminary study, R. glutinosa demonstrated positive effects on cognitive function and acted as an anti-inflammatory agent on both cholinergic communication and in the hippocampus (Lee et al., 2011). In a human cell trial, R. glutinosa, in its Chinese prepared form, was found to stimulate the gene expression of a growth factor that supports the production of dopaminergic neurons (Zhang et al., 2012); this is particularly important in the consideration of Parkinson’s disease and is further strengthened by a second human cell study showing a similar result and elucidating that this effect may happen via two independent gene expression pathways (Yu et al., 2006).

Rehmannia demonstrates value as an antiaging agent, a role supported by Chinese medicine theory as the herb enters the Kidney meridian which often controls many aspects of aging, and signs of premature aging are often considered a Kidney Deficiency (Bensky et al., 2004). A human cell study looking at adipose-derived mesenchymal, or adult-derived stem cells, showed promise of rehmannia as a potential regenerative agent; likely due to its ability to act on growth factors, this research shows its ability to support the proliferation of this cell type and decrease hydrogen peroxide-induced apoptosis (Wang et al., 2018). Initial studies have demonstrated its ability to lessen senescence as well as augment survival rate of cells (Bai et al., 2018). This has specifically been demonstrated with hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) with oral introduction of R. glutinosa. The study also resulted in decreased numbers of short-term HSCs and in turn increased numbers of long-term HSCs ability to self-renew (Wang et al., 2018). Part of its antiaging effect is credited to its antioxidative effects as R. glutinosa decreases reactive oxygen species and downregulation of p53 and p16, which are both associated with senescence in cells (Bai et al., 2018). Other studies support rehmannia’s use for aging-related diseases such as in the recovery from or forestalling of cerebral ischemic stroke (Yang et al., 2022). 

As mentioned previously, R. glutinosa displays anti-inflammatory properties, which are also shown to support the digestive system. A study explored the effect of fresh aqueous extract on mice with lipopolysaccharide-induced intestinal inflammation (Li et al., 2023). Researchers looked at three different doses administered over the course of 3 consecutive weeks, after which the results indicated that R. glutinosa oligosaccharides augmented the intestinal lining and decreased inflammatory cytokines, including interleukins and tumor necrosis factor alpha. Antioxidant levels improved as well (Li et al., 2023). A second study evaluated the effects of R. glutinosa polysaccharides on porcine cells, specifically inflammation of the intestines, and found similarly that this herb demonstrated inhibition of pro-inflammatory factors including toll-like receptor 4/nuclear factor kappa beta (Ren et al., 2023). It is its anti-inflammatory, as well as adaptogenic, effects that also make rehmannia a valued herb when addressing viral hepatitis (Hoffmann, 2003) and protecting and supporting a weak liver (Chevallier, 2000).

R. glutinosa as an immunostimulatory agent has been investigated for its potential anticancer properties. In vivo studies demonstrate its ability to act as a potential immunostimulatory agent in human dendritic cells including with the increase of proinflammatory cell signals (Wang et al., 2018). In Holistic Cancer Care, medical herbalist Chanchal Cabrera (2023) considers prepared rehmannia a deep immune tonic, nutritive, blood-building, restorative, and a herb to be taken in preparation for surgery. In a study in vivo by Xu et al., researchers tested the polysaccharide extract from R. glutinosa compared to that of Escherichia coli lipopolysaccharide. Results demonstrated that R. glutinosa polysaccharide increased natural killer (NK) cell numbers in the blood and production of NK cells in the spleen, and acted on other immune pathways such as lymph node involvement, interferon-gamma production, and gene expression (Xu et al., 2017). The study also showed increased cytotoxic activity against lymphoma cells (Xu et al., 2017; Wright & Bonavida, 1983). An in vitro study demonstrated rehmannia’s anticancer potential based on its polysaccharide’s anti-inflammatory properties as well as inhibition of survival of lung cancer cells (Lu et al., 2022). This preparation of R. glutinosa was made via a repeat steam and dry process with the ninth cycle of this demonstrating the strongest inhibitory activity on bacterial lipopolysaccharides. Other mechanisms of immune modulation and anti-inflammatory activity were also identified in this study (Lu et al., 2022). Other studies demonstrate catalpol’s antitumor effects on breast, bladder, colon, and ovarian cancer (Zhu et al., 2017). Chinese medicine practitioner Pauline Sok Yin Hwang (2022)  uses rehmannia in her oncology practice as a tonifying herb used for marrow suppression. Still, further human studies are needed to support rehmannia’s use in addressing cancer.

R. glutinosa is credited with cardioprotective properties given its catalpol content. In one preliminary study, scientists introduced rats to 30 minutes of myocardial ischemia and were then reperfused for 3 hours with either saline, catalpol, or catalpol with wortmannin (a fungal metabolite) (Huang et al., 2013). Results indicated that catalpol enhanced cardiac effects including reduction of myocardial infarction, apoptosis, and necrosis of cardiomyocytes (Huang et al., 2013). Other studies have demonstrated cardioprotective activity via rehmannia’s ability to inhibit cardiac hypertrophy in mice due to its geniposide content (Zhou et al., 2019).

R. glutinosa demonstrates antifibrotic activity. A study on idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis was conducted to better elucidate the effects of catalpol on this often fatal condition (Yang et al., 2021). The outcome of the study revealed that catalpol may bind to certain proteins and receptors that ultimately lead to a reduction of oxidative stress; additionally, catalpol showed the ability to inhibit the process by which epithelial cells turn into mesenchymal cells. This, combined with catalpol’s ability to act on collagen remodeling, led to less fibrosis and upregulation of proteins involved with better tissue integrity (Yang et al., 2021). Another study investigated the antifibrotic effects of R. glutinosa on renal fibrosis. Researchers discovered that the R. glutinosa in the root prepared form has the ability to inhibit renal fibrosis progressing via activation of regulating the miR-122-5p/PKM axis in the case of in vivo mouse cells (Liu et al., 2022).

Rehmannia’s effects extend to the endocrine and immune systems. Herbalist Kerry Bone (2003) uses rehmannia in his clinical practice primarily to offset the negative effects of corticosteroids on the immune and endocrine systems. In a study evaluating its effect on perimenopausal women with breast cancer using the Kupperman index as a way to quantify and assess their symptoms, those taking the Chinese herbal extract of R. glutinosa exhibited lower Kupperman indices; though a significant change in gonadal hormones was not demonstrated, there were no adverse effects and positive immunological findings were exhibited (Kong et al., 2025). Rehmannia’s action as a kidney nourisher supports the endocrine system’s production of the hormone erythropoietin, which in turn improves both levels of bone marrow stem cells and red blood cells (Cabrera, 2023). Furthermore, regarding rehmannia’s ability to support kidney function, a study evaluating 479 individuals with primary, chronic glomerulonephritis found that those given the angiotensin receptor blocker, irbesartan, and R. glutinosa exhibited a higher reduction of proteinuria than those treated with irbesartan alone (Qiu et al., 2013).

Considered an anodyne, R. glutinosa is valued for its ability to improve pain perception. Part of this may be due to R. glutinosa’s ability to both decrease inflammation on neuropeptide secretion and delay cartilage degradation, as demonstrated in a murine study (Huang et al., 2023). Other studies support this as well, citing rehmannia’s catalpol content as the primary cause. In a study conducted both in vivo and in vitro, rats given catalpol experienced inhibition of tissue inflammation, astrocyte activation, and increased inflammatory factor expression, which resulted in mitigation of their inflammatory pain (Zhao et al., 2022). The active constituent catalpol also exhibited a decrease in induced, mechanical allodynia and thermal hyperalgesia when given through intrathecal injection (Zhao et al., 2022). 

Given that the majority of the research for this herb is comprised of murine and in vitro studies, more research is needed to investigate its efficacy on humans.

Adult Dose

Decoction: The typically accepted dose for this herb in Chinese medicine is 9-30 g for shu di huang (the prepared form) or 9-15 g for sheng di huang (the unprepared form), divided into 2-3 doses, however it is always advised to consult with a herbalist regarding the proper use of this herb (Bensky et al., 2004; Tierra, 1988).

Tincture: 1-3 mL (1:5; 50%) 1-3x/day (Easley & Horne, 2016).

Safety

Rehmannia can result in adverse gastrointestinal effects such as diarrhea and stomach pain and Duke (2002) notes that both cooked and raw rehmannia are contraindicated with diarrhea, and the prepared root contraindicates with indigestion while the raw root is not advised when there is a lack of appetite. Because prepared rehmannia can be difficult to digest, it should be avoided in individuals with weak digestion. With high doses of either form of rehmannia, respiratory and cardiovascular adverse effects can occur such as dizziness, vertigo, palpitations, fatigue, and low blood pressure. Allergic reactions, including anaphylaxis, have also been reported with this herb in a few cases (Bensky et al., 2004). Herbalist Chanchal Cabrera (2023) suggests that the raw, unprepared version of rehmannia ought to be avoided due to potential for slight toxicity. 

The sweet taste profile and the harshness of this herb in an unprepared form can cause disruption of digestion and Middle Jiao or stagnation of qi, thus herbalists often pair this herb with another such as sha ren (Amomum villosum) to promote the movement of Qi and counter this effect (Bensky et al., 2004). In cases of Spleen Deficiency, Qi Stagnation, or Phlegm, rehmannia should be avoided to stop bloating and/or loose stools (Tierra, 2003). Addressing digestion first, before considering rehmannia, is advised, in addition to toning down rehmannia’s sweet and cooling actions by combining it with warming carminatives such as cardamom (Elettaria cardamomum) seed or ginger (Zingiber officinale) rhizome.

In regards to pregnancy, rehmannia is categorized as a B3: “No increase in the frequency of malformation or other harmful effects on the fetus from limited use in women. Evidence of increased fetal damage in animal studies exists, although the relevance to humans is unknown” though it may be taken while lactating (Mills & Bone, 2005, p. 552).
