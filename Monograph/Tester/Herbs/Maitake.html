Native To

Maitake is native to temperate hardwood regions of Northeast Japan, China, Europe, and the Northeastern United States (Halpern, 2007; Shen, 2001).

Geographic Distribution

Maitake has a circumpolar range in the Northern Hemisphere & appears in southwestern & northeastern China & northeast Japan with reports in Australia and New Zealand (The Global Fungal Red List Initiative, 2019; Chen et al., 2000). Maitake has been reported in the Western U.S. (Hobbs, 1986) and its growth has been boosted with spore capsules on Cherokee lands in Oklahoma (Chavez, 2009). It can be found in eastern Canada as well as throughout the Northeast & Mid-Atlantic U.S.

Botanical Description

Maitake is a perennial fungus that features undulating, overlapping shelves and flourishes on aging, dying, or deceased hardwood trees. Maitake often favors oak or beech as its host (Halpern, 2007) but may also be found growing on elm, larch, blackgum, or maple as well as trees of other species such as Pinus, Prunus, Castanea, or Pseudotsuga (Shen, 2001). The mushroom or fruiting body is white-brown or greyish in coloration and is frequently compared to a cauliflower in appearance (Winston & Kuhn, 2008). Maitake grows in clusters that can weigh around 30 pounds but also reach a weight of 80 pounds, and its overlapping caps tend to be 4-5 inches across (Halpern, 2007). It is a polypore mushroom, which means that it possesses tubes or pores rather than gills underneath the fruiting bodies.

Key Constituents

Fruiting body: polysaccharides (β-glucans D and MD, heteroglycans) (Halpern, 2007; Wu et al., 2021), unsaturated fatty acids (octadecenoic and octadecadienoic acids), proteins, glycoproteins, fiber, niacin, potassium, vitamin D, sterols, phenolic compounds, phosphatidylcholine, phosphatidylinositol, phosphatidylserine, and phosphatide acid (Hobbs, 1986; Stamets, 2005; Wu et al., 2021)

Mycelium: phosphatidylethanolamine

Sustainability

Maitake has been recorded by The Global Fungal Red List Initiative, which has assessed the mushroom largely for its European trends under their criterion A, that of “population reduction:” maitake displays a decline of 20% over the previous 50 years with the expectation of continued decline throughout the next 50 years and so and has been given a status of “near threatened” (The Global Fungal Red List, 2019).

It has also been generally suggested that because maitake grows on old, large, deciduous hardwood trees that there is also a global decline in its numbers stemming from deforestation and especially from large-scale logging of old-growth trees (The Global Fungal Red List, 2019).

Harvesting Guidelines

Maitake will grow in the same location for several years and can be found ready for harvesting in the autumn, often after rain. As the mushroom can absorb dirt and debris, it can sometimes be ideal to select smaller mushrooms rather than very large specimens (Bergo, 2013). To harvest, cut the primary stem of the mushroom just above the surface from which it is growing.

Uses

Grifola frondosa is valued as a particularly delicious mushroom and has been accordingly long prized for its use in the culinary arts. Its location was kept as a family secret both in Asia and—what is less known—among some Indigenous peoples of North America. In the past 30 years, scientific research has taken a closer look at maitake, particularly with a focus on how its polysaccharide constituents influence immunity, tumors, and blood sugar levels.

Maitake is known as the “dancing mushroom” in Japanese, mai– meaning “dance” and -take meaning “mushroom” (Halpern, 2007; He et al., 2018). The mushroom is often connected to dancing because it is said that those who came across maitake and picked the mushroom danced with jubilation over having been fortunate enough to find it. This might be plausible because maitake was historically a truly expensive commodity: in feudal times, maitake was offered as a gift to the shogun, and local lords are supposed to have offered silver equivalent to a specimen’s weight (Halpern, 2007). Another theory is that the overlapping caps of maitake resemble dancing butterflies (Halpern, 2007).

Grifola frondosa’s connection with dancing for joy may also be traced back to some etymological confusion in an 11th century CE collection of Japanese folktales known as the Konjaku monogatari (Tales from Long Ago), which references maitake in a tale of nuns and woodcutters in the Kitayama mountains, who indeed danced with ecstatic joy upon coming across the mushroom and partaking of it. This tale concludes with its author’s confused musing:

When we think about it this is a striking story. For even though we still have this kind of mushroom [the maitake] people who eat them do not dance. Thus this exceedingly strange story has been handed down (Sanford, 1972, quoting the Konjaku monogatrishū, vol. 5, pp. 96-97).

Further ethnographic research has demonstrated that this ancient version of the tale may erroneously reference maitake, when it is far more likely to have been the wairaitake mushroom (Pholiota spectabilis) that was responsible for the ecstatic dancing in the story (Sanford, 1972).

Maitake’s binomial genus, Grifola, is a bit of a mystery. Most writers on the mushroom assert without a clear reasoning that the name is a reference to the mythological creature known as a griffin (Halpern, 2007), a word which is traceable back to the ancient Greek noun γρύψ (grups) in fact meaning “griffin” (or a bearded vulture). Grifola could theoretically also be traced back to the ancient Greek masculine noun γρῖφος (griphos), a word that represents a fishing basket (and by extension also a riddle) but metaphorically was used to describe anything intricate, which the undulated surface of maitake certainly is. Maitake’s second botanical name, frondosa, is a direct form of the Latin adjective for “leafy” or “full of leaves,” doubtless a reference to maitake’s layers.

The layers of the mushroom have also been compared to the fluffed-up feathers of a bird; another of its common names, “hen-of-the-woods,” may in part refer to this appearance. Maitake mushrooms may grow to enormous sizes, hence the common name “king of the mushrooms.” Some folklore appears to indicate that the mushrooms prefer to grow where lightning has left its mark upon a tree (Halpern, 2007). For ages, the only way to obtain maitake in Asia was to find it in the wild, and its foragers then closely guarded their secret harvesting places, often keeping mum until disclosing the location to their sons only upon one’s deathbed (Halpern, 2007).

The taste of maitake has been compared to that of eggplant (Rogers, 2012). Many folks commonly attest to its delicious, meaty texture. In addition to its primary constituent of polysaccharides, maitake possesses fatty acids; offers a reasonable amount of fiber and protein; and grants, via a 2-handful serving, 87% percent of one’s daily intake of niacin, and a whopping 464% of potassium (Stamets, 2005). If left exposed to sunlight after picking for the duration of 6-8 hours, the mushroom’s vitamin D content rises from 460 IU up to 31,900 IU per one hundred grams (Rogers, 2012; Stamets, 2005).

By the 1970s, growing maitake via artificial cultivation was achieved and commercial production has recently been estimated at about 50,000 tons worldwide (Halpern, 2007). There may be differences in chemical constituents depending on how maitake has been grown. For example, the vitamin D content may also be much higher in those mushrooms grown in sawdust rather than on a log (Tabata et al., 2004).

In Japan, maitake is used traditionally as more than just a tasty food to support the immune system, addressing high blood pressure and cancer (Halpern, 2007). In the Japanese herbal system known as Kampo, the mushroom has been used to support a range of issues as well as to serve as a strengthening tonic for overall health maintenance (Babel, 2011).

About 2000 years ago, maitake was utilized in Chinese medicine and referred to as Keisho, a preparation which can be traced back to Shen Nong’s Herbal (or the Shen Nong Ben Cao Jing, one of the earliest Chinese medical texts of the first or second century CE) and was used for improving the health of Stomach and Spleen and possessing indications for soothing nerves and hemorrhoids (Shen, 2001). In Chinese medicine today, maitake is often used in relation to the tropisms of Kidney, Lung, and Large Intestine along with a sustained history of calming the mind (for this last indication, there isn’t much research out there to connect traditional uses with the nervous system, but some animal studies have begun demonstrating the antidepressive effects of maitake’s polysaccharides (Bao et al., 2016)).

Maitake is sometimes considered by Chinese medicine to be the most cleansing functional mushroom available, especially when it comes to directly working with the liver and the lungs for detoxification of the body (Belleme & Belleme, 2007). Note that another species, however, is very often used in Chinese medicine and confused with maitake, the polyporus mushroom Grifola umbellata/Polyporus umbellatus, which differs slightly in form, color and spores as well as in indications, history, and research (Shen, 2001).

Grifola frondosa was first described in the global West around 1785 in Britain, and although maitake is morphologically similar throughout its range, it is now suggested that there may be species differences—if followed up and established with research, different names would need to be eventually given to North American and Asian populations of the mushroom for differentiation (The Global Fungal Red List Initiative, 2019).

On the other side of the Atlantic, some native North American tribes make great use of mushrooms including maitake. While maitake has long been used as a beverage, food, for its health benefits, and in sacred and ceremonial contexts (Kavasch & Baar, 1999), little to no extant ethnographic data discusses its historical and tribal uses. One author of Cherokee, Creek, and Powhatan descent remarks that “Our ancestors simply ate it and saved it for reliable winter uses, as it is a tasty meat substitute” (Kavasch, 2013, p. 83).

The Cherokee nation, however, calls maitake wishi or wissy and some members consider the mushrooms—which grow on the tribal lands in Oklahoma, often at the base of red oak trees—a delicacy (Chavez, 2009). The tribe recently purchased plugs to artificially increase production of maitake on tribal lands, and one individual undertaking the experiment also remarked that:

Several years ago a number of elders had wishi trees, and with wishi trees the locations are kept secret and passed down to their family. I was lucky to have a few trees passed down to me. A lot of those trees have died,’ he said. ‘Several years of drought have wreaked havoc on the wishi population. What we’re really trying is get those numbers back up (Chavez, 2009, para. 12).

Thus in the American West among Indigenous peoples, the location of the mushroom is historically a closely guarded family secret, just as in Asia. One way of preparing the maitake mushroom by the tribal members attempting to raise production numbers of the mushrooms in Oklahoma is to boil it 2-3 times (for removal of debris and insects), and then chop, salt, and flour the mushroom before deep-frying it (Chavez, 2009).

Around the 1970s, maitake became the focus of many scientific studies (Halpern, 2007). Today it is generally known for four major impacts upon the body: enhancing immunity; fighting cancer; assisting in diabetes and other related conditions by lowering blood sugar levels; and supporting heart health via lowering blood pressure and cholesterol while promoting weight loss. More recently, studies pertaining to the liver have surfaced.

For nearly 30 years, maitake’s main active constituent, a polysaccharide B-glucan, has been studied in-depth. By 2010, Grifola frondosa polysaccharide, also known as “GFP” or the “D-fraction,” was approved in China as a supplement for cancer (He et al., 2018). D-fraction is a standardized form of the beta glucans found in maitake that are held responsible for stimulating macrophages and was discovered by the Japanese mycologist Hiroaki Nanba in 1984 (Halpern, 2007). D-fraction has been demonstrated to enhance immunity (Halpern, 2007) and researchers have concluded that maitake’s effect on the immune system is better classified as immunomodulating, rather than immunostimulating (Deng et al., 2009). While most polysaccharides from mushrooms are inactive in oral preparations, GFP is not (Halpern, 2007). Almost all scientific inquiry into maitake and GFP, however, are preclinical trials on animals or cells, which need further research using clinical trials on human subjects (He et al., 2018).

It is the D-fraction from maitake which is most widely focused on within scientific research on cancer and immunity. Granted a patent and continuing his research along with a colleague, Dr. Nanba further isolated what is called the “MD-fraction,” considered by the researchers to be more powerful than the D-fraction as well as orally bioavailable and more purified (Halpern, 2007). Meanwhile, new polysaccharides continue to be extracted from maitake, such as the recent isolation of “GFP-22”, which is thought to have further immunomodulatory applications (Li et al., 2018).

Maitake, in supplement form or whole herbal form, enhances immunity largely by virtue of its polysaccharides: beta glucans are known to support the immune system and to increase the creation of specific immune cells. Furthermore, because of the way that maitake enhances the immune system, it may be viewed as adaptogenic. Those beta glucans found in maitake assist the macrophages in producing more cytokines (Halpern, 2007) and also result in reduced oxidative stress levels, indicating that maitake shows promise in supporting longevity and exhibiting neuroprotective effects (Tripodi et al., 2022). D-fraction, furthermore, not only activates macrophages but also dendritic cells and T cells, with the outcome that tumor cells may be inhibited (Halpern, 2007).

Maitake’s prowess in the arena of immunity has also led to its incorporation into HIV research. As early as the late 1980s, Japanese researchers concluded in animal trials that oral doses of D-fraction enhanced the helper T cells that are attacked by HIV (Halpern, 2007). Subsequent MD-fraction research exhibits a potential use against HIV and a long-term human trial found maitake’s polysaccharides to be working on multiple levels within the body (Halpern, 2007).

Maitake’s connection to immunity is also related to its antitumor activity. Its beta glucans assist in the production of white blood cells known as macrophages, which target tumor cells, supporting chemotherapy. Maitake may also reduce the side effects of some cancer drugs by decreasing hair loss as well as nausea and vomiting. Research results on humans suggest that maitake is most useful for breast, liver, and lung cancers and less so for those of blood, bone, or stomach (Babel, 2011), and has revealed how maitake’s polysaccharide constituents play a role in inducing apoptosis. GFP was recently combined with vitamin C with the results of cell apoptosis and autophagy in vitro, demonstrating potential antitumor activity in vivo (Zhao et al., 2017).

Maitake has shown particular relevance to breast cancer research. In this vein, it may exert an amphoteric effect of sorts; an oral extract (again highlighting the polysaccharides) was found to both stimulate and inhibit the immune functioning of breast cancer patients (Deng et al., 2009). This is not without dissent, however; a recent review notes the immunological potential of maitake but concludes that its uses remain uncertain with respect to supporting breast cancer (Rossi et al., 2018).

Some 2017 studies, on the other hand, have asserted that the polysaccharides of maitake induce breast cancer cell apoptosis and warrant use as a potential adjunct (Zhang et al., 2017) and that D-Fraction impacts mammary tumor cells directly and while modulating the cellular processes in cancer progression, has a host of impacts, among them the generation of less aggressive cell behavior, decrease in cell viability but increase of cell adhesion, and reduction of invasion and migration of mammary tumor cells, all while decreasing the amount of lung metastases in murine models and decreasing tumor burden (Alonso et al., 2017).

Maitake also possesses synergistic qualities that have been explored in chemotherapy for all types of cancer, with the results of lessening hair loss (up to 90%) and pain (83%) in study subjects (Babel, 2011). GFP has shown antiradiation effects in animal studies (Wang, 2003; Yan et al., 2010). That being said, crude polysaccharides have sometimes been demonstratively more active than purified fractions when it comes to inhibiting tumors (He et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2003).

Maitake has a strong impact on cardiovascular balance with an antihypertensive action. It may regulate blood lipids, blood glucose levels, fat metabolism, assist in weight loss, and lower blood pressure (Halpern, 2007). There is a connection here with weight loss; research shows that rats ingesting maitake lost body fat, and a Tokyo clinic used this observation to study 30 obese humans, whose diets were not altered in any way other than the inclusion of dried and powdered maitake, with each subject nearing their optimal weight and the average individual losing between 11-13 pounds in 2 months (Belleme & Belleme, 2007).

Even when consumed in culinary dishes, the mushroom may amplify fainting spells or low blood pressure in individuals with these pre-existing conditions (Halpern, 2007). This ability to lower blood pressure has led to exploring maitake’s use in addressing high blood pressure or hypertension. However, the effects found were not deemed powerful enough, and so more research is now undertaken regarding its potential synergistic effects, namely in combining maitake with reishi (Ganoderma lucidum), cordyceps (Cordyceps sinensis), and/or shaggy inky cap (Coprinus comatus) (Halpern, 2007). Maitake also possesses a connection to cholesterol: by inhibiting lipids, intake of the fungus may decrease cholesterol levels (Halpern, 2007), although only animal trials have been conclusive.

Maitake and maitake extracts have been studied repeatedly and for decades with reference to how its polysaccharides also work to lower blood sugar levels, although most studies again remain grounded in animal models (Kubo et al., 1994; Ma et al., 2014; Preuss et al., 2007, 2010, 2012). The mushroom thus has potential to assist in bringing balance to some cases of diabetes and other conditions in which blood sugar levels are relevant via lowering blood sugar levels and thus decreasing insulin resistance (however, if one already has low blood sugar levels, it may worsen the condition) (Halpern, 2007). Because polycystic ovarian syndrome (PCOS) is associated with insulin resistance, maitake may be of use there as well (Powell, 2015): in a human trial, women with PCOS were able to ovulate after partaking of maitake polysaccharide extract (Chen et al., 2010).

Although maitake is understood in Chinese medicine to be directly associated with the Liver and Large Intestine organ channels—indeed, ancient books apparently point to its role in protecting the liver (He et al., 2017)—research in these areas is just beginning, and although based still in animal models, is beginning to bear out the traditions that assert that maitake is hepatoprotective. At least three studies have shown that maitake’s polysaccharides possess an antihepatocellular carcinoma effect (Hu et al., 2015; Mao et al., 2015; Zhang et al., 2016).

Many experiments also indicate that maitake assists the liver against damage as well as that of the gastrointestinal tract (Halpern, 2007) and works to lower liver and serum lipids (Kubo & Nanba, 1996). This may have future import for protecting human livers from alcoholic oxidative injury as demonstrated in murine studies (Wu et al., 2002; Zhou et al., 2002); in ameliorating liver damage due to antiatherosclerotic effects; for use as functional food to forestall or address atherosclerosis and hyperlipidaemia (Ding et al., 2016); and in addressing poor diets in general, particularly those low in fiber (Halpern, 2007).

Grifola frondosa is also used as a homeopathic preparation in Germany, where it may be indicated for diabetes mellitus, hypertonia, neoplastic conditions, or stimulation of the immune system (Rogers, 2012).

Adult Dose

Fresh: 4 oz/day (Winston & Kuhn, 2008)

Dried: 5-10 g/day (Winston & Kuhn, 2008); powder, 3-7 g/day; and capsules, 2 500 milligram capsules 2x/day (Rogers, 2012)

Decoction: 2-3 tsp dried per 10-12 oz water decocted for an hour, 2-4 cups/day (Winston & Kuhn, 2008)

Tincture: 5-6 mL (1:5, 22.5%) (Winston & Kuhn, 2008); 15-30 drops, 3x day (Rogers, 2012)

D-fraction liquid: up to 6 mg 2x/day (Rogers, 2012)

Note that vitamin C may increase efficacy of all preparations (Rogers, 2012).

Safety

Maitake is contraindicated in cases of mushroom allergy (Winston & Kuhn, 2008).

Large doses can create flatulence and/or gastric upset (Winston & Kuhn, 2008).

In terms of drug interactions, maitake can lower fasting blood glucose by 30-63%: an open-label trial confirmed this result within 2-4 weeks via the ingestion of 500 g tablets taken 3x/day in tandem with oral hypoglycemic agents metformin, glipizide, and glyburide (Brinker, 2010).
