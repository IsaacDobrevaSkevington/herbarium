Geographic Distribution

There are around 15 agrimony species, at least four of which have strong herbal traditions: Agrimonia eupatoria, which is native to Europe, North Africa, Southwest Asia, and Macaronesia, was introduced to North America (Allen & Hatfield, 2004). Agrimonia pilosa is native to Asia and Eastern Europe. Agrimonia gryposepala and A. parviflora are native to North America.

Botanical Description

Agrimony is a perennial plant that tends to bloom from early summer to early autumn in most locations where it grows. Its steep flower spikes perched atop a leafy base has led it to be compared visually to church spires. The flowers themselves are small, many in number, and bright yellow in color.

While in blossom, the flowers are orientated upwards or outwards towards the sunlight. After the time of full flowering, the blossoms point downward and the spike becomes covered at the end with hairs, often referred to as “burs.” The seeds will then naturally cling to passersby for dispersal.

The blossoms as well as the leaves are said to have a spicy fragrance, which is said to resemble apricots (Grieve, 1931), and the leaves are also fragrant. While there is a great deal of variation among specimens, the greatest fragrance belongs to Agrimonia odorata (Grieve, 1931).

The plant as a whole can grow up to 2 feet or so in height and is rather downy all over. The pinnate leaves are sharply saw-toothed and the leaves near the base of the plant at least twice as large as the top ones.

Key Constituents

Tannins, polysaccharides, furanocoumarins, flavonoids, terpenes, silica (Kostryco & Chwil, 2017).

Sustainability

While agrimony is declining in Eastern Europe (but not listed as threatened because it is widely distributed with stable populations and no large threats), it is critically threatened in Cyprus (due to land abandonment and development) and is officially threatened in Belgium (Khela, 2012). The caterpillar of the Endotricha flammealis moth is nourished by Agrimonia eupatoria.

Harvesting Guidelines

Aerial portions of agrimony are generally harvested just prior to flowering or during flowering. Herbalist Matthew Wood (2008) has noted that agrimony is more astringent and less sweet in its post-flowering phase and accordingly has suggested that it be picked when there is a strong odor about the plant. The leaves may be picked from the sides of the stem to leave the main stalk to go to seed (Wood, 2008).

Uses

Agrimony is a gentle herb that has exhibited an incredible range of actions and indications throughout history while occupying a solid position in herbal traditions (it is rather like chamomile in this respect). Agrimonia eupatoria, however, has not received a great deal of parallel attention in scientific literature, from which there is still much to expect.

The threads that often stand out in the rich historical and traditional tapestry of agrimony are its applicability to liver balance as well as its indications for wounds, gastrointestinal health, and urinary issues, thanks in large part to its tannin content.

Its genus name, Agrimonia, is a rather misleading one, and it is not entirely clear how the word came to be connected to agrimony as we know it. The prefix agri- indicates a relation to the field or the land (from the Latin noun ager, agrī, meaning, among other connotations, “field,” “land,” or “country”). Agrimonia has traditionally been traced back to an ancient Greek word for an eye condition that featured white specks in the eye: ἄργεμον (argemon), which is generally believed to have been a reference to cataracts.

The word argemon, however, was transferred from a species altogether different than agrimony: another ancient Greek word, ἀργεμώνη (argemônê), referred to a type of poppy mentioned both by the ancient Greek botanical writer Theophrastus (c. 371-287 BCE) and was also written as argemonia in Latin by Pliny the Elder (23-79 CE) when discussing said poppy’s virtues, some of which are only vaguely similar to agrimony.

Agrimony’s species name, eupatoria, refers to the ancient herbalist-king of Pontus, Mithridates VI (c.135-63 BCE), sometimes called Mithridates Eupator. The “Eupator” portion of this king’s name was also given to another plant mistaken for agrimony, Eupatorium cannabinum (Barker, 2001), causing more confusion among herbal writers throughout the centuries. It was sometimes inferred by later writers that agrimony was named after the king in this way because he was the one credited with discovering the plant (Gerard, 1636) and in fact the earliest extant Latin record of agrimony also hints at the same idea (see Pliny, below).

Despite this etymological muddiness, common agrimony—and perhaps other species—as we know it today was known in antiquity: in the ancient Greek language, agrimony was often called εὐπατόριον (eupatorion). In Latin, agrimony was referred to as eupatoria.

Adding to the general word confusion are two other facts. The first is a link with the above-mentioned argemon: Dioscorides recorded that some folks accidentally called argemon “eupatorium” due to a confusion over the color of the flowers (Dioscorides, 70/2000) and Galen noticed the same confusion. Secondly, white horehound was also known as eupatorium (Dioscorides, 70/2000) and perhaps other herbs as well. In other words: use caution when these terms appear in old texts.

The earliest and most dominant use of agrimony that endures to our day is in addressing loose bowels, and this indication goes directly back to antiquity and to the first clear citation of agrimony. The Roman polymath of the first century CE, Pliny the Elder, briefly mentioned the eupatoria plant as being under royal origination (likely hinting at Mithridates Eupator) and described its seed, used in a wine infusion, as an “unparalleled remedy” for diarrhea or dysentery (Pliny, 77/1906).

Scholars are divided, however, as to whether the eupatoria Pliny wrote of is actually our Agrimonia eupatorium or rather the Eupatorium cannabinum. Pliny also remarked that the root of agrimony is useless (Pliny, 77/1906), which is interesting in light of other historical herbal indications. For example, some Native Americans tribes such as the Cherokee and Ojibwa have predominantly used the root of other agrimony species (Native American Ethnobotany Database, 2018).

Following very close after Pliny in time, the Greek physician and botanist Dioscorides (c. 40-90 CE) also described agrimony. Unlike Pliny, Dioscorides mentioned both seed and herb used in infused wine for dysentery or diarrhea, as well as for snake bites (Dioscorides, 70/2000). He also indicated agrimony’s leaves (pounded finely and mixed with old swines’ grease) for addressing difficult scars on ulcers (Dioscorides, 70/2000). Here we glimpse the blood purifying or tonic action of agrimony as well as the vulnerary, which, along with its antidiarrheal agency, are the most prominent extant ancient uses of the herb, all of which have been sustained down to the modern age.

In extant texts, Dioscorides was also first to indicate agrimony for liver health, and the Renaissance writers were also aware of this. Dioscorides had related that agrimony was called hepatorium or hepatitis, both words with a prefix meaning “liver” (hepa-, from the ancient Greek ἧπαρ, or hêpar, meaning “liver”). These names make perfect sense given agrimony’s major indication down the ages for liver balance; agrimony contains bitter principles and possesses a spectrum of actions well suited to liver balance (among them alterative, anti-inflammatory, cholagogue, hepatic, and liver tonic).

(Dioscorides’ assertion of the hepa– names might have been true in his age, but English herbalist John Gerard in the early 17th century noted that the German apothecaries used another herb with the name hepatorium, namely Hepatorium adulterinum (Gerard, 1636) and, a few years later, Jean de Renou, doctor to the French monarchs, argued that hepatorium was actually Agrimonia cannabinum (de Renou, 1653), a similar species of agrimony about whose confusion with Agrimonia eupatorium other Renaissance writers had things to say: see below.)

Dioscorides also directly indicated agrimony for “faults of the liver.” It should be noted that this phrase does not appear in many English translations of Dioscorides available today—rather, only his indirect mention of hepatorium and hepatitis. This is likely due to a difference in manuscripts being used in translation, and while that inquiry is outside the scope of this exploration, other manuscripts of Dioscorides did mention agrimony’s role for liver health directly, because the phrase iocineris vitiis (“for faults of the liver”) is included in Dioscorides’ text in Johannus Ruellius’ (1474-1537) translation of Dioscorides into Latin (Dioscorides, 70/1529).

A hundred years or so after Dioscorides, the writings of Galen (c.129-200/216 CE) also indicated agrimony for the liver. In Galen’s work, the liver connection was elaborated upon so it becomes clear that it was used for obstructions in the liver and in strengthening the liver through a binding action—likely a reference to its astringency, due to the presence of tannins (Tobyn et al., 2016).

Some 600 years later in the Arabic herbal tradition, Serapion the Younger (12-13th century) wrote that Abū Bakr Muhammad ibn Zakariyyā al-Rāzī (also known as Rhazes, who lived 854-925 CE) had demonstrated that the liver indication for agrimony as given by Dioscorides was not as efficacious as using one of the Artemisia plants instead (Tobyn et al., 2016). Agrimonia eupatoria, however, would go on to retain a place in Iranian traditional medicine (ITM) for hepatic conditions (Akbarzadeh et al., 2015; Asadi-Samani et al., 2015).

The example of Rhazes is also of interest because Dioscorides remarked that some people, being deceived, once called agrimony “artemisia” due to its many uses (Dioscorides, 70/2000). Furthermore, there is commentary on the Artemisia connection in Johannus Ruellius’ medieval translation and interpretation of Dioscorides, but it remains to be translated from the Latin.

Flashing forward again a few centuries, Agrimonia eupatoria re-appears in Anglo-Saxon manuscripts and use of the herb in the British Isles appears to originally be intrusive as records of folk use herald from areas of Norse settlements (Allen & Hatfield, 2004). Herbalist and author Maud Grieve also remarked that Anglo-Saxons taught the use of agrimony, which they called “garclive,” for warts, wounds, and snake bites (Grieve, 1931).

A recent study, meanwhile, chose three herbs from Anglo-Saxon manuscripts used as vulneraries in 10th–century England—among them Agrimonia eupatoria—to evaluate their antimicrobial properties against bacterial infections and wounds, and agrimony demonstrated moderate effects (Watkins et al., 2012). Indeed, Agrimonia eupatoria has been shown to exhibit antibacterial activity, particularly against Staphylococcus aureus and a-haemolytic Streptococci, among others (Al-Snafi, 2015).

Agrimony was at some point also used magically in the British Isles; Grieve copied out a magical rhyme pertaining to agrimony from an old English manuscript:

‘If it be leyd under mann’s heed,
He shal sleepyn as he were deed;
He shal never drede ne wakyn
Till fro under his heed it be takyn’ (Grieve, 1931, p. 13).

By the 11th century, agrimony appears again, this time at length in the botanical writings of German Benedictine abbess and herbalist, Hildegard von Bingen (1098-1179). Like Galen, von Bingen regarded agrimony as a hot herb and included a lengthy entry on the plant in her work entitled Physica. In particular, von Bingen seems to have favored agrimony as a form of energetic support for an individual who had taken leave of their senses, slipping into a loss of understanding:

Let a person who has lost understanding and knowledge have the hair cut from his or her head since the hair creates a horrible and shaking tremor. Then cook agrimony in water and wash the person’s head with this warm water. Also, the herb should be tied warm over the heart when the person first senses mindlessness. Then place it warm over the forehead and temples. The person’s understanding and knowledge will be purified, and the mindlessness will leave (von Bingen, 1155/2001, pp. 107-108).

This may seem like so much mindless medieval superstition upon a first reading, but we might also bear in mind modern herbalist Matthew Wood’s discussion and indication of agrimony for mental tension (Wood, 2006) as well as the herb’s mental health connection in the form of a Bach flower remedy.

von Bingen also used agrimony for an indication that, as we have seen, goes directly back to antiquity: diarrhea. She furthermore used it for skin issues (which could be indirectly linked to the liver) and other gastrointestinal upsets. Notably, she used it also for eye issues, which re-elicits that ancient confusion over the plant’s name, its indications, and its identification in antiquity. Specifically, her additional indications were as follows (von Bingen, 1155/2001):

  • bile and mucus from intestines (wine infusion)
  • saliva, excretions, and runny nose (in complex formula, pill form)
  • leprous skin condition due to incontinence or lust (complex formula, bath)
  • cloudy eyes (pounded agrimony placed around the eyes at night with a cloth, not entering the eyes)

The indication of agrimony for the eyes re-appears in the writings of physician Gilbertus Anglicus (also known as Gilbertinus or Gilbert of England) of the early Medieval period (late 12th-early 13th century CE). Anglicus listed agrimony in formulas for feeble sight and eye cankers—as well as in preparations for ears (in a plaster), rotting teeth (likely due to astringency), and others (Anglicus, 1230/2010).

By the time we’ve proceeded to the days of Chaucer (mid-to-late 14th century CE), it appears that agrimony’s use as a vulnerary was still going strong as well as its faint connection to Artemisia; Grieve noted that the herb was at this time called egrimoyne (as it was by Anglicus, above) and combined with vinegar and mugwort for all types of wounds as well as for a “bad back” (Grieve, 1931, p. 13).

Grieve (1931) added that another writer of the time (no source cited) recommended that agrimony be mixed with human blood and pounded up frogs for internal hemorrhages. It was also used in the renowned arquebusade water for wounds, which received mention in accounts of the Battle of Morat in 1476 (Grieve, 1931). This l’eau de arquebusade, which is formulated with aromatic herbs, was still being applied to bruises and sprains in the early 20th century (Grieve, 1931). One can still find it for sale in France even today, where it is made by such companies as Bouet, whose formulation contains 75 herbs and whose byline proudly declares that they’ve been producing it since 1634.

By the age of the Renaissance, English herbalist and botanist John Gerard (c. 1545–1612) may be found remarking, rather amusingly to our modern ears, that agrimony was for those “with naughty livers” (a skewed translation of Dioscorides’ original “faulty livers”; the Latin word for faulty could also mean “vice”), and reasoning, like Galen, that agrimony strengthened the liver through its binding quality (Gerard, 1636, p. 712).

While Gerard echoes Pliny and Dioscorides in that agrimony’s seed could be used in wine for bloody flux and for snake bites, and the leaves to close old ulcers that were not easy to heal, a new slant is also found in his assertion that agrimony, boiled in wine, could be used for chronic hepatic fluxes specifically in the elderly (Gerard, 1636). This may have been because agrimony is a gentle herb, suited to the fragility of age. Another of agrimony’s primary body-system connections—to the kidneys and the urinary system—also comes into focus here, as he also recorded that agrimony could be used for bloody urine caused by kidney imbalance (Gerard, 1636).

His rough contemporary, John Parkinson (1567–1650), the apothecary to King James I who also doubled as botanist to King Charles of the mid 17th century and who was adept at compiling herbal notes from past herbalists and writers all the way back to antiquity, notes at great length the different types of agrimony in addition to eupatoria, even including American species.

Parkinson, too, echoes in many places the ancient sources examined above, including the herb’s primary application to the liver. Parkinson also used his entry on agrimony as an occasion to mount his personal soapbox, in doing so revealing that Agrimonia eupatoria wasn’t always the go-to species, which he viewed as an error, and resuscitating some of the confusion we have noted in the ancient sources:

All the Apothecaries of our Land, especially of London now adayes, doe use this first kind of Agrimony [Agrimonia eupatoria], as the most assured Eupatorium of Dioscorides; howsoever in former times, both we and they beyond the seas, did usually take the Eupatorium Cannabinum, which they called Eupatorium Vulgare, for the true kinde, and either of ignorance not knowing the right, or of willfulnesses in not enduring to be drawne from their old errours, but our age hath reformed very many of these errours, we were formerly nusled up in; and no doubt by the diligent search of divers for the truth; both the light that hath risen hath appeared, and the darknesse that remains may be expelled, which time must as it hath done in part already, bring to passe, the pertinatious wilfulnesse of many being the cause that it is not wholly performed as yet (Parkinson, 1640, p. 596).

He also wrote that sweet agrimony (A. odorata) was much preferred, but as it couldn’t be had in any great quantity, the native one (A. eupatoria) served well. Parkinson then went on at even greater length to discuss the figures who disagreed about the Eupatorium, including Avicena [Avicenna], before he got around to agrimony’s virtues.

His many indications echo almost all previous sources but given the texts that have survived, his seemingly more original contributions to collective materia medica included use of agrimony for the respiratory system, namely for cough, colic, and clearing of the chest; agues; old cancers as well as ulcers; ears; limbs out of joint; drawing out things stuck in the flesh; and a distilled water preparation for all his many listed indications, either internal or external (Parkinson, 1640).

Also of Parkinson’s day was Nicolas Culpeper (1616-1654), who added that agrimony was found under Jupiter and in the sign of Cancer and delved a bit more into the herbal astrology involved. Culpeper also touched upon a reasoning for agrimony’s predominant use for liver imbalance: he asserted that agrimony was “a most admirable remedy for such whose lives are annoyed either by heat or by cold. The liver is the former of blood, and blood the nourisher of the body, and agrimony a strengthener of blood” (Culpeper, 1653/1880, p.19).

By the very early 18th century, we know from the writings of doctor William Salmon (who spent time in the American colonies and went back to practice in England but was disdained by physicians, rather like Culpeper) that shops were actively keeping Agrimonia eupatoria in dried form, both leaves and tops; as a distilled water, of the whole plant, gathered in June; and in syrup form, made from the juice (Salmon, 1710). According to him, recommended preparations at this time included (Salmon, 1710):

  • juice of leaves and flowers
  • essence of whole green plant
  • powder
  • sulphureous tincture
  • saline tincture
  • oily tincture
  • balsam
  • fixed salt of whole plant calcined

That’s quite a few preparations! For the many indications and details on how these preparations were formulated, see Salmon (available online). He included most all aforementioned indications from antiquity, including application to poor eyesight.

Less than a decade later, in 1718, English apothecary John Quincy remarked that: “the country people use the herb [agrimony] bruised, or its juice, in contusions and fresh wounds” (Quincy, 1718/1782, p. 106). That use hasn’t been found in extant folk records in the British Isles and may have fallen into disuse; the same is true for a claim a century later that agrimony was a favored use for ulcers in rural districts (Allen & Hatfield, 2004). We know, however, that both of those uses are notable traditional ones despite a dearth of extant sources in England.

Despite the vast number of indications and preparations in such works as that by Salmon, by the second half of the 18th century, agrimony fell by the wayside in formal herbalism. Some notable herbal practitioners such as Hill and Coffin continued to use it, however, particularly for jaundice and for supporting children with infectious agents, among them chicken pox, scarlet fever, or measles (Tobyn et al., 2016).

Meanwhile, the folk medicine later collected in the British Isles included agrimony as a widely used tonic or stimulant throughout England as well as in southeastern Scotland. The records show that it was popularly indicated for liver imbalances (Allen & Hatfield, 2004). The use for colds and coughs first found extant in Culpeper remain, as well as historical use for kidney or bladder imbalances. There are also new indications in the folk records there, different from what has survived in earlier records: sore throats and rheumatism (Allen & Hatfield, 2004).

There is also evidence that another species, the “fragrant agrimony,” or Agrimonia procera, was preferred over eupatoria in the British Isles (Allen & Hatfield, 2004). Scientific study has shown that in fact Agrimonia procera is a better source of the constituent agrimoniin (Granica et al., 2015). Sometimes agrimony’s vulnerary agency was applied to animals as well, as was the case in Norfolk, where cattle and horses received it for cuts (Allen & Hatfield, 2004).

Over in Ireland, meanwhile, agrimony was also being used for colds and liver issues as well as urinary imbalances, and also for scurvy and that most ancient of uses, old ulcers (Allen & Hatfield, 2004).

It has caused some confusion that agrimony was a favored herb in Donegal for sore eyes because eyebright was also favored and naturally available there (Allen & Hatfield, 2004). This indication for eyes, hinted at since the muddled etymology of the word agrimonia in antiquity, might give us pause; it seems that a very old applicability to the eyes has been long forgotten. It is also noticeable that American herbalist and naturopath Dr. Christopher of the 20th century used agrimony in formula along with eyebright for sore eyes.

In Ireland, where agrimony might be known in Gaelic as marbhdhraighean, it was also sometimes known as “tea-plant”; the herb was used in substitution when tea itself was rare, particularly in County Clare and County Galway; the flavor was compared to lemon or apricot (Mac Coitir, 2015). This substitution is also listed elsewhere, possibly in England (Grieve, 1931).

Agrimony still found its way to both table and home in other ways in the early 20th century: it was a popular ingredient in springtime drinks or “diet drinks” and was at one time popularly taken as a beverage in France (Grieve, 1931). Herbalist Thomas Bartram gave a recipe for such a drink including agrimony, lemon balm (Melissa officinalis), nettle (Urtica dioica), and red raspberry (Rubus idaeus) (Tobyn et al., 2016). Agrimony also yields a yellow dye that varies in brightness depending on when the plant is harvested and is capable of dying wool a dark yellow (Grieve, 1931).

Grieve also mentioned in passing a past and by-then obsolete use for agrimony as a vermifuge (Grieve, 1931). The indication for worming may be found among the Eclectics as well, who were aware of a tradition in which agrimony had a positive reputation for such use (King, 1854) and it can in fact be found at least as far back as in the late Renaissance age, where the juice of hemp agrimony was used in pill form to kill worms, particularly in children (Parkinson, 1640), but this remains to be fully traced and documented.

Grieve noted that agrimony was, at one time, part of the official London materia medica for its vulnerary virtues, but by her day (in the early 1930s), “modern official medicine does not recognize its virtues, though it is still fully appreciated in herbal practice as a mild astringent and tonic, useful in coughs, diarrhea and relaxed bowels” (Grieve, 1931, p.14).Today the herb is recognized by the German Commission E for diarrhea.

Agrimony also had a role to play in the New World. Different species were actively being used despite a drop-off of popularity in herbalists across the pond (Tobyn et al., 2016). Agrimonia eupatoria was introduced to North America but by 1922 was listed as a common perennial by the Eclectics (Felter, 1922), among whom agrimony’s indications grew in number and specificity—expanding even to support uterine and ovarian health and issues such as dysmenorrhea (Ellingwood, 1919).

Among the Eclectics, agrimony was used for its tonic and astringent actions, for kidney gravel, for lumbar region colicky pain or sensations from kidney imbalance, for irritation in the urinary tract with moodiness and ill-smelling urine, for dribbling in older individuals, occasionally as a gargle, and as an infusion for digestion (Ellingwood, 1919; Felter, 1922). It was also used as a specific by the Eclectics to stimulate the appetite and advised to be taken infused then cold in doses 1 hour prior to meals (Hool, 1918), among many other indications, such as to support menstrual and reproductive health.

The use of the gargle, meanwhile, was later elaborated on by Grieve, who sugested its use for a relaxed throat by pouring boiling water on the dried aerial portions of the plant (Grieve, 1931). Agrimony would go on to be recognized in the 20th century by the German E Commission as a gargle for sore throat. Some modern herbalists suggest an infusion for laryngitis and occupational voice loss on the basis of its antiseptic and anti-inflammatory actions (Kostryco & Chwil, 2017).

Other species of agrimony, namely A. gryposepala or A. parviflora, have been used extensively in infusion form by some Native American tribes for many uses similar to A. eupatoria. Herbalist Matthew Wood has noted that A. gryposepala is fairly interchangeable with A. eupatoria and is perhaps a bit more sweet and aromatic (Wood, 2008).

The Cherokee name for agrimony is a la s ga lo gi, and it has been used in traditional herbal formulations of the direction East both as a tonic and blood purifier (Garrett, 2003). As a traditional herb of the direction West, agrimony has been applied as an overall system-calmer in combination often with peppermint and for gastric issues. Today, agrimony is used for gallbladder issues, prepared as a tea for diarrhea, and the dried plant used for sore throat (Garrett, 2003).

Agrimony has also been used in preparation for the game competitions called “little wars”; the ballplayers have traditionally used it for a week prior to the game for strength and clarity (Garrett, 2003). One elder recalled the use of its leaves as a styptic, and, according to Garrett (2003), agrimony remains popular in Cherokee life as a mild astringent that includes both antibacterial and antifungal actions.

The Cherokee also have a history of using infusions (often of the root) for (Native American Ethnobotany Database, 2018):

  • diarrhea (burs)
  • blood tonic (root)
  • dermatological support, specifically for pox
  • febrifuge (burs)
  • gastrointestinal balance (root, cold infusion)
  • gynecological discharges (burs)
  • dietary aid for hungry children (root)

Meanwhile, the Iroquois tribe have a history of using the same species for children’s diarrhea and vomiting as well as liver health. Additionally, an infusion of the roots and flowers has a history of traditional use as an energetic application on any items meant for sale (Native American Ethnobotany Database, 2018). The traditional uses within other tribes include (Native American Ethnobotany Database, 2018):

  • Meskwaki: root used for nosebleeds
  • Ojibwa: root used for urinary issues
  • Potawatomi: herb used as a styptic and for nosebleeds

Almost all of these indications are similar to the traditional Western ones, even though the preferred part of the plant is different (i.e., the root versus aerial portions—the root was traditionally used in Western herbalism but not nearly so frequently).

It is somewhat surprising that not a great deal of scientific research has been done on Agrimonia eupatoria, or at least not until the second decade of this century. More than half of current research is focused on hairy agrimony, or Agrimonia pilosa, which is the most prevalent species in Asia and used in Chinese medicine, rather than on A. eupatoria.

Agrimony is used both internally and externally. There is some disagreement regarding its energetics and plant theories: since antiquity through the Renaissance it was viewed as energetically hot. Today, it is also seen as cooling (Holmes, 2006).

Of all its many uses, agrimony’s astringent action stands out and accounts for some of its applications for wounds as well as gastrointestinal, renal, and urinary imbalances. Because it is a source of hydrolysable tannins, one of agrimony’s chief actions is its astringency (like many other herbs rich in hydrolysable tannins, agrimony belongs to the Rosaceae family, which has a reputation for astringent herbs).

Herbalist Peter Holmes has summed up agrimony by comparing it to Alchemilla vulgaris (to which it is similar on many levels, not the least of which is a shared plant family), suggesting that it is ideally suited to the presentation of damp and stagnation with a tendency to heat, and describing it as an herb that combines restoring and draining agencies to yield a regulating function (Holmes, 2006). Agrimony is furthermore viewed as often suited to those who have an underlying stagnation in liver, kidneys, or stomach (Holmes, 2006), which also backs up predominant historical uses we’ve just traced.

Agrimony’s traditional use as a vulnerary, however, has not been entirely confirmed nor deeply investigated by scientific inquiry. Its tannin content certainly contributes to this action, and a recent evaluation of the herb’s chemical constituents indicates that the polysaccharides isolated from Agrimonia eupatoria, which make up about 20% of its content, are strongly anticoagulant (Kostryco & Chwil, 2017).

In Chinese medicine, however, agrimony has long been used in a four-herb formula dating back 2,000 years, called the ANBP. This formula includes Agrimonia eupatoria (A), Nelumbo nucifera (N), Boswellia carteri (B), and the pollen (P) of Typha angustifolia, and it has recently been shown to alleviate scar formation and promote healing (Hou et al., 2014). Agrimonia pilosa, meanwhile, has been found to hasten skin barrier restoration (Nam et al., 2017).

Despite its notable reputation as a liver herb since antiquity, investigation along this line of inquiry is also very recent. While agrimony has been found to protect against alcohol injury in rats (Yoon et al., 2012), there has been at least one very recent double-blind, randomized, and controlled clinical trial, which indicated that when given to individuals with elevated alanine transaminase levels, Agrimonia eupatoria protected against liver injury. The study ascribed this to antioxidant and lipid-lowering agency (Cho et al., 2018). Another 2018 publication, a review of 30 hepatoprotective herbs for alcoholic liver disease, also included Agrimonia eupatoria (Firdous et al., 2018).

Another of agrimony’s long standing indications—gastrointestinal issues—does not seem to have been widely investigated, other than being included in a study that looked at anti-adhesive herbs for Campylobacter jejuni, a bacterial cause of diarrhea, in which A. eupatoria was not found to have effect (Bensch et al., 2011). Agrimony was also used with success in a 1982 clinical trial for chronic gastroduodenitis but in a compound herbal preparation (Al-Snafi, 2015). Agrimony’s oldest extant tradition, as we have seen, is as a specific for diarrhea and for bloody diarrhea. Matthew Wood again proves instructive here, when he notices that it may be specific to diarrhea with fatty stools, indicating poor discharge of bile and digestion, hence linking agrimony back to its hepatic action (Wood, 2008).

Far less touted, perhaps, are agrimony’s applications to the respiratory system, namely asthma and bronchitis, which appear prominently in early 20th–century works, particularly in the Eclectic writings, where it was also indicated for tuberculosis. Aforementioned folk records from the British Isles recorded agrimony for coughs and congestion, but that also went back as far as Parkinson in the 17th century. Agrimony is presumably of use here in the respiratory system through antispasmodic and expectorant actions. Scientific studies have as of yet only focused on Agrimonia pilosa but have suggested value in attending not only asthma but also a variety of inflammatory conditions through anti-inflammatory and antiallergic actions and inhibition of ovalbumin-induced airway inflammation (Kim et al., 2012).

Agrimony has also demonstrated a strong historical and traditional link to kidney health and the urinary system, as we have seen it being used thus in Ireland, by some Native American tribes, by the early Eclectics, as well as during the Renaissance. This is also parallel to some Eastern practices; agrimony may be used for clearing Kidney fire and Kidney qi stagnation (Holmes, 2006).

Today this traditional usage for the urinary system remains strong in practice; herbalist David Winston (2003) indicates it particularly for scalding urine associated with cystitis and urethritis. Scientific research, however, has yet to catch up, although it is generally understood to be of specific assistance through not only its astringency but also its diuretic action.

One other adjacent emerging indication for agrimony worth noting here is for diabetes. At least as far back as 1918, and possibly further than that to around 1868 (Tobyn et al., 2016), Agrimonia eupatoria was indicated for diabetes (Hool, 1918). Recent research has underscored agrimony’s antidiabetic action, albeit in animal studies (Kuczmannová et al., 2016) and has suggested insulin-releasing and antihyperglycemic activity (Al-Snafi, 2015).

Agrimonia eupatoria may be said in some ways to have acquired a reputation as a near panaceal by the 20th century. Its indications for liver, gastrointestinal, and kidney/urinary health as well as its vulnerary action, which go back to the earliest records, remain prominent in practice. Research, however, has not quite caught up to agrimony’s many longstanding uses, especially when it comes to the A. eupatoria species; more remains to be confirmed, discovered, and rediscovered.

Adult Dose

Tincture: 1-4 mL (1:5 strength, 45%) 3x/day (Hoffman, 2003).
Infusion: 1-2 teaspoons dried herb per cup of boiling water, 3x/day (Hoffmann, 2003).

Safety

Agrimony is generally considered a mild herb.
Because agrimony is an astringent herb (which can inhibit nutrient absorption and impact digestion in large or sustained doses), use common sense, proper dosage, and caution in the presence of constipation, malnutrition, dryness, or anemia. Do not combine with alkaloid-rich herbs.
