Asparagus racemosus

Native To

Geographic Distribution

Nepal, Sri Lanka, India, Asia, Australia, Eastern and Southern Africa, favoring tropical areas.

Botanical Description

Shatavari is a prickly, woody, climbing vine that grows up to 2 meters long. Its leaves are narrow and uniform, not unlike pine needles, and it flowers in small spikes of tiny white blossoms on short spiky stems from July to August (Plants for a Future, n.d.). It fruits in September, producing blackish-purple berries (Thomsen, n.d.). Shatavari is a common shade-loving ground cover at low altitudes and tropical climates.

Key Constituents

Steroidal saponins (notably the sarsasapogenins shatavarin-I to –IV and diosgenin), asparagamine, β-Sitosterol, isoflavones (including 8-methoxy-5,6,4′- trihydroxyisoflavone 7-O-beta-D-glucopyranoside), kaempferol, terpenes, tannins, phenolic compounds, ascorbic acid (vitamin C), oligosaccharides, polysaccharides, amino acids, sulfur-containing acids, and trace elements of zinc, copper, manganese, iron, cobalt, sodium, potassium, calcium, and lithium (Bopana & Saxena, 2007; Hannan, 2007; Joshi, 1988; Negi et al., 2010; Visavadiya & Narasimhacharya, 2009).

Sustainability

Shatavari’s growing popularity in the herbal marketplace combined with a gradual loss of traditional cultivation and harvesting techniques has contributed to the overharvesting of the root from its natural habitat. It is considered endangered in some areas, and efforts are being made to ensure sustainable cultivation practices (Bopana & Saxena, 2007).

Harvesting Guidelines

Shatavari is well suited to rich, sandy loam but can be grown in any rich soil. It prefers warmer climates, generally needing a frost-free environment; in cooler areas, tubers can be harvested and stored through the winter then replanted in the spring (Bown, 1995; Huxley et al., 1992).

Seeds can be propagated in the spring (or in early autumn in greenhouses) after soaking in warm water for 12 hours, and will germinate within 6 weeks at 77° F. When they are large enough to handle, transfer seedlings into individual pots and keep them in a greenhouse or a sunny place indoors through the first winter. Roots can be divided in early spring, with the plants then transferred outdoors in late spring or early summer (Plants for a Future, n.d.).

Plants are harvested after 40 months; roots are dug in winter and peeled, sliced, blanched, and shade-dried (National Medicinal Plants Board, n.d.).

Uses

Shatavari is an important herb in the ayurvedic tradition, where it is known as a rasayana—an herb for longevity that revitalizes the brain and body tissues, generally bolsters the body against health imbalances, and strengthens the vital force. In Ayurveda, shatavari is considered a building and strengthening herb with demulcent and nourishing qualities. Translated from Sanskrit as “one who possesses one hundred husbands,” its name speaks to its reputation in Ayurveda as an important sexual and reproductive tonic. Shatavari is significantly phytoestrogenic and for this reason has a range of effects on hormonal health (Saxena & Chourasia, 2001). It supports healthy fertility, breastfeeding, and fetal development, regulates the menstrual cycle and menopause, and is useful in cases of threatened abortion or multiple miscarriages, insufficient lactation, low sperm count, and low libido (Maizes, 2015; Winston, 2019). Traditionally it is given in milk, which makes sense given the lipid-solubility of steroidal saponins, which are abundant in shatavari (Bathija, 2014).

A number of energetic and physiological actions contribute to shatavari’s effectiveness. From the ayurvedic perspective, it “intensifies fertility by nourishing the ovum and organizes the womb for fertilization” (Bathija, 2014, p. 98) and supports the motility of sperm by building up healthy vaginal fluids (Bathija, 2014). Shatavari is also used in Ayurveda to regulate ovulation. Shatavarins, the steroidal saponins present in the herb, have been shown in numerous animal studies to modulate hormonal levels and flow, with a specific affinity for breast, gonadal, and genitourinary tissue (Pandey et al., 2005; Thomsen, n.d.). A popular galactagogue, shatavari appears to significantly increase corticosteroid and prolactin levels, leading to increased weight and size of mammary tissue, increased milk production, and weight gain and thriving in infants (Gupta & Shaw, 2011). Shatavari is also used as an alternative to false unicorn root (Chamaelirium luteum) in cases of threatened miscarriage and as a tonic in instances of multiple miscarriages (Thomsen, n.d.). In animal studies, the isolated constituent shatavarin I has been shown to competitively block oxytocin-induced contraction of uterine muscle cells both in vitro and in vivo (Joshi, 1988).

Although shatavari is often used for ovo-uterine health, it supports reproductive and sexual health in all genders and has been shown to improve penile erection and increase sperm count and motility (Thakur et al., 2011). The constituent racemol, isolated from shatavari root, has been shown to interact with androgen receptors (Sekine et al., 1997). This, in addition to shatavari’s antitumor and antioxidant qualities, may inhibit androgen-dependent prostatic growth and discourage the development of prostate cancer.

Shatavari is also considered helpful in supporting healthy digestion and addressing a number of gastrointestinal concerns, particularly where there is inflammation and irritation. Its soothing demulcency and support of the mucosa make it extremely helpful for dyspepsia (for example, in gastrointestinal reflux disease and gastritis), and also for inflammatory conditions of the urinary tract where it acts additionally to inhibit crystallization of minerals that could form stones (Narayanan et al., 2011; Sairam et al., 2003). Shatavari also protects the gastrointestinal lining with an antiulcerogenic action and helps to soothe both acute and chronic gastric ulcers with varying causes. Shatavari is also used as a digestive herb and has been found to be as effective as pharmaceuticals (e.g., Metoclopramide) in aiding gastric emptying, an important factor in dyspepsia (Dalvi et al., 1990).

In Ayurveda, shatavari has also traditionally been used for dysentery and high pitta digestive imbalances, such as Crohn’s disease, pain and inflammation in the gut, and burning indigestion (Pole, 2013). The herb not only supports motility of the gut but also shows antimicrobial effects against Escherichia coli, Shigella dysenteriae, Shigella sonnei, Shigella flexneri, Vibrio cholerae, Salmonella typhi, Salmonella typhimurium, Pseudomonas putida, Bacillus subtilis, and Staphylococcus aureus (Mandal et al., 2000). Like all adaptogens, shatavari also supports immunity, making it useful for diarrhea with bacterial causes as well (Chinsembu, 2016; Narayanan et al., 2011). Its moistening energetics can also be supportive for the fluid loss that accompanies diarrhea.

Often, rasayanas share qualities with herbs that in the Western herbal tradition are considered adaptogens, immunomodulators, or antimutagenic herbs (Puri, 2003). Furthermore, some herbs—like shatavari—are now used in both Eastern and Western traditions. As an adaptogen, shatavari is both antioxidant and broadly anti-inflammatory, making it useful in countering the effects of stress, including oxidative damage, and helpful in supporting inflammatory chronic diseases such as type 2 diabetes. In diabetes, shatavari root appears to improve glucose tolerance by inhibiting its absorption in the gut, while enhancing pancreatic secretion of insulin (Hannan et al., 2012). Shatavari is also cytoprotective and neuroprotective, so it may be helpful in Alzheimer’s and Parkinson’s diseases or other degenerative conditions in which excitotoxicity and oxidative stress are the main mechanisms contributing to cell death and the degradation of brain tissue (Parihar & Hemnani, 2004; Rege et al., 1999).

Shatavari is an immunity-enhancing herb that acts in a number of ways. As an immunomodulator, shatavari has been shown to increase scavenging activity of macrophages; in at least one study, this was shown to be significant in reducing intraperitoneal adhesions, bands of fibrous tissue that develop within the abdominal wall as a response to trauma to the peritoneum (such as surgery, infection, or irradiation) and that can cause significant pain or obstruction (Rege et al., 1989). In animal models, shatavari extract inhibits drug-induced carcinogenesis, making it potentially protective against the development of cancers (Mitra et al., 2012).

Adult Dose

Powder: 3-4 g dried root/day (Caldecott, n.d.). 2 tsp dried powdered root with milk or honey 2x/day (Bathija, 2014).

Tincture: 3-15 mL (1:3, 25%) daily (Pole, 2013).

Fluid Extract: 4.5-8.5 mL (1:2) daily (Bone, 2003).

Safety

Shatavari is safe to use while breastfeeding, but should be used with caution in pregnancy; studies on potential teratogenic effects have had mixed results. Studies detect no toxicity or change in organ activity with doses of 50mg/kg (Muralidhar et al., 1993; Rege et al., 1999).
