Session 1: Nutritive Tonics + Common Conditions

60 minutes · Easy

Introduction

Welcome to Session 1 of the Herbal Support for Pregnancy Intensive! We’re so glad you’re here. This session will walk you through the use of some nutritive and tonic herbs that can be incorporated into daily life during pregnancy, as well as some herbs for stress and some other common pregnancy concerns. It will also cover some general information about herbal use during pregnancy as well as insight based on experience from clinical herbalists offering herbal support to clients during pregnancy.

Join Lisa Olson and Shona Richter MacDougall in this video as they discuss the topics they also explore below, including safe herbal use during pregnancy, nutritive herbs, and herbal support for some common conditions! Lisa and Shona are both clinical herbalists registered with the American Herbalists Guild and are excited to share their experiences working with clients during pregnancy.

Safe Herbal Use During Pregnancy

Before we dive in, there are some really important safety caveats to share with you. As you can imagine, we need to be especially careful when using herbs during pregnancy, as it’s a time when we need to be concerned about the safety of the growing fetus, maternal health, and maintaining the pregnancy. If you choose to use any herb or protocol discussed in this intensive, it should always be under the supervision of an experienced midwife, obstetrician (OB), or your medical provider.  

There are a lot of physiological changes that occur during pregnancy that we may not be keyed in on as herbalists. These unique physiological aspects of pregnancy need to be considered and monitored by your medical provider, especially in high-risk conditions such as hyperemesis, gestational diabetes, and hypertension, to name a few.  

In an ideal world, someone wanting to use herbs for pregnancy-related conditions would work with a midwife or OB who is also an herbalist, but such practitioners can be rare! So most of the time, it’s best to work with a clinical herbalist experienced with using herbs during pregnancy alongside a midwife, OB, or other medical provider who is open to herbal use, so that care can be collaborative.  

One of the things that an experienced practitioner can help with is weighing all of the factors involved in herbal use during pregnancy. There are a lot of elements that go into choosing safe herbs during pregnancy—their age, whether or not they have a history of miscarriage, their state of health pre-pregnancy, any medications they are currently taking, whether or not they’ve used herbs before, whether or not they have a high-risk pregnancy or pregnancy-related condition, the stage of pregnancy, and the care that they are receiving—for example, we know that Black and Indigenous people face inequitable healthcare/poorer outcomes during pregnancy and birth (Heck et al., 2021; Ozimek & Kilpatrick, 2018; Shah et al., 2011; Willis et al., 2014). These are just a few of the many factors that need to be considered! 

So with that in mind, when might we use herbs during pregnancy? Many practitioners choose to avoid herbs except in small doses for culinary use during the first trimester—even nutritive tonics—unless there is an active issue that requires support. So, for example, raspberry (Rubus spp.) leaf—which is commonly used as a partus preparator throughout pregnancy—is often not recommended until the second trimester even though it is a pretty safe, tonic herb. However, if someone is experiencing nausea and/or vomiting during the first trimester, which is when “morning sickness” most commonly crops up, herbs can then be considered, especially those that are widely regarded as safe during pregnancy and if the nausea and vomiting is affecting quality of life—which, of course, it almost always is! 

Other practitioners, however, aren’t as strict about using herbs during the first trimester, especially for someone that hasn’t previously experienced miscarriage, is well-versed in herbal use, and does not have any high-risk conditions, and these practitioners support individuals in adding tonic and nutritive herbs during the beginning of pregnancy, as well as for any complications that may arise.

That being said, there are some herbs that should never be used in pregnancy. Teratogens are substances known to cause fetal malformation, and these include herbs such as tobacco (Nicotiana spp.), wild cherry (Prunus spp.), asafoetida (Ferula narthex), and datura (Datura spp.), to name a few examples.

One concern that commonly comes up about herb use during pregnancy is whether or not it’s safe to take tinctures while pregnant, due to the alcohol content. While tinctures are alcohol-based extracts, the daily dose is typically so small that the amount of alcohol ingested is minimal. For example, if you take a teaspoon of a tincture that is 40% alcohol, you are ingesting less than 2.5 mL (½ teaspoon) of alcohol. Most practitioners feel that it is safe to take alcohol-based tinctures in pregnancy, but if you have any concerns at all there are a wide range of alternative preparations to choose from, including glycerites, vinegars, capsules, tablets, and teas. This article offers many ideas on herbal products beyond tinctures.  

So let’s get into some of those everyday herbs that can sometimes be used throughout pregnancy. First, we’ll talk about nutritives—herbs that provide important vitamins and minerals—then we’ll discuss some gentle nervines that can be used to ease stress and sleep issues, and we’ll end this session with some discussion on topical use of herbs for itchy skin, stretch marks, and general discomfort. 

Nutritives

Let’s start our discussion of some everyday pregnancy herbs with herbal nutritives. These are mineral- and vitamin-rich herbs that are often eaten as food or made into decoctions or long-steeped infusions. Pregnancy can take a huge toll on the body and it really is a time when there is an increased need for certain nutrients, including calcium, folic acid, and iron (The American College of Obstetrics and Gynecologists, 2022). Fluid needs increase during pregnancy as well (Montgomery, 2002).  

It is generally recommended to take a prenatal vitamin during pregnancy, as consumption of a multivitamin that contains a source of folic acid is associated with a decreased risk of perinatal depression (Jin et al., 2022), preeclampsia (Liu et al., 2018), and congenital anomalies such as neural tube defects (Ingrid Goh et al., 2006). Finding a prenatal vitamin that includes methylfolate rather than folic acid may be especially helpful, as a significant percentage of the population has genetic variations that impair their ability to convert folic acid to the biologically active form of folate, l-methylfolate (Greenberg & Bell, 2011). There are also some foods that are high in folic acid that can be incorporated into the diet, such as dark green leafy vegetables, beans, peanuts, sunflower seeds, fresh fruit, whole grains, liver, seafood, and eggs.

Some trial and error is needed to find the right vitamin for a particular person, as vitamins can trigger nausea at times and some brands and formulations may be less nausea-inducing than others for an individual. 

Although there’s a lot to be said for the desire to get as many nutrients from foods and nutritive herbs as possible during this time, pregnancy can be a period of fatigue and perhaps even nausea, so it can be difficult to have the energy—or the stomach—to make and eat nutrient-dense meals. A prenatal vitamin can be a good safety net—one that can be augmented with the use of nutrient-rich herbs and foods.  

Herbal infusions and decoctions are great ways of extracting vitamins and minerals from herbs, and they are simple to make and easily assimilated by the body. For that reason, they often do not contribute to digestive upset and may even be tolerated by those experiencing morning sickness. In addition, infusions and decoctions can help increase fluid intake during pregnancy. These can also be incorporated into smoothies to improve their nutritive value. Although we will start off this section of the intensive by discussing nutritive herbs that can be incorporated into infusions and decoctions, some of the herbs we discuss later in the intensive for stress, nausea, and other conditions can be combined with nutritives into a pregnancy-safe tea formula.  

Please keep in mind that nutrient-rich herbs will need to be steeped for an extended period of time or decocted in order to extract some of the nutrients they provide. Consider steeping your herbs overnight or for 8-10 hours or simmering in water for 20 minutes to increase the content of some minerals and vitamins. The exception for this is rose hips, which we’ll discuss more in a bit. 

Nettle (Urtica dioica) leaf is a common ingredient in pregnancy-safe teas due to its nutritive value and stellar safety profile. Nettle provides many minerals, such as calcium, iron, magnesium, potassium, and zinc (U.S. Department of Agriculture [USDA], 2016e). It also contains considerable amounts of vitamins C, B1, B3, and B5, beta-carotene, and flavonoids such as quercetin, as well as being a source of protein (USDA, 2016e). Nettle is highly versatile in the kitchen and if you have access to fresh nettle leaf you can add it to any recipe that calls for spinach (as long as it is cooked, blended, or dried to eliminate its sting). And just like spinach, fresh nettle can be frozen for later use. Dried leaf can also be added to stocks and broths, while powdered leaf can be added to smoothies and baked goods. Nettle leaf also makes an excellent herbal vinegar that can be added to many foods and drinks. Overnight infusions are a great way to prepare nettle. One can add a bit of peppermint (Mentha x piperita) leaf, ginger (Zingiber officinale) rhizome, or lemon balm (Melissa officinalis) leaf to change the flavor profile, as some love the flavor and others find it less palatable.  

Oat (Avena sativa) straw and milky seed Oat straw is rich in a variety of vitamins and minerals, including vitamins A, B1, B2, B3, B6, and C, calcium, copper, iron, magnesium, potassium, selenium, and zinc; oat also contains protein (USDA, 2016a). It makes a very mild-tasting decoction or long-steeped infusion and is safe to use regularly during pregnancy. Because of its mild flavor, oat straw can easily be used during preparation of stocks and broths as well. The milky oat tops of the oat plant are also rich in these nutrients and can additionally be used as a restorative to the nervous system. (Note that milky oat tops are best extracted fresh for nervous system use but are fine to use dry for nutritive purposes.) Some individuals who are gluten intolerant are able to tolerate oat straw and oat seed, and others do not do well with it. What I have found in my practice is that if a person can tolerate gluten-free oats in foods, they can tolerate oat in herb form. Be sure to obtain oat straw or seed that is not contaminated with gluten if this is a concern.  

Red clover (Trifolium pratense) flower contains many nutrients such as vitamins C, B2, and B3, calcium, iron, manganese, potassium, selenium, zinc, and flavonoids such as quercetin (USDA, 2016d). It can add other value as well, such as by acting as an alterative for skin concerns discussed later in this session. Red clover can be used both internally and topically for skin issues and is particularly useful in dermatitis, eczema, psoriasis, and mild burns, as it stimulates tissue repair. The taste tends to be mild, so it is well suited for those with a sensitive palate. It’s important to note that the use of red clover in pregnancy is somewhat controversial, although many herbalists consider it to be a safe, tonic herb that can fit well in a pregnancy protocol (Yarnell, n.d.). Because red clover acts as a hormone modulant, some sources advise against its use in pregnancy because of the concern that it may have an impact on hormone levels. However, even though red clover is sometimes used to modulate hormone levels, this is usually done with standardized extracts; and many herbalists feel that whole-plant preparations of red clover, such as infusions, are considered to have a mild enough hormone-modulating effect to be safe in pregnancy, akin to eating beans and other legumes.

Rose (Rosa spp.) hip contains protein, beta-carotene, iron, potassium, magnesium, calcium, zinc, and vitamins B1, B2 and B3, and is rich in vitamin C and flavonoids (USDA, 2016b; USDA, 2016c). Unlike the other herbs discussed in this section, rose hip is best prepared in warm water extractions for short amounts of time to preserve vitamin C content. Try adding rose hip toward the end of steeping time if you’re combining it with other herbs. In a study on the effect of brewing time on antioxidant properties of rose hips, steeping for 5-8 minutes was found to yield the highest amount of vitamin C (İlyasoğlu & Arpa, 2017). Because of the vitamin C and flavonoid content of rose hip, it has significant anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties—due to these properties, rose hip can be used to support musculoskeletal health, especially having to do with ligaments, bone, and other connective tissue, and help manage chronic inflammatory conditions. In addition to water infusions, rose hip makes a great jam, jelly, or syrup.  

Nervines

Stress and Anxiety 

Emotional stress is such a common experience in today’s fast-paced and information-overloaded world, and many people experience a continuation of their day-to-day stressors once they become pregnant. Stress may even be augmented during pregnancy, as additional strains come into the picture: the unknown of what life will be like with a new baby, the financial or interpersonal stress associated with the upcoming change, anxiety about labor and delivery, not to mention the physiological stress of a changing pregnant body! In addition to day-to-day stress, many people experience anxiety and other mood disorders during pregnancy. For some, these are chronic conditions carried over into pregnancy; for others, these may begin in pregnancy, often due to hormonal fluctuations.  

Because prenatal stress and anxiety can affect not only the quality of life during pregnancy but has also been associated with preterm birth, low birth weight (Schetter, 2011), and future childhood anxiety (Davis & Sandman, 2012), herbal and other support for easing anxiety and managing stress can be particularly important. Here are some herbal tools to support you in finding calm during this time!


Skullcap (Scutellaria lateriflora) aerial parts Skullcap is a member of the mint family along with other well-known nervines such as lemon balm (Melissa officinalis) and peppermint (Mentha x piperita), but unlike many mint-family plants, skullcap is not rich in flavorful volatile oils. In fact, skullcap has a mild bitter taste, and for this reason—and because it is most potent when used fresh or freshly dried—it is typically taken as a fresh plant tincture or glycerite, rather than a tea. 

Although skullcap hasn’t been well researched in humans, it has a long history of use for anxiety, irritability, and overwhelm. Because of its cooling energetic nature, it is my go-to for people that get easily irritated or “blow up” when they’re stressed or anxious, pregnant or otherwise. One small randomized controlled study found that skullcap can reduce symptoms of anxiety in some individuals at a dose of 350 mg/day for 2 weeks, and that skullcap enhances mood without reducing energy or cognition (Brock et al., 2014). However, for some people, it can have a mild sedative effect so take care if it’s your first time taking it during the day and start with a low dose. Skullcap combines well with other nervines, such as those discussed here. This can help offset any sedative effects that may occur. Although skullcap has not been specifically studied in pregnancy, no safety concerns have been identified during pregnancy (Gardner & McGuffin, 2013; Yarnell, n.d.).  

Chamomile (Matricaria chamomilla) flower is an easy-to-find herb often used in pregnancy for a variety or reasons: as a mild sedative, it can help encourage a restful night’s sleep; as a calming nervine, it can help to ease feelings of stress, anxiety, and overwhelm both during the day and at night; and as a calming carminative, it can help to ease digestive discomfort, including nausea and vomiting. While chamomile tea is probably the most common way to take chamomile, chamomile tincture and glycerite are also an option, especially if taking chamomile at night. Also, the tincture or glycerite tends to be stronger than chamomile tea as some find chamomile to be bitter when infused for over 10 minutes and steep the tea for a short period of time, which produces a milder preparation.  

Chamomile has been found to reduce generalized anxiety disorder (GAD) in multiple randomized, controlled trials (Amsterdam et al., 2009; Mao et al., 2016), and in a recent systematic review and meta-analysis of the scientific literature, researchers reported that chamomile is effective for generalized anxiety disorder as well as improving sleep quality. However, this meta-analysis did not find evidence for an effect on state anxiety (Hieu et al., 2019)—transient anxiety from a specific event. Thus, chamomile may be a better fit for someone with chronic anxiety rather than for someone looking for a calming cup of tea after a stressful event. With this said, chamomile is often used by herbalists in times of acute stress and found to be effective. Although these studies are not specific to the prenatal period, no safety concerns have been identified during pregnancy (Gardner & McGuffin, 2013). 

Lemon balm (Melissa officinalis) aerial parts Lemon balm is a mint-family plant with a lovely, lemony aroma and flavor. The lemon flavor is most pronounced in fresh or recently-dried plant material, and a tea of freshly dried lemon balm or a tincture or glycerite made from fresh plant material are the best options for optimum results. Lemon balm has long been used to promote a calm mood and its bright, lemony flavor can also be quite uplifting, so lemon balm is particularly useful for people who tend toward sadness when stressed or fluctuate between depression and anxiety. 

Human trials have demonstrated that lemon balm can reduce anxiety and insomnia (Cases et al., 2011) and promote feelings of calmness (Kennedy et al., 2002). In a systematic review and meta-analysis, lemon balm was found to be effective at reducing anxiety and depression, especially in acute situations (Ghazizadeh et al., 2021). Thus, lemon balm may be a great choice for easing acute anxiety and stress. 

Other calming nervines that can be used in pregnancy include hawthorn (Crataegus spp.) aerial parts, rose (Rosa spp.) petal, linden (Tilia spp.) bract and flower, and milky oat (Avena sativa) tops. Hawthorn, rose, and linden all make lovely additions to teas, while milky oat is best as a fresh extract and has very little nervine action when used dry. Hawthorn and linden are both particularly well suited for individuals that are experiencing stress that is affecting the cardiovascular system, as we’ll discuss in Session 3. I have found rose to be especially nice for anxiety that has a heavy quality to it, for example when anxiety is associated with grief, deep worry, or a past traumatic event. And milky oats is a wonderful nervine for people with chronic anxiety that has left them feeling frazzled, exhausted, or otherwise depleted; one does not tend to feel the effects of milky oats in the same way as other nervines, as milky oats does its work quietly behind the scenes—this is a slow-working herb that needs to be taken on an ongoing basis for addressing nervous system imbalance, in relatively high doses.  

Stress-Support Tea

The herbs in this formula can lend a hand in helping to manage stress and anxiety during pregnancy.  

Recipe

3 parts lemon balm (Melissa officinalis) aerial parts
2 parts chamomile (Matricaria chamomilla) flower
1 part linden (Tilia spp.) bract and flower 

Directions

  • Blend herbs together in a clean jar or container. Note that a “part” can be any form of measurement, depending on the size of the batch you’d like to make. If you’re making a large batch, then you may choose to have 1 part = 1 cup. For a smaller batch, you may choose to have 1 part = 1 teaspoon or 1 tablespoon.
  • Use approximately 1-2 tablespoons of the herbal mixture per 8-12 fluid ounces of water.
  • In a heatproof mug, combine herbs with water just off the boil. Cover and steep for 5-10 minutes, strain, and enjoy 1-3 times/day. 
  •  If drinking tea isn’t your thing, you can purchase these herbs as tinctures or glycerites and combine them into a formula and take 1-2 mLs (1-2 droppersful) 2-3x/day.

Insomnia 

Herbal nervines are not only helpful during the day to calm the body and mind, but they can also be tremendously useful in the evening to promote a restful night’s sleep. Insomnia during pregnancy is common, and can be caused by a variety of factors—hormonal changes, nausea and vomiting, an increased need to urinate, pregnancy-related aches and pains, having to sleep in a position that one isn’t accustomed to, and worries about the pregnancy, the birth, and life with a new baby can all keep someone up at night or make it difficult to sleep through the night. In a 2007 National Sleep Foundation survey, 84% of pregnant Americans experienced sleep issues at least three nights/week (National Sleep Foundation, 2007).  

Sometimes by minimizing daytime stress, sleep comes easier and sedatives are not needed. But other times, the use of sedative herbs is extremely helpful, especially when used in addition to easing daytime stress.  

When working with herbs for sleep, one thing to consider beyond the particular herb(s) to use is the delivery method. I like to use forms other than infusions and decoctions, as high water content can lead to more frequent urination, which is a concern that is already present in pregnancy, so there is no need to contribute further to it. Consider using tinctures, glycerites, oxymels, syrups, powders, electuaries, and other herbal products with no to low water content that are right for you.  

Lavender (Lavandula spp.) flower bud is a calming, sedative nervine that can be incorporated in multiple forms to improve sleep and ease anxiety during pregnancy. Lavender flower buds can be incorporated into tea—just take care not to steep too long to avoid a bitter taste!—and can also be made into a tincture or glycerite. Lavender essential oil diffused into a room or incorporated into topical products at proper dilution is also an option. 

Aromatherapy with lavender essential oil has been demonstrated to improve anxiety and quality of sleep in non-pregnant populations (Cheng et al., 2022; Karadag et al., 2015; Kim et al., 2021; Seiiedi-Biarag & Mirghafourvand, 2022). There has even been research on the use of lavender aromatherapy to reduce anxiety and pain during labor with positive effect (Tabatabaeichehr & Mortazavi, 2020). One study by Effati-Daryani et al. (2018) on the use of lavender during pregnancy tested the effect of a lavender-based cream applied at 25-28 weeks gestation on sleep quality. The lavender cream was made with a 1.25% dilution of Lavandula angustifolia essential oil and was applied to the legs an hour and a half before bed every evening for 8 weeks. One third of the participants in the study applied a placebo cream, one third applied the lavender cream only, and one third applied the lavender cream and then did a 10-20 minute footbath 30 minutes after applying the cream. The use of lavender cream (with or without the footbath) was found to improve sleep quality (Effati-Daryani et al., 2018).

 For clients that are experiencing trouble with sleep during pregnancy, lavender is often my first choice, as it seems to have the broadest applicability. One simple way of incorporating lavender into a bedtime routine is using a lavender dream pillow—these can be purchased or you can make your own. Another option, inspired by the clinical trial just discussed, is to purchase a scent-free lotion, cream, or body butter that you enjoy and incorporate lavender (Lavandula angustifolia) essential oil into the product at a 1% dilution. For example, if you have 1 fluid ounce of cream, you can stir in 6 drops of essential oil. You can then apply this to your feet before bed.

Valerian (Valeriana officinalis) root is the quintessential sedative herb, long used for insomnia, with, luckily, no known safety concerns during pregnancy (Gardner & McGuffin, 2013). I have found valerian particularly helpful for folks that are “too wound up” to sleep, have anxiety or tension that are keeping them awake, or tend to be hypervigilant and not feel safe enough to fall asleep. Valerian has a taste that many people liken to “smelly socks,” so it’s typically taken as a tincture or glycerite, rather than a tea, before bed.  

Valerian has been well studied for its ability to ease insomnia and anxiety and found to be particularly useful for insomnia associated with anxiety (Shinjyo et al., 2020). One thing to note about valerian is that it is very warming, and it can actually have a stimulating or aggravating effect for folks that already run warm, although in my practice this seems to happen much less often with fresh plant extractions than with dry plant use. So if it’s the middle of summer and someone is already having a hard time sleeping because they can’t get cool enough, valerian may not be the best fit—for these people, using some of the other nervines covered in this session, such as lemon balm, passionflower and skullcap, before bed might be a better fit! But for others that aren’t experiencing excess heat, valerian can work like a charm. It can also be combined with some of the cooling herbs we have discussed to offset its heating energetics.  As with so many things in herbalism, sometimes trial is needed here. If you are not sure if valerian is the right fit, consider taking it at naptime for the first time.  

Passionflower (Passiflora incarnata) aerial parts is a wonderful nervine, anxiolytic, and sedative. One common reason for being unable to fall asleep is unwanted thoughts. The Eclectic physicians of the 19th and 20th centuries used passionflower for “disturbed sleep from mental worry” (Ellingwood, 1919, p. 324), where one can not stop going through lists in their mind, or put unwanted thoughts aside, which is sometimes described as circular thinking. These were specific indications for this plant, and passionflower is still considered to be a specific for circular thinking by many herbalists today. Passionflower is especially effective in helping one let go of thoughts that often occupy one’s mind, and then allowing the body to relax and fall asleep. Study after study shows passionflower to be effective for both anxiety and sleep support. One such study shows that passionflower is as effective as oxazepam (a benzodiazepine) in treating generalized anxiety disorder, without resulting in the job performance impairment that oxazepam can sometimes cause (Akhondzadeh et al., 2001). While most clinical trials test the use of passionflower extract, tablet, or capsule, one clinical trial showed that passionflower tea improved sleep quality (Ngan & Conduit, 2011). In my practice I do not find that passionflower is a heavy sedative herb the way that valerian is, but instead it reduces the mental load one is carrying, allowing the body to return to the parasympathetic (rest and digest) mode of the nervous system, allowing one to reach a state of relaxation. Passionflower can combine well with valerian for sleep; it also combines well with skullcap and lemon balm for stress and anxiety both during the day and at night. Although passionflower has not been specifically studied in pregnancy, no safety concerns have been identified during pregnancy (Gardner & McGuffin, 2013, Yarnell, n.d.). 

Self-care practices 

In addition to herbal support, there are many self-care strategies that may help to ease stress, anxiety, and insomnia during pregnancy. This will vary from person to person, but incorporating a daily practice that helps you feel calm and at peace can make all the difference. Some practices that have been specifically studied and were found to help reduce prenatal anxiety, stress, and/or sleep disturbance include tai chi, yoga (Field et al., 2013; Nadholta et al., 2020), and meditation (Green et al., 2022) and mindfulness practices (Dhillon et al., 2017). Of course there are many practices not specifically studied that may be helpful as well. Consider walks in the woods, tree hugging, grounding exercises, journaling, and many others! Sometimes there is trial and error as one finds the supportive practices that are most beneficial to them. 

Topical Support

Pregnancy can be difficult on the body—extra weight, an increase in blood volume, hormonal changes, and pressure from the uterus all contribute to discomfort. One may experience sore, achy feet, overall body aches, hip pain, back pain, skin itchiness, irritation, or rashes, and issues with varicose veins among other things. Each pregnancy journey is different, but chances are that some support for one or more of these discomforts may be necessary. Let’s talk about a few of these concerns in more detail along with some ideas for herbal support. 

There are many possible changes to the skin as one’s pregnancy progresses. These changes can be pre-existing conditions or they can begin during pregnancy—some skin changes are related to the hormone changes that occur in pregnancy and others are brought on by other aspects of pregnancy. Although pregnancy-related skin changes are common, we don’t always know the reason that they occur. Several of these conditions cause itchy skin—from stretch marks to a variety of skin irritations and rashes. Most skin issues during pregnancy are uncomfortable but benign and resolve during the postpartum period, but skin complaints during pregnancy can occasionally be a sign of a more serious complication, so be sure to mention any skin discomfort to your health care provider.  

Stretch marks, also called striae gravidarum (SG), are one of the most common skin issues associated with pregnancy, affecting up to 90% of pregnancies (Freedberg et al., 2003, as cited in Chang et al., 2004). Stretch marks commonly occur on the belly, breasts, and thighs. They can cause itchiness and can be difficult to adjust to emotionally, but have no effect on the health of the pregnancy. One can try to address this both during the pregnancy and postpartum by using herbs and oils applied through massage. Stretch marks do tend to fade as time goes on, but tend not to disappear completely. It can be helpful to think of stretch marks as a sort of badge of honor—one earned through carrying, growing, and birthing another human.  

If skin conditions such as eczema and other forms of dermatitis, psoriasis, or hives are present pre-pregnancy these concerns can sometimes be exacerbated during pregnancy. Keep a watchful eye on these conditions, try to avoid known triggers, and address anything that may arise promptly when possible.  

In addition to skin changes, vascular changes also occur frequently during pregnancy due to significant increases in blood volume, which puts extra pressure on the veins. Vascular changes often show up as spider veins, varicose veins, flushing, and sensitivity to cold. Spider veins tend to appear on the neck, throat, face, upper chest, arms, and hands, while varicose veins tend to show up in the legs due to reduced blood flow to the lower extremities, pressure from the uterus, and hormonal changes. Pressure from the uterus can also cause a small amount of swelling in the feet, but swelling may also be a sign of a larger issue—if you are experiencing swelling in your hands and feet be sure to check in with your medical provider.

Body aches are also common throughout pregnancy, from back pain to pelvic pressure or discomfort. Massage, general gentle exercise as well as exercises that pinpoint the pelvis, pelvic floor, and abdominal muscles, and chiropractic work can all ease some of these complaints. Many of these complaints are unavoidable, but the use of things like comfortable shoes, an abdominal support band, or body pillows and extra support while sleeping, or elevating the feet during the day or night when possible can help this be more of a nuisance than a major discomfort. Herbs can help here too! 

Horse chestnut (Aesculus hippocastanum) seed prepared as diluted horse chestnut tincture can be applied topically to soothe varicose veins. While internal use of horse chestnut has been well studied to and found improve leg pain, edema, and skin itchiness associated with venous insufficiency (Pittler & Ernst, 2012) and there are no known complications associated with horse chestnut use in pregnancy, many people prefer to use it topically during pregnancy and avoid internal use especially in the first trimester as there is some data that suggests very high doses may decrease fetal weight in non-human animals (Gardner & McGuffin, 2013). One part horse chestnut tincture to one part calendula (Calendula officinalis) infused oil can be a nice pregnancy-safe preparation when massaged into the legs or other area of the body affected by varicosities. And a daily massage or self massage with horse chestnut can have the wonderful added benefit of relaxation!  

Gotu kola (Centella asiatica) leaf has traditionally been used for both stretch marks and skin irritations. It is well studied for its ability to minimize the appearance and formation of scars (Cotellese et al., 2018; Damkerngsuntorn et al., 2020; Jenwitheesuk et al., 2018). As a stretch mark is a type of scar, this may be one of the reasons it is helpful in reducing the appearance of stretch marks. In a randomized, controlled clinical trial, a combination of gotu kola, rosehip seed oil, and vitamin E was found to not only lessen the severity of new stretch marks, but also reduced the severity of stretch marks from previous pregnancies (García Hernández et al., 2012). This study reported no difference in adverse events between the treatment group and the control group. In another human study, gotu kola lotion alone was effective at reducing stretch mark lines and pigmentation (Nuraini & Rahayu, 2021). I typically suggest applying gotu kola infused oil topically during and after pregnancy to minimize the appearance of stretch marks, but gotu kola tincture can also be added to your favorite lotion or cream (approximately 3 mL per 30 mL/28 g of cream or lotion).  

Chickweed (Stellaria media) aboveground parts is the ultimate herb for itchy skin and has an excellent safety profile whether used topically or internally during pregnancy. Chickweed can be eaten during pregnancy, and makes a wonderfully nutritive addition to smoothies and salads, but it is most often used topically in the prenatal period. A compress made with strong chickweed infusion or fresh plant material, or chickweed-infused oil used as is or incorporated into creams, salves, butters, or lotions can all be helpful preparations to ease the itchy skin that often occurs as the belly grows during pregnancy. Chickweed is most effective when used as a fresh plant, in whatever preparation you choose to use it in.  

Calendula (Calendula officinalis) flower is the royalty of skin care! One could not discuss herbal care for the skin without including calendula. It can be used for so many concerns, from rashes and irritation to swelling and inflammation, from dermatitis and eczema to wounds, both in terms of lessening bleeding as well as speeding up healing times. Calendula also soothes itchy skin, burns, varicose veins, cracked nipples, and bruising. It has so many helpful skin-related actions, such as antifungal, antibacterial, antiviral, anti-inflammatory, vulnerary, antiseptic, and styptic. Applied topically calendula is used to lessen oozing and weeping of wounds, rashes, eczema and other skin concerns, and promote faster healing. In pregnancy, it is particularly helpful for the management of stretch marks and can be combined with bitter almond oil for this purpose. Calendula can be used as a poultice, infused oil, salve, cream, fomentation, or liniment. It is so versatile and is often found in many homes as part of an herbal first-aid kit. Although it should be avoided internally in early pregnancy (Brinker, 2010), it appears to be safe for external use, as there is a long history of traditional use and no adverse events associated with topical use reported in the scientific literature (Mills & Bone, 2005). 

Bitter almond oil, a carrier oil produced from the kernels of bitter almond (Prunus amygdalus var. amara), can be massaged into areas affected by stretch marks, both during pregnancy and afterward. One study showed that massaging bitter almond oil into the skin for 15-minute intervals reduced the development of stretch marks; simply applying the oil was not nearly as effective as applying it with massage (Timur Taşhan & Kafkasli, 2012). Bitter almond oil can be massaged into the breasts, abdomen, and thighs throughout pregnancy. It can also be used as a carrier for some of the other herbs mentioned in this section.  

CONCLUSION

That wraps up our time with nutritives, nervines, and topical support that can sometimes be used throughout pregnancy, and in the next two sessions we’ll focus on some common conditions that occur during pregnancy and the herbs that can be used to help manage them. Next up: morning sickness, heartburn, constipation, yeast infections, and colds and flu. We’ll see you in Session 2!
