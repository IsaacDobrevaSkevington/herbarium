Session 3: Common Conditions During Pregnancy, Part III

60 minutes · Easy

This session of the intensive covers three common conditions that can occur during pregnancy: iron-deficiency anemia, gestational diabetes, and high blood pressure, as well as the use of herbs to prepare the uterus for birth. 

As a reminder, any of the herbs and protocols that are discussed here should be implemented under the care of a qualified midwife or obstetrician (OB). It’s also important to remember that an herb that can be used safely during one pregnancy is not necessarily safe during another—herbal safety during the prenatal period varies quite a bit depending on the factors discussed in Session 1. In addition, it’s important to be regularly monitored by your medical team, especially with conditions that can severely impact maternal or fetal health, such as high blood pressure and gestational diabetes, to be sure that any herbs you take are having the desired impact as well as to determine when it’s time to switch to or add in conventional treatment options in order to avoid adverse events, such as preterm birth, pregnancy loss, or compromised maternal or fetal health.

With that important safety consideration in mind, join clinical herbalist Lisa Olson in this video for a discussion on pregnancy-associated anemia, gestational diabetes, hypertension (high blood pressure), and partus preparators to ready the uterus for labor and delivery — all which she also explores in the lesson below!

IRON-DEFICIENCY ANEMIA

Iron-deficiency anemia is a relatively common diagnosis during pregnancy, and it’s often discovered during the first trimester screening—somewhere around 12 weeks—though it tends to become aggravated as the pregnancy progresses. One of the reasons this is such a common pregnancy concern is that blood volume increases significantly during pregnancy. If you’ve been pregnant, you may remember finding yourself out of breath frequently, even before the uterus has grown big enough to impact the space your lungs take up—and increased blood volume is the reason (along with an increase in progesterone) behind this uncomfortable symptom—your heart needs to work a lot harder to move all that blood around!

Blood volume increases most significantly during the first few weeks of pregnancy and then continues to increase throughout. Although the amount it increases varies from pregnancy to pregnancy, blood volume typically increases by about 45% (Sanghavi & Rutherford, 2014)! This increase in blood circulating throughout the body means a relative reduction in the amount of hemoglobin—and thus, iron—in a given volume of blood, which may lead to a diagnosis of iron-deficiency anemia. 

Why do we care about decreased iron during pregnancy? There are a few reasons. Iron-deficiency anemia can cause symptoms such as dizziness, shortness of breath, and tachycardia (increased heart rate). Iron deficiency during pregnancy may also result in low birth weight and premature birth (Sifakis & Pharmakides, 2006). It is even associated with higher levels of depression during pregnancy (Dama et al., 2018) and may increase the risk for postpartum depression (Wassef et al., 2019).

Conventional treatment for iron-deficiency anemia is typically a recommendation for increasing dietary sources of iron and iron supplementation—usually in the form of ferrous sulfate. However, this form of iron can cause constipation—something that many pregnant people are already struggling with during pregnancy. Iron chelate, on the other hand, is known to cause less gastrointestinal disturbance (Abdel Moety et al., 2017; Milman, 2012), and may even be better absorbed than ferrous sulfate (Abdel Moety et al., 2017).

Dietary sources of iron include red meat and poultry, which contain heme iron (the more easily absorbed form of iron). Non-heme iron is found in plant foods including vegetables such as dark leafy greens, spirulina, nuts and seeds, and some dried fruits such as apricots, figs, and dates. Molasses is also rich in non-heme iron, along with compounds such as sulfur and fructose that increase its absorbability (Jain & Venkatasubramanian, 2017). However, it’s important to consider that at the point in which iron-deficiency anemia is most often diagnosed during pregnancy, symptoms of significant nausea and/or vomiting may also be present, making it exceedingly difficult to get enough iron-rich foods in the diet. 

An iron-rich herbal syrup is a popular choice for iron-deficiency anemia—commercial herbal syrups are available and they can also be easily made at home. I typically combine nettle (Urtica dioica) leaf, alfalfa (Medicago sativa) leaf, dandelion (Taraxacum officinale) root, and yellow dock (Rumex crispus) root with molasses into an herbal syrup. In some cases this has been enough to bring iron levels back up into a safe range; in other instances, clients have needed to take iron supplements, sometimes along with an herbal iron syrup, to get back in a “normal” range. As previously mentioned, you’ll want to talk with a midwife or OB to decide if this might be a good approach for you. If iron levels are very low, it may be best to start off with a conventional iron supplement, such as an iron chelate, and use the herbal iron syrup concurrently if desired.

While some medical professionals are not concerned with excess iron intake during pregnancy, there is some evidence that too much iron is associated with decreased fetal growth (Hwang et al., 2013), so be sure to discuss a recommended dose with your medical team. The World Health Organization recommends 30-60 mg of iron per day during pregnancy, and it may be best to avoid going over 60 mg (World Health Organization, 2012). Keep in mind that prenatal vitamins include varying amounts of iron, so take the amount of iron in your prenatal vitamin into consideration when choosing additional supplementation.

Homemade herbal iron syrups can be considered to be food-like preparations, akin to ingesting more iron-rich foods in your diet, and many practitioners consider it safe to combine an herbal iron syrup with a conventional iron supplement during pregnancy—they can even work synergistically—but, again, it’s important to discuss this with your medical team. While a commercial herbal iron syrup will have an amount of iron per dose on the label, it’s difficult to judge how much iron a homemade syrup contains, as the iron content of plant material varies considerably from batch to batch, depending on the soil in which the plant was grown, among other factors.

If someone is concerned about gestational diabetes, either because it runs in their family or they’ve been diagnosed with it in previous pregnancies or their current pregnancy, they may need to avoid the use of herbal iron syrups due to their sugar content. Instead, a decoction or infused vinegar can be prepared with the same herbs. However, the taste of a decoction or herb-infused vinegar may not be palatable for everyone, and some individuals may opt for a conventional iron supplement instead.

Dandelion root, nettle leaf, alfalfa leaf, and yellow dock root all contain non-heme iron, which is less absorbable than heme-iron from animal sources. One good way to improve non-heme iron absorbability is by taking the plant-based source of iron with vitamin C (Higdon et al., 2016). Some iron-rich herbs contain small amounts of vitamin C, but adding lemon juice to an herbal preparation can significantly increase the vitamin C content. 

Dandelion (Taraxacum officinale) root is a common “weed” that is packed with nutrition. Although the leaf has some iron content, it is also highly diuretic so not the best fit during pregnancy (when there is plenty of diuresis occurring naturally!); thus, dandelion root is preferred during pregnancy. Dandelion root also contains appreciable amounts of vitamin C, calcium, magnesium, and potassium (U.S. Department of Agriculture, 2016c)—all helpful nutrients needed during pregnancy. Dandelion root is also a digestive tonic, so it can help the body digest and absorb the iron and other nutrients in a meal or herbal formula. Dandelion root can also be used fresh by adding it to soups, stews, and other dishes.

Nettle (Urtica dioica) leaf, the poster child for herbal tonics, is a common ingredient in pregnancy-safe teas due to its nutritive value and stellar safety profile. Nettle’s vitamin and mineral content extends far beyond iron, with considerable amounts of vitamins C, B1, B3, and B5, beta-carotene, calcium, magnesium, potassium, and zinc (U.S. Department of Agriculture, 2016d). Fresh or dried leaf can also be added to soups, stir frys, and other dishes.

Alfalfa (Medicago sativa) leaf is popular as animal fodder, as it is chock full of vitamins and minerals! In addition to iron, alfalfa leaf contains vitamins B1, B3, B5, B7, C, and E, beta-carotene, calcium, magnesium, potassium, and even folate (U.S. Department of Agriculture, 2016a)! Another great way to get the benefits of alfalfa is by adding alfalfa sprouts to your diet; however, it is generally recommended to avoid eating raw sprouts in pregnancy due to potential microbial contamination, so these would need to be cooked, by adding them in as the last step in preparation of a soup or other cooked dish.

Yellow dock (Rumex crispus) root is a triple whammy for iron-deficiency anemia in pregnancy. Like dandelion root, yellow dock shines as a digestive bitter, improving digestion and assimilation. In addition to its iron content and support in improving iron absorption, yellow dock is also a gentle laxative—one of the only herbal laxatives that is safe in most pregnancies. Thus, it can also help counteract the constipating effects of iron. Win-win-win! Yellow dock also contains vitamin C, beta-carotene, vitamins B1, B2, and B3, calcium, magnesium, potassium, and zinc (U.S. Department of Agriculture, 2016b). Note that yellow dock can bioaccumulate cadmium—if there are high levels of cadmium in the soil where yellow dock is growing, there may be high levels of cadmium in the root (Zhang et al., 2014), so it’s important to source yellow dock root carefully. 

If iron-deficiency anemia is an issue for someone, it’s a good idea to check in about their digestion. If an individual previously had digestive difficulties—bloating, decreased appetite, indigestion, constipation, etc.—before getting pregnant, or if digestive difficulties have started with pregnancy, gastrointestinal health can be addressed with mild digestive tonics, such as dandelion root, as well as through supportive lifestyle and dietary strategies, such as including digestive spices in meals, eating mindfully, and eating on a regular schedule, so that iron assimilation is more likely to happen.

GESTATIONAL DIABETES

Gestational diabetes (GD) is a type of diabetes that is unique to pregnancy; diabetes is considered gestational if it is in someone that did not have diabetes previous to becoming pregnant. Gestational diabetes occurs when a pregnant body is not able to make enough insulin, which helps cells take in sugar from the blood, to meet the increased demands during pregnancy, resulting in high blood sugar levels. Gestational diabetes is somewhat common—the CDC estimates that it occurs in 2-10% of pregnancies (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2021). Risk factors for GD include a family history of GD, advanced age, and a polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) diagnosis.

Gestational diabetes is a concern that needs to be monitored by a qualified midwife or OB, as untreated GD is known to cause potential complications with delivery—GD is associated with high-weight babies that may be more difficult to birth vaginally, preterm birth, and low blood sugar in the baby postnatally, which can in turn result in other concerns for the newborn. There is also an increased risk for the baby of developing type 2 diabetes later in life. In addition, there is increased risk of maternal high blood pressure and preeclampsia as well as future diabetes.

Gestational diabetes doesn’t tend to develop until the second or third trimester, and it’s often diagnosed during a glucose challenge test that is typically given at around 24 weeks of pregnancy. Some midwives and OBs will give the alternative option of keeping a log of daily fasting and postprandial (post-meal) blood sugar levels using a glucometer instead of taking the glucose challenge test. In either case, it’s a good idea for those that are at risk for or who have been diagnosed with gestational diabetes or borderline gestational diabetes to purchase a glucometer so that they can monitor their blood sugar and understand which foods and activities are affecting their blood sugar levels the most, in addition to helping practitioners (under the supervision of a midwife or OB) determine if herbal support is making a positive impact on blood sugar levels.


If the glucose challenge test or log of daily blood sugar levels indicates that someone has GD, they may need to take insulin or other medications, such as metformin, to keep their blood sugar in a safe range to avoid the aforementioned complications. Sometimes, however, someone is considered “borderline” and they are given a certain period of time to get their blood sugar levels back on track before insulin or other medications are prescribed. This is when herbal support can be really helpful! Herbs can also be helpful before the glucose tolerance test is given, when someone knows they are at risk of developing GD.

Dietary and lifestyle practices for managing blood sugar are recommended by conventional and complementary practitioners alike. Adhering to these practices is often the most effective means to managing GD, apart from pharmaceutical intervention. However, herbs can also play a part for some individuals. 

One goal of GD management is to minimize spikes in blood sugar, and reducing (and sometimes eliminating) sugar—including maple syrup and honey—and refined carbohydrates from the diet is often the most helpful strategy. Making sure to include healthy fat and protein with every meal—even snacks—is also key. To keep blood sugar levels from going up and down drastically, it’s also often recommended to have small meals throughout the day rather than three large meals. These are a few generally accepted suggestions; however, there is a large amount of conflicting information available about what people with GD should eat. Thus, dietary choices will depend on what an individual feels comfortable with, what aligns with their personal dietary philosophy, and what their medical team recommends for their unique situation, and it can be really helpful to meet with a nutritionist after a GD diagnosis. 

The important thing to remember about dietary strategies and GD is to keep track of blood sugar levels to discover what foods increase blood sugar for you—everyone responds to foods in different ways. Some people with GD may be able to eat a bit of dessert as long as they have a protein-and-fat-rich meal beforehand, and others may not even be able to eat sweet potatoes without spiking blood sugar! Other tactics that have been helpful for some people include not eating too close to bedtime, eating the largest meal of the day at breakfast, and eating consistently at the same times every day. Hydration also seems to be key, as dehydration can spike blood sugar levels simply because blood sugar becomes more concentrated when the amount of water in your blood decreases.

Exercise is also important when managing GD. And exercise doesn’t have to mean intense workouts! Being pregnant itself requires a lot of energy, so simply adding a small amount of movement can make a big difference. I often recommend taking 15-20 minute walks after each meal, as there is some evidence that exercise can help to prevent GD (Barakat et al., 2019; Mijatovic-Vukas et al., 2018), and post-meal walks specifically can help to lower blood sugar in diabetes in the general population when combined with a low-carbohydrate diet (Li et al., 2018; Myette-Côté et al., 2018). There is even some promising preliminary evidence that this is true in pregnancy as well (Christie et al., 2022).

It’s important for people to find what exercise works best for them, so that they will be motivated to stick with it and it brings them joy rather than dread. If postmeal walks aren’t an option, dancing, swimming, tai chi, and yoga are all also great choices. In one randomized controlled trial, 15-20 minutes of yoga practice 5 days per week for 8 weeks combined with mindful eating practices was found to reduce fasting and postprandial blood glucose levels in participants diagnosed with GD (Youngwanichsetha et al., 2014).

Now, on to some helpful herbs! 

Bitter herbs may be helpful for some people, when taken before meals, to improve digestion in general and improve the body’s ability to take in sugar specifically. Bitters that are generally considered safe in pregnancy include dandelion (Taraxacum officinale) root, yellow dock (Rumex crispus) root, and artichoke (Cynara scolymus) leaf, but there may be others that are appropriate in specific cases. I typically suggest just a small dose (10-20 drops) on the tongue before each meal to activate the bitter taste receptors that kickstart digestive processes.

Artichoke (Cynara scolymus) leaf is often used as a general digestive tonic and bitter to improve digestion and assimilation as well as to address common digestive disturbances, such as gas, bloating, constipation, and indigestion—all commonly experienced during pregnancy! But improving digestion isn’t the only way that artichoke might address GD; artichoke and artichoke-based products have been found to significantly reduce fasting blood sugar levels (Jalili et al., 2020).

Cinnamon (Cinnamomum spp.) bark is a well-supported herb for managing diabetes and insulin resistance in the general population (Deyno et al., 2019; Shang et al., 2021), and it is my go-to herb for GD. I’ve seen it make enough of a difference for some people that they are able to eat a wider variety of foods and stay off of insulin when taking it. It’s important to note here that many sources recommend against using cinnamon in pregnancy unless under the guidance of a qualified practitioner—this is largely due to a study that fed rats large amounts of a single cinnamon constituent (Mantovani et al., 1989), and thus may not be applicable for whole-herb preparations at reasonable doses. However, there are also some Chinese medicine texts that indicate that cinnamon is not safe in pregnancy (Gardner & McGuffin, 2013), so it’s important to check in with your midwife or OB to determine if cinnamon might be safe for you. 

In my practice, I tend to suggest (with the approval of a client’s midwife or OB) 1-2 grams of cinnamon per day as a capsule—these are capsules that contain powdered cinnamon bark, not extracted capsules. This amounts to less than a teaspoon of cinnamon, which someone might easily consume in their diet daily while pregnant, so I consider it a safe dose. However, another option is to simply add a teaspoon or so of cinnamon powder to meals daily. 

Some individuals have voiced concern that taking cinnamon will aggravate their pregnancy-induced heartburn. For those taking cinnamon capsules, I have not found this to be true, but it’s certainly something to look out for.

Another herb that I tend to use in GD is maitake (Grifola frondosa)—a mushroom that is safe for most people during pregnancy and also has blood sugar modulating effects, though this has not been confirmed through clinical trials. However, preclinical trials have shown that it may be particularly helpful to restore insulin sensitivity and regulate blood glucose in people with type 2 diabetes, and in one clinical trial, maitake polysaccharide extract was found to improve insulin sensitivity and induce ovulation in participants with polycystic ovary syndrome (Chen et al., 2010). Simply adding fresh (or dried and reconstituted) maitake mushroom to the diet is a great choice; extract powders and decoctions are also an option.

Another herb that bears mentioning is raspberry (Rubus spp.) leaf. Some people choose to use raspberry leaf in the second and third trimesters as a partus preparator, which we’ll be talking about in a bit, and it may also help to manage blood sugar. In one published case report, it was proposed that raspberry leaf tea lowered the required insulin dosage for someone with GD (Cheang et al., 2016).

This brings up another important point—the use of any herb can have an impact on blood sugar or insulin; thus, individuals that are taking insulin, metformin, or another blood sugar medication during pregnancy need to be closely monitored if they choose to also incorporate herbal support under supervision, as medication doses may need to be shifted by their prescribing physician.

This is another reason that testing blood sugar every day is important. Creating a daily log of fasting and postprandial blood sugar levels is a great opportunity to learn about your body, as well. What foods increase your blood sugar to concerning levels? What activities help you lower blood sugar so that it is within an acceptable range? What herbs work best for you and does taking them at a certain time of day make a difference? Again, this can and should all be done under the supervision of your medical team.

It’s also important to note that patterns in blood sugar can be challenging to make sense of—sometimes levels go up for apparently no reason at all, sometimes numbers are great for apparently no reason at all. But with a regular routine of herbs and dietary and lifestyle practices, many people are able to see an overall trend of improved blood sugar levels with time. However, sometimes the amount of changes that need to be made in order to see a difference can be exhausting and frustrating. It’s not uncommon for people to wonder “why does so-and-so get to eat all the chocolate cake they want during their pregnancy and I can’t even eat a carrot!?” For this reason, some people do choose to stop being strict about their diet and exercise and opt for going on insulin or other medication so that they can just relax and enjoy their pregnancy, and that is an understandable and valid choice. 

Because managing GD can be frustrating and stressful, I almost always add nervines to clients’ formulas when they are working hard to keep their blood sugar within range, especially because stress can also impact blood sugar levels! Revisit Session 1 for a list of pregnancy-safe nervines. 

I also often turn to shatavari (Asparagus racemosus) root for GD—its adaptogenic properties can help individuals with stress management, and there is also some preclinical evidence that it can help to modulate blood sugar through its inhibitory effects on enzymes that break down carbohydrates (Vadivelan et al., 2018). Shatavari is commonly used in ayurvedic medicine to maintain pregnancy and prepare for birth, and I find it to be a wonderfully nourishing support before, during, and after pregnancy. 

HYPERTENSION

Like gestational diabetes, hypertension during pregnancy can cause maternal and fetal complications, so it’s incredibly important to work alongside and be monitored by an OB or midwife when taking herbs to manage hypertension. Hypertension in pregnancy can be a sign of preeclampsia, which can lead to kidney and other organ damage as well as eclampsia (seizures). High blood pressure can also mean decreased blood flow to the placenta and preterm birth, among other things. So this is a condition that needs to be very closely monitored, regardless of whether or not herbal support is being used. The cutoff that most practitioners use when diagnosing hypertension during pregnancy is that systolic blood pressure should not exceed 140 mm hg, while diastolic blood pressure should not rise above 90 mm hg, though this guideline varies from practitioner to practitioner.

Because of the safety concerns just mentioned, at the point that high blood pressure is diagnosed during pregnancy it may be too late to turn to herbal support. Ideally, herbs should be incorporated before a diagnosis of high blood pressure. A midwife or OB will typically take blood pressure readings at every prenatal visit; thus, there should be a record of blood pressure changes. If blood pressure begins to rise beyond the natural rise in blood pressure that occurs in the second half of pregnancy, herbs can be incorporated before conventional treatment is needed. You can also get an at-home blood pressure monitor to check in between appointments, though these aren’t always as accurate as in-office readings. At-home readings should be taken at the same time each day. Herbal support for high blood pressure in pregnancy can also be considered from an early pregnancy stage for someone that has risk factors for high blood pressure, such as diabetes, being pregnant with twins, or having had high blood pressure in a previous pregnancy.

As with other conditions of pregnancy, there is not a lot of clinical research on herbal support for hypertension during the prenatal period. However, there are some hypotensives typically used in herbalism that are sometimes safe in pregnancy,, including passionflower (Passiflora incarnata), linden (Tilia spp.), hawthorn (Crataegus spp.), cramp bark (Viburnum opulus), and hibiscus (Hibiscus sabdariffa).  

Passionflower (Passiflora incarnata) aerial parts can be helpful during pregnancy for a number of different reasons. Its nervine and mildly sedative effects can help release tension, promote a calm mood, and encourage restful sleep. Passionflower has also been traditionally used to manage hypertension and no safety concerns are noted during pregnancy (Gardner & McGuffin, 2013). It may be particularly helpful for those that are experiencing high blood pressure due to stress.

Linden (Tilia spp.) bract and flower is similar to passionflower, as a gentle nervine and hypotensive that can be particularly helpful if stress is contributing to high blood pressure. Linden is traditionally used as an overall cardiovascular tonic and may also be a good fit for someone with a genetic predisposition to high blood pressure. Linden has the added benefit of having a pleasant taste, and a cup of linden tea can be a delightful daily cup as part of a self-care practice for those that are prone to high blood pressure.

Hawthorn (Crataegus spp.) berry and aerial parts is probably the most common herbal support for all-things cardiovascular, hawthorn has a long history of use for high blood pressure. There has been a considerable amount of research on the use of hawthorn for various heart conditions (Pittler et al., 2008; Wu et al., 2020), and a 2006 randomized, controlled clinical trial found that in patients with type 2 diabetes, blood pressure decreased with the use of 1200 mg hawthorn extract per day for 16 weeks (Walker et al., 2006). Although this and other trials did not study hawthorn in pregnancy specifically, the results are promising for hypertension during pregnancy, as hawthorn does not have any known safety concerns in pregnancy (Gardner & McGuffin, 2013).

Cramp bark (Viburnum opulus) bark is most commonly used during pregnancy to ease uterine spasming and threatened miscarriage, but it is also a mild hypotensive that can safely be used throughout pregnancy for most people. Cramp bark likely works to lower blood pressure through its antispasmodic effect on smooth muscle, and it may also be helpful for the muscle cramps and body aches associated with pregnancy.

Hibiscus (Hibiscus sabdariffa) calyx is a popular herb for helping to manage hypertension, and randomized clinical trials indicate that it can lower blood pressure in those with mild to moderate hypertension (Abdelmonem et al., 2022). Hibiscus has a pleasantly tart taste and can easily be incorporated into a daily tea. Its cooling effect may also be appreciated in pregnancy, as many people become aggravated by hot weather while pregnant. No safety concerns are noted in pregnancy in moderate doses (Gardner & McGuffin, 2013). 

I typically suggest a blend of these herbs for a hypotensive tea or tincture formula, but I’ve also had clients simply purchase linden or hibiscus tea bags and use those regularly.

One other safety precaution to be aware of regarding herbal use for hypertension in pregnancy: if you’re familiar with herbal support for hypertension in general—not during pregnancy—you are probably wondering about the use of herbal diuretics, such as dandelion (Taraxacum officinale) leaf. While dandelion leaf could be considered safe during pregnancy in certain situations or as a tonic food, there are typically more than enough trips to the bathroom that occur during pregnancy and another diuretic on top of pregnancy hormones and pressure on the bladder is not needed!

As in GD, exercise can make a big difference for those with hypertension in pregnancy. One systematic review of the scientific literature found that 140 minutes of moderate-intensity exercises (e.g., brisk walking) per week can decrease a woman’s risk of GD, preeclampsia, and gestational hypertension by 25% (Davenport et al., 2018)—that’s just 20 minutes per day.

Likewise, stress-reduction techniques are also important. One small study found that pregnant women that practiced meditative yoga 3 days per week for 3 months had significantly less occurrence of hypertension and preeclampsia compared to a control group (Jayashree et al., 2013).

PARTUS PREPARATORS

We’ll close this session of the Herbal Support for Pregnancy Intensive with a short discussion on partus preparators. A question that often comes up during pregnancy is “are there any herbs I can take to help prepare my body for birth?” Historically, herbs known as partus preparators were taken during the last weeks or months of pregnancy to strengthen the uterus in preparation for birth. These were commonly used by the Eclectic and Physiomedicalist physicians of the 19th and early 20th centuries and are still sometimes used by herbalist midwives today. Herbal partus preparators were also historically a part of the materia medica of Indigenous people of North America, and are still used by Native American practitioners today (Ford, 2015).

Partus preparators include black cohosh (Actaea racemosa) root, partridge berry (Mitchella repens) aerial parts, cramp bark (Viburnum opulus) bark, black haw (Viburnum prunifolium) bark, and raspberry (Rubus spp.) leaf. Most of these are not commonly used in this way anymore, except for raspberry leaf, which is known even outside of herbal circles for its use during pregnancy.

Raspberry (Rubus spp.) leaf is thought to strengthen and tonify the uterine muscles, helping to avoid disorganized contractions and promote efficient labor and delivery. In one retrospective study, researchers concluded that raspberry leaf is safe in pregnancy, and can shorten the first stage of labor, reduce the incidence of preterm and postterm birth, and increase the likelihood of having an unassisted birth (Parsons et al., 1999). A double-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled clinical trial demonstrated that 2.4 grams of raspberry leaf per day resulted in a shortened second stage of labor and a lower rate of forceps deliveries; these researchers also noted no safety concerns (Simpson et al., 2001).

It’s important to note here that, historically, blue cohosh (Caulophyllum thalictroides) root was also a popular partus preparator. However, blue cohosh is no longer recommended in pregnancy due to a number of reported adverse events, including perinatal stroke, heart failure, and the failure of other organs in infants born with the use of blue cohosh before or during labor (Gardner & McGuffin, 2013).

CONCLUSION

In this session we’ve covered a few ideas for herbal support for anemia, diabetes, and hypertension in pregnancy. These are herbs that I commonly use in my clinical practice, but everyone will have their own unique history and pregnancy complications, so it’s important to discuss herbal use with a qualified practitioner under the supervision of a midwife or OB. Herbal use can be safe for many pregnancies, and can lend such a valuable hand in helping to manage the conditions that often crop up during this short but significant season of life.
