Session 2: Common Conditions During Pregnancy, Part II

60 minutes · Easy

INTRODUCTION

This session focuses on common conditions that affect many pregnancies, including morning sickness, heartburn, constipation, yeast infections, and colds and the flu, and how herbs and lifestyle considerations can be supportive for these concerns. 

It is important to remember that an herb that can be used safely during one pregnancy is not necessarily safe during another pregnancy—herbal safety during pregnancy varies quite a bit depending on what someone’s personal experience is during the prenatal period, and whether any complications have arisen. During pregnancy, health concerns sometimes come up, and then the choice must be made regarding how to most effectively and safely address these concerns, whether that be with herbs and supplements or turning to conventional medicine. There are times when short-term use of an herb that may be contraindicated in pregnancy may be appropriate under the watchful eye of your healthcare professional. However, there are some herbs called teratogens (discussed in Session 1) that should never be used in pregnancy. In addition, it’s important to be regularly monitored by your obstetric care team, whether you are using a midwife, OB-GYN, or general practitioner, especially when any concerns arise. 

Join clinical herbalist Shona Richter MacDougall in this video for a discussion on herbal support for common conditions including morning sickness, heartburn, constipation, yeast infections, and colds and the flu — all which she also explores in the lesson below!

MORNING SICKNESS

There can be many causes of nausea and vomiting when pregnant, from morning sickness to stomach flu, and lack of room in the final months. It is said that up to 80-90% of pregnancies are affected by some degree of nausea, often occurring early in the pregnancy and tapering off by week 16; the unlucky 15-30% will experience nausea and vomiting throughout their entire pregnancy (Viljoen et al., 2014). This is called hyperemesis gravidarum (HG). Since HG consists of severe vomiting that can sometimes last the entire duration of the pregnancy, there can be an increased risk for complications and those with HG need to be monitored closely by a medical professional, so I will not focus on this here. 

Morning sickness can be caused by high levels of human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG), which is a hormone produced by the body right after a fertilized egg attaches to the uterine lining. Increased estrogen levels during pregnancy can also contribute to nausea and vomiting (Viljoen et al., 2014). Low blood sugar can also contribute to morning sickness, and at times there is no known cause (Cleveland Clinic, 2017). 

Stomach bugs are often viral but can be caused by bacteria as well. Both kinds of pathogens can lead to nausea, vomiting, and diarrhea during pregnancy. The same herbs discussed for morning sickness below can also be helpful for supporting stomach flu during the prenatal period. If vomiting becomes uncontrollable, goes on for an elongated period of time, or if any concerns regarding dehydration are present, be sure to contact your medical professional. 

We will focus on support for morning sickness here, including both dietary and herbal suggestions.

Small, frequent meals can be helpful, as well as high-protein, low-fat foods at snack time. Identifying known triggers (foods, scents, tastes, etc.) that lead to nausea and vomiting can be very helpful. Once these are identified, avoiding triggers whenever possible is ideal. At times iron supplementation can cause gastric upset, so take notice of the timing of nausea and vomiting if this occurs after taking certain supplements, herbs, or medications. Sometimes taking supplements after larger meals can help with this, but at times one has to find an alternative. 

Ginger (Zingiber officinale) rhizome is a known antinauseant and antiemetic. It is recognized in both scientific literature and traditional use to lessen the duration and severity of vomiting episodes, lower incidence of dry heaving, and decrease nausea. Fresh or dried ginger can be used, and ginger candy can be a good option, as it is easy to carry when out and about. Taking up to 1 g of dried ginger daily is considered safe during pregnancy. Larger doses should be limited, as ginger is an emmenagogue at very large doses (Viljoen et al., 2014).

Fresh ginger on the gums above the two front teeth is an option as well. This has worked for many women that I have worked with as it can be worn there all day if needed. It can be preferable to tea at times, as it is easy to transport and does not contribute to frequent urination. It is harder to reach the daily limit on ginger when using it this way if one is nauseous all day. 

Peppermint (Mentha x piperita) leaf is both antinauseant and antiemetic. Peppermint infusion is used as a digestive aid and for calming digestive spasms. In my experience this is best with a bit of milk (dairy or nondairy)—I really have no idea why, but it is much more effective if used this way. Peppermint is best used in smaller doses, as an infusion (herbal tea) only, as large amounts of the volatile oils can cross into the placenta (Romm, n.d.a; Winston & Kuhn, 2008). Sometimes mixing other herbs with peppermint is helpful in stretching the use of peppermint for the entire day. One can make an infusion of peppermint, ginger (Zingiber officinale) rhizome, chamomile (Matricaria chamomilla) flower, and/or lemon balm (Melissa officinalis) aerial parts. Peppermint essential oil can also be used in the form of an aromatherapy inhaler throughout the day, but should not be applied to the skin nor taken internally.

Vitamin B6 can improve mild to moderate nausea (Romm, n.d.a), and for some this works miracles. Studies show that B6 lessens nausea and vomiting. One study comparing two groups of women with morning sickness found that vomiting completely resolved in over two thirds of the women  taking B6 every 8 hours for 72 hours (Sahakian et al., 1991). The dose is 10-25 mg up to 3-4 times daily.  

Wild yam (Dioscorea villosa) rhizome can be used to ease stomach cramping and reduce spasms. Used by both the Eclectic physicians and contemporary midwives, this herb is often tried after other options have been exhausted and are not effective. A dose of the tincture consists of 20-30 drops; this can be repeated up to 3 times a day. 

Chamomile (Matricaria chamomilla) flower is antispasmodic and carminative, can reduce spasms and cramping as well as support digestion. This can be used as a stand-alone herb or mixed with some of the herbs listed above. Chamomile also has a relaxing action, so watch to make sure this does not exacerbate any tiredness one is experiencing. 

Dandelion (Taraxacum officinale) root can help with morning sickness as well. Dandelion root stimulates the appetite and helps support digestion through its bitter qualities and inulin content, and supports liver function by increasing bile flow. Small doses are suggested at 15-20 drops of tincture, or it can be used as a decoction. 

Ginger-Mint Glycerite

This herbal glycerite can be taken throughout the day to help ease feelings of nausea. The sweet taste of this preparation won’t aggravate nausea and vomiting in most people and it also won’t affect blood sugar—a common factor in morning sickness.

Use dried herbs for this recipe. This formula can also be made into an alcohol-based tincture, if preferred. Simply swap out the vegetable glycerin with 100-proof alcohol, such as vodka or brandy, and omit moistening the herbs with water.

Ingredients

3 parts ginger (Zingiber officinale) rhizome
2 parts dandelion (Taraxacum officinale) root
1 part peppermint (Mentha x piperita) leaf
Food-grade vegetable glycerin

Directions

  • Place herbs in a sanitized glass jar. Note that a “part” can be any form of measurement, depending on the size of the batch you’d like to make. If you’re making a large batch, then you may choose to have 1 part = ¼ cup. For a smaller batch, you may choose to have 1 part = 1 tablespoon.
  • Fill the jar half full with dried herb, which will expand as the plant material absorbs the glycerin and water. 
  • Add enough just-boiled water to wet the herbs. You want to be able to press the herbs with a sanitized spoon and see the water squeeze out of them, but you don’t want so much water that they’re floating in it. 
  • Fill the remainder of the jar with glycerin—enough to cover the herbs by 1 inch. 
  • Place a square piece of natural waxed paper on top of the jar (this protects the glycerite from any chemical coating that may be on the lid), then seal the jar with a lid.
  • Give the jar a shake to ensure that the herbs and glycerin are thoroughly mixed.
  • Label, and store in a dark location such as a cupboard.
  • Check back the next day to see if the glycerin needs to be topped off a bit due to expansion of the herbs. All plant material should remain thoroughly saturated with the water/glycerin mixture to reduce the chance of the herbs decomposing or molding.
  • Let the glycerite sit for 4-6 weeks, visiting every few days to give the jar a shake.
  • After 4-6 weeks, place a few layers of cheesecloth or finer cloth within a funnel placed in the mouth of a sanitized glass jar, and decant the mixture. With clean hands, gather the cloth up and squeeze strongly, squeezing as much liquid from the herbs as possible. Compost the herbs.
  • Cover the jar, and let the liquid settle overnight in a dark location. This will allow any herb sediment to settle to the bottom of the jar, and you can pour the liquid into another jar, leaving the bottom layer of sediment behind. You can also strain the glycerite through a finer filter such as an unbleached coffee filter to remove sediment.
  • Transfer the glycerite into a sanitized, dark-colored glass bottle or bottles with dropper tops or caps.
  • Label and store in a cool, dark location. Use within 1 year. Take 1 dropperful as needed, 3-4 x/day.

HEARTBURN


Heartburn is very common in pregnancy. Aviva Romm, M.D. and herbalist, reports that approximately two thirds of women experience heartburn by the third trimester (Romm, n.d.a). Some experience this much earlier in the pregnancy, and others not until the end. Luckily this resolves itself once the baby is born! 

Heartburn can be caused by multiple different factors, such as relaxed lower esophageal tone due to pregnancy hormones. Specifically, progesterone affects this by creating less tone in the lower esophageal sphincter, allowing gastric juices to flow backward through this sphincter (Vazquez et al., 2015). Pregnancy hormones can also slow the actions of the digestive system making for longer transit time; therefore, the food is moving slower through the gastrointestinal (GI) tract, which can cause both bloating and heartburn (Cleveland Clinic, 2021). And then of course there is extra pressure on the stomach from the uterus as it grows, especially during the last trimester, as the uterus is pressing on the rectum (Cleveland Clinic, 2021). It is important to address heartburn rather than just dealing with this and waiting for it to resolve on its own, as digestive juices can degrade the tone of the esophageal sphincter and lead to heartburn or gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) issues in the future. 

There are some things to consider that address heartburn that do not involve taking herbs or supplements. You may need to experiment with these to find what may be triggers for you and what adjustments may be helpful:  

  • Try eating multiple small meals rather than three larger meals; this seems to be most helpful during the last trimester. 
  • Eat meals at least 3 hours before bed. If you do decide to snack, try to have smaller, lighter snacks just before bed. 
  • Do not lie down right after eating. 
  • Drink water and other fluids between meals rather than with meals. 
  • Try to avoid foods that cause heartburn. Triggers can differ for everyone, but here are a few common trigger foods to be aware of: citrus, tomatoes and tomato products, fried foods, spicy foods, and high-fat foods.
  • Avoid peppermint if having heartburn as it can relax the esophageal sphincter. 
  • Sometimes ginger is too hot and can contribute to heartburn. Sometimes smaller amounts are tolerated and helpful, but too much is not a good thing.  
  • Try adding raw almonds to the diet as they can improve esophageal sphincter tone. Eat 8-10 almonds up to several times a day as needed. 

Now, on to the herbs! 

Marshmallow (Althaea officinalis) root is anti-inflammatory and has mucilaginous qualities; it is coating and soothing and demulcent to the digestive tract. It is a cooling herb so it combats the heat of heartburn well. Marshmallow has a long history of traditional use in multiple modalities. Most often the root is used, but some people find they tolerate the leaf and flower better. Any part of the plant that one can find and use is great, as they all offer mucilaginous properties.

The best way to use this herb is in a cold water infusion or in powder form. One can make a hot or warm infusion if that is preferred, especially if mixing with other herbs. Hot water will degrade some of the polysaccharides (one of the constituents in marshmallow that can be sensitive to heat), but a hot infusion is still effective, and some find that it creates more mucilage. Some folks have a really difficult time with the slimy texture of marshmallow, as it really has no taste. If the texture is an issue, the powdered form tends to work well, and can be added to water, juice, tea, yogurt, or applesauce. Ingest this quickly after mixing the powder into your chosen carrier, as the longer it sits the more mucilaginous it becomes. For many this works so well that they can get beyond the odd texture. 

Papaya enzymes are an option for heartburn. This supplement is traditionally used and often suggested by midwives. I have used this myself as well as with multiple pregnant clients, with good success and no negative outcomes. They taste sweet and are easy to transport which are both positive. There is a bit of controversy using papaya enzymes, as there is some theoretical information that unripe papaya can cause uterine contractions. I could only find a study on rats, rather than on humans, showing that papaya did not affect prenatal development nor cause maternal toxicity (Schmidt, 1995). Ripe papaya is not known to be a problem. 

Melatonin may help tone the esophageal sphincter (Romm, n.d.a) and has been found to protect the GI mucosa from damage (Kandil et al., 2010). Melatonin has the added benefit of supporting sleep when taken at night at 0.3 mg doses.

Deglycyrrhizinated licorice (DGL) is demulcent, anti-inflammatory, and coating and soothing to the mucous membranes in the GI tract as well as other areas of the body. DGL can soothe irritated mucous membranes. In several clinical studies DGL is found to be either equally effective or more effective than antacids (Morgan et al., 2020; Murray, 2020). Do not use licorice root (Glycyrrhiza glabra) in its raw or whole-plant form during pregnancy, as only the deglycyrrhizinated licorice appears to be safe in pregnancy.

Slippery elm (Ulmus rubra) bark is something to consider as a last resort if none of these other options work. Slippery elm is a wonderful herb, however I do not use it in my practice as it is considered to be an at-risk herb by United Plant Savers. It does have wonderful coating and soothing properties due to its demulcent action, and can work if marshmallow fails. This can be taken as an infusion, powder, or lozenges, which are super convenient. 

CONSTIPATION

Constipation is defined by most as less than three bowel movements a week, although most folks are uncomfortable when not having bowel movements at least four times weekly and others are uncomfortable when not having a daily evacuation. Up to 38% of pregnancies are affected by constipation at some point during the prenatal period (Romm, n.d.a). For people who tend toward constipation at times, this often becomes more of an issue during pregnancy. Watch for hard stool that is difficult to evacuate. It is important to be cautious not to irritate the anus during pregnancy as this can lead to hemorrhoids, which are not only uncomfortable but can also be a concern during the birthing process. 

Elevated progesterone levels during pregnancy can relax smooth muscles, which leads to lower GI motility; this slows transit time and leads to higher water absorption by the intestines, which can lead to constipation. Iron deficiency can also contribute to constipation as can iron supplements. In advanced pregnancy, pressure on the rectum can also contribute to constipation. Constipation can persist postpartum, but usually resolves itself within 3 months after the birth. 


There are some things that one can do to address constipation before or in addition to taking herbs. As with so many things, you may need to experiment with these to find what may be triggers for you and what adjustments may be helpful:

  • Drink plenty of water and other liquids. 
  • Give thought to eating high-fiber foods such as beans, vegetables, and whole grains.  Be sure to try to eat 25-35 g of high-fiber foods daily. 
  • Avoid constipation-causing foods such as bananas, meats, and especially processed dairy. Pay attention to those foods which affect you the most. 
  • Physical activity can reduce constipation, so make sure you are getting some exercise daily. 
  • Sometimes bending the knees and elevating one’s feet during a bowel movement can allow for an easier evacuation. This can be done by placing a stool under one’s feet.  
  • Foods that are considered bulk laxatives, such as whole flax seeds, psyllium seeds or husks, and/or wheat bran, can be supportive for constipation. Flax and psyllium is soaked for several hours in water or apple juice, then ingested with a large amount of liquids. 
  • Magnesium in citrate form in particular has laxative effects for some people. 

Probiotics with multiple strains, especially those with fructooligosaccharides (FOS) can be helpful. Look for probiotics that contain Bifidobacterium, Lactobacillus, and Streptococcus strains as these appear to be the most effective in studies using probiotics to increase bowel movements (Šola et al., 2022). 

Now, on to the herbs! 

Yellow dock (Rumex crispus) root is an aperient and contains anthraquinone, which has mild laxative actions and can stimulate bowel movements without causing cramping. Yellow dock can be used during pregnancy safely and has been traditionally used with no issues by many midwives and herbalists. 

Senna (Senna alexandrina) leaf is an effective, quick-acting, strong laxative. It should only be used sporadically and should not be used daily. It is good for acute situations when one is needing to get their body back into balance while other herbs and diet changes are implemented. Senna can cause intestinal cramping, so consider using it alongside carminative herbs, such as fennel (Foeniculum vulgare) seed, chamomile (Matricaria chamomilla) flower, ginger (Zingiber officinale) rhizome, and anise (Pimpinella anisum) seed to reduce griping. Avoid senna during the first trimester. 

Do not use aloe (Aloe vera) products, cascara (Cascara sagrada) bark, or castor (Ricinus communis) oil during pregnancy, as these are contraindicated (Romm, 2018). There is some clinical evidence that using castor oil at or after 40 weeks gestation is safe and can be used to bring on labor (Moradi et al., 2022). This is not something I do in my practice so I have no further information to share. I tend to avoid the use of castor during any stage of pregnancy. 

YEAST INFECTION

Yeast infections are also called vaginal candidiasis, vulvovaginal candidiasis, or candidal vaginitis. Usually a yeast infection is caused by an imbalance of Candida albicans, but it can also be caused by other species such as Candida glabrata or Candida tropicalis. One may experience white to yellow curd-like discharge with or without smell, redness, swelling, itching, and sensitivity in the vulva and surrounding genital areas. If you are experiencing unusual vaginal discharge be sure to get this checked by a medical professional as discharge can be a sign of other complications. It can be helpful to work with both partners especially if yeast infections are recurring as this can be swapped back and forth through sexual intercourse. Addressing the penis in addition to the vagina is particularly important, as penises do not often show outward signs but do seem to carry and transfer the yeast (Superti & De Seta, 2020). 

Sometimes sexual lubricants can lead to yeast infections, so if you are having recurring yeast issues, then consider changing lubes. Look for a water-based lubricant if possible. Tight-fitting clothing and scented personal products can also contribute to yeast infections and should be avoided. 

There are some dietary strategies that can be utilized, such as removing sugar, including fruit sugar and other sweeteners, as the sugar can feed the yeast imbalance. Simple carbohydrates can also contribute to yeast overgrowth. 

Let’s talk about herbs and supplements that can provide support for yeast infections.

Garlic (Allium sativum) bulb is antifungal and studies have shown that fresh garlic is effective against multiple strains of Candida (Ghannoum, 1998). It is readily available and inexpensive so it can be a great option here. There are a couple of ways to use garlic to address yeast. Garlic can be inserted vaginally. A good trick for this is to tie floss around the garlic bulb, and then the floss can hang outside of the vagina for easy retrieval. Nicking the bulb just a bit with a knife or fingernail before inserting it into the vagina can help to release allicin, one of the active constituents. Keep in mind that garlic can irritate the vaginal walls, and it is not uncommon for it to sting the first time or two it is applied; this is worse for some folks more than others, and others are not bothered by this at all. In my experience the discomfort seems to abate as the yeast diminishes often after the first two applications. One can eat fresh garlic as well, adding it to foods or eating foods such as pesto that are high in garlic. It does not seem to be as effective as applying garlic vaginally, but adding fresh garlic to the diet while combating a yeast infection is definitely helpful. 

Oral probiotics can be helpful for some, as either standalone support or in combination with other approaches. Look for probiotics that include Lactobacillus reuteri and Lacticaseibacillus rhamnosus. Take these daily for at least a week if this is a one-time issue, or for up to 6 months if yeast infections are a recurring problem. Vaginal probiotics that contain Lactobacillus crispatus may also be considered. These are inserted vaginally every other day. One can use a probiotic that is not designed for vaginal applications as long as it has the correct strains of flora. Capsules can be inserted or a sugar-free yogurt can be applied vaginally. This can be a bit messy, so consider using a menstrual pad after application, and doing this right before a nap or bedtime. 

Honey and sugar-free yogurt can be mixed and applied vaginally to assess yeast imbalances. Studies show that this is as effective as clotrimazole (Darvishi et al., 2015), as well as safe during pregnancy (Abdelmonem et al., 2012). To raise the efficacy consider adding the probiotic we just discussed for vaginal purposes. This can be a bit messy, so consider using a menstrual pad after application and doing this right before a nap or bedtime. 

A Word on Safety

Addressing yeast infections can be easy at times and simple solutions can work, such as probiotics, coconut oil (discussed shortly), and garlic. Other times, more aggressive or stronger solutions are needed. Unfortunately some of the best herbs for yeast infections are completely contraindicated during pregnancy. At times one-on-one assessment is warranted to evaluate if a certain solution is right for a particular person and their situation. One must keep in mind the overall constitution of the pregnant client, any medications they are taking, and any pregnancy complications they might be experiencing. The following information is considered to be advanced herbalism. I do understand that some of what I am about to share may be considered controversial, but I am sharing things that have worked for myself and others. It is unfortunate that at times when pregnant choices must be made regarding how to address certain illnesses and situations that arise. It is up to the individual if they feel more comfortable using conventional medicine or herbs. If someone is more comfortable using conventional medicine in certain situations then those thoughts should be honored. Please be sure to check in with your midwife or OB-GYN if you are unsure if any of the following options are right for you. None of these ideas are long-term solutions, and if after a couple days of using them, they are not working, please be sure to discontinue and seek appropriate medical care. 

Vaginal suppositories can be made with tea tree (Melaleuca alternifolia) and/or lavender (Lavandula angustifolia) essential oils in a coconut oil base (Romm, 2018). Studies show that tea tree oil is effective against several species of Candida, both in its ability to break through the plasma membrane wall, as well as inhibit replication (Carson et al., 2006). Coconut oil has also been shown to be effective against Candida albicans (Shino et al., 2016), which makes this a great carrier for the essential oils, or in cases of mild yeast infections it may be effective on its own. I do note that in some instances oil can aggravate a yeast infection; if you have any indications this is a consideration, please avoid use of oils vaginally. Safety note: if using any essential oils be sure to follow proper dilution guidelines. 

Goldenseal (Hydrastis canadensis) rhizome is an herb to consider here for vaginal use only. This can not be used internally during pregnancy and should only be used vaginally for short amounts of time after one is at least 20 weeks into gestation (Yarnell, n.d.). Goldenseal is not only antifungal, but research has shown that it is effective against Candida albicans (Liu et al., 2016). The constituent berberine, present in goldenseal, is even effective where fluconazole-resistant yeast is present, and is also effective against Candida albicans biofilms which may be why goldenseal works so well (da Silva et al., 2016). Goldenseal is also a mucous membrane tonic, and as the vaginal walls are mucous membranes this is an ideal herb to consider using in this situation. I have used this successfully with no complications with multiple clients, but this is advanced herbalism, and should be treated as such. Only use this where other options have failed. Safety and sourcing note: Goldenseal is expensive so I try not to use it that often. Only organically grown goldenseal should be used; never purchase wildcrafted goldenseal due to overharvesting concerns. It’s important to check in with your midwife or OB-GYN to determine if goldenseal might be safe for you. 

COLDS AND FLU

Colds and flu are both caused by viruses that affect the upper respiratory tract. Colds tend to be more mild, with less symptoms than the flu. The flu tends to cause fever, while colds do not. Much of the time both colds and the flu can be addressed at home and do not need medical attention. However, watch for signs of shortness of breath, coughing of blood, severe vomiting, dizziness or fainting, chest pain, and uncontrollable or long-lasting fevers—if these occur, seek appropriate medical attention. 

Echinacea (Echinacea purpurea, Echinacea angustifolia, and Echinacea pallida) root and aerial parts can be a first line of immune defense for many illnesses. Studies show that use of echinacea during pregnancy does not affect the health of the baby and is not associated with increased risk of malformations (Gallo et al., 2000), so this is a great herb to use in pregnancy or lactation. Usually taken at the first sign of illness, echinacea stimulates phagocytosis (Melchart et al., 1995), inhibits hyaluronidase activity (Facino et al., 1993), and downregulates inflammatory cytokines (Dapas et al., 2014), all of which are thought to contribute to echinacea’s ability to help the body fight colds, the flu, herpes simplex virus (HSV), and certain strains of Staphylococcus and Streptococcus bacteria (Hudson, 2012; Winston & Kuhn, 2008; Hudson 2011). Often echinacea works best in frequent doses, such as 4-6 times daily. 

Ginger (Zingiber officinale) rhizome, most especially fresh rhizome, is helpful for fever, aches, and coughs, and can lower the severity of colds (Winston & Kuhn, 2008) due to its diaphoretic, antispasmodic, and antimicrobial actions. It can be useful for both colds and the flu. I like fresh rhizome infusion or decoction best in this situation; if you are looking for fever support, ginger must be used warm or hot. 

Elderberry and elderflower (Sambucus canadensis, S. nigra) both exhibit antiviral properties. The berries in particular can lower both the severity of a cold or flu as well as the length of time one is ill, as the berries can interfere with viral replication (Winston & Kuhn, 2008). The flower has some antibacterial actions and is used for fever support as a diaphoretic. Both the berry and flower are safe in pregnancy (Winston & Kuhn, 2008). The berry needs to be cooked or dried in order to be safe for consumption. 

Nasal rinses using a neti pot or other nasal irrigation tools can be helpful in reducing nasal catarrh and easing inflammation of the nasal pathways, therefore allowing for easier nasal breathing. The neti pot can be filled with a saline solution only or a small amount of echinacea can be added to the saline solution. Care should be taken when using the neti pot, to not get the wash in the ear canal. 

Vitamin C can be safely taken during pregnancy. Daily recommendations for pregnancy are anywhere between 200-400 mg depending on the source, with amounts up to 2,000 mg in acute situations for short periods of time are considered safe (Brown & Wright, 2020). At times higher doses of vitamin C can lead to loose stools. If this happens lower the dose to an amount that does not affect the bowels. 

Honey and lemon juice can be added to hot water to soothe an irritated throat or calm a cough. Raw honey has so many helpful actions that apply here: honey is antibacterial (against both gram-positive and gram-negative bacteria), antiviral, anti-inflammatory (Eteraf et al., 2013), coating and lubricating, and it has an affinity for the lungs. Honey also contains many minerals and vitamins so it can be useful for electrolyte balancing if there is any concern regarding dehydration (Eteraf et al., 2013). Honey has been used as a folk remedy by many for this purpose. One can add ginger to a cup of hot honey-lemon water to add taste or further herbal support. Or use herbal honey (that has herbs that are safe for pregnancy) and add lemon and hot water. 

Raw garlic (Allium sativum) bulb can be used for colds and the flu. Fresh garlic contains allicin, which has significant antimicrobial activity against both gram-negative and gram-positive bacteria (Ankri & Mirelman, 1999). Garlic has an affinity for the lungs and expectorant action, so it may be helpful for bronchitis, coughs, and pneumonia. One can eat the cloves whole or chop and add to food. Eating foods with high quantities of garlic like pesto or hummus is also an easy way to consume. Extra raw garlic can be added to foods to raise the content. Garlic honey is a wonderful way to get fresh garlic into folks and can be made into a nice syrup to support coughs. 

Cleavers (Galium aparine) aerial parts are a mild lymph mover to consider when sick, especially when swollen glands are present. I really like to include lymph movers in all cold and flu formulas, as the lymphatic system is like a garbage truck and helps to remove waste products from the body. Cleavers are a cooling lymphatic, so it is especially appropriate with hot conditions like fevers, and is most effective when used fresh.

Lemon balm (Melissa officinalis) aerial parts have antiviral, calming, diaphoretic actions. This herb mixes well with elderflower for fevers. Due to its antispasmodic lemon balm also has relaxing effects in the respiratory system, and can help to ease uncontrollable coughing. It tastes great, so is a good addition to infusions for both actions and flavor. Lemon balm is best used as a fresh plant extract or freshly dried. 

Mullein (Verbascum thapsus) leaf is ideal for dry, deep, tickly coughs, as it supports and lubricates lungs due to its mucilaginous properties. It may reduce tightness in the throat, open the lungs and support easier breathing, lubricate the lungs, relax a tight throat and/or chest, reduce coughing, and bring feelings of opening the sinuses. Mullein is considered to be a tonic for the lungs, and can support relapsing or chronic lung conditions. Note that it is important to strain mullein tea through a fine filter before consuming to remove the tiny hairs which can irritate the mouth or throat.

CONCLUSION

I have covered multiple conditions today that commonly arise in pregnancy.  The herbs I have discussed are herbs that I have used in my clinical practice over the years with good results and offer safe and effective support during this exciting but somewhat complicated time!
