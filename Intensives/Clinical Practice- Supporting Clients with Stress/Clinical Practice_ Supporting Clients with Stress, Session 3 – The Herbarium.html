Clinical Practice: Supporting Clients with Stress, Session 3

The Role of Nervines

In the first two sessions of this special intensive series, we looked at how stress and feeling overwhelmed can affect many different systems in the body and how we might choose herbs to deal with several different areas. We will always want to suggest herbs to support the adrenal function and we will usually want to give herbs to reduce nervous system problems like anxiety, insomnia, or irritability. After that, we look at the individual client and the other organ systems that may have been affected due to underlying chronic conditions—like digestion or the skin, for example.

In this session, I am going to dedicate my time to looking at a class of herbs called nervines. These herbs are prized mainly for acting on the nervous system—although, as with any herbs, they are usually great multi-taskers and work on other systems as well. Nervines are herbs that we rely on to reduce nervous system symptoms such as anxiety, palpitations, feelings of stress, irritability, and so on. They are supportive of  the nervous system and help it recover after a period of stress. They can range from simple tonics like flowering oat tops (Avena sativa), to calming herbs like chamomile (Matricaria recutita), to the heavier, more sedating herbs like valerian (Valeriana officinalis). I usually ask people to imagine that the volume has been turned up on their nervous system and that nervines have the ability to turn the volume down. In this article I have simply picked the herbs that I use most often and have found to be most reliable.

Now that we know the effects stress can have on the body, as well as all the diverse skills and information we can pull together to help our clients, it is time to turn our attention directly to the herbs. Often herbalists will jump to those first—so I hope that you have learned we have a lot to consider even before we get to this step. Think of it as the endpoint of our first meeting with a client, rather than something we need to pin down early on. Often I find that if I leave choosing the herbs until the end of the meeting that gives room for the whole protocol to come together naturally. While I take a case history, as a person tells me his or her story, I am usually jotting down names of herbs in the margin of my notes. That way I do not have to hold the names in my head, meaning that I can still be totally focused on what the client is telling me. Added to that, the herbs are sitting next to the notes about the relevant signs and symptoms, so when I review the notes later I can see why I may have thought of that herb in that moment. The following are my top herb choices starting with the main players vervain (Verbena officinalis) and valerian and working down to the more specific, secondary herbs.

Vervain (Verbena officinalis)

A very important plant in our pharmacopoeia and in folklore throughout the world, vervain has been held in high regard by cultures from Ancient Egypt, Ancient Rome and Druid tradition. It is extremely good at helping with stress symptoms on many different levels. It is my first choice for people who need to let go, i.e., the people who are hanging on in order to remain in control of something, or who are stuck in a situation that is no longer serving them.

During my time in university, our lecturer told us to think about vervain as having one gentle hand on the small of your back, pushing you through the doorway to a new situation, and another hand peeling your fingers one by one off the doorframe as you grip it in fear. It is such a descriptive way of remembering what vervain is good at and I have never forgotten it.

During an herb tasting session I ran some years ago, an elderly participant with known shoulder and neck problems reported that she felt the constant tension in her neck was significantly eased by using this herb. I have since used it often in that way to good effect.

Vervain is thought to have a mild antidepressant action and is specific to soothe anxiety and nervous exhaustion following long-term stress (Chevallier, 2001). In my practice, I also use it to help deepen sleep, as I was taught by my herbal mentor. The mechanism of this is unknown, but it seems to me that if it is able to reduce muscular tension and reduce adrenal response, and therefore cortisol levels in the body, then this may be part of the reason. However, it seems to be a particular character of vervain that the quality of sleep a person gets improves.

From time to time we come across herbs that partner up really well. In this case vervain and milky oat tops are such a partnership. This is easily remembered with their rhyming Latin names—Avena and Verbena. Milky oat tops are particularly indicated for people who have dealt with stress for a long time and are suffering from nervous debility (Chevallier, 2001). If they just feel that they cannot go on, that their situation is intolerable, then milky oat tops is the remedy for them. Be aware that oat straw is a fine remedy for the musculoskeletal system, but will do little for the nervous system. With the actual grain of the plant being an important food, oats provide a key lesson in how each part of the plant is important and often chemically quite different in make-up, and thus, in function.

Valerian (Valeriana officinalis)

Valerian is a plant that brings out strong reactions in people—just like that strong odor from its fibrous root. People either love it or hate it—the reaction is rarely without some exclamation.

There is a great myth surrounding valerian in clinical practice that some people cannot take it and that instead of the wonderfully calming effect it usually elicits, it causes those people to climb the walls with agitation. In my experience this can be true—particularly for hot, Pitta type constitutions. Remember that Pitta is the Ayurvedic constitution dominated by fire and water. The energetics of valerian are hot too, so it can exacerbate the Pitta type and fuel their distress—particularly if agitation and irritability are present. In these cases, opt for more cooling remedies. Vervain would be a good choice here.

It has also been discussed in herbal circles whether it is possible that valerian is an herb that is sometimes suitable for a person but at other times not. During my studies we often found that when a person was strongly attracted to the smell it would work well for them, but if they were strongly repulsed by the smell then it was more likely to cause an adverse reaction. I still think there is something in this approach, although there is yet to be any research that I know of on this theory. I certainly find that it works for me in practice, and personally. If I am unsure whether valerian is indicated, I will often ask my client to smell the bottle of tincture and their reaction will usually clearly show me whether this would be a herb to suggest to them or not.

Valerian is considered to decrease Vata and Kapha and increase Pitta (Frawley & Lad, 2001). Lad (2001) says that “valerian is one of the best herbs for Vatagenic nervous disorders… It clears the nerve channels of accumulated Vata. At the same time, owing to the large amount of the earth element contained within it, it is grounding” ( p. 151). This makes valerian a very useful herb for Vata type people who may be considering using anti-anxiety medication or medication for insomnia and need to stop taking it. A class of drugs called benzodiazepines are often prescribed for these problems. These drugs inhibit the breakdown of the neurotransmitter GABAA and this helps reduce anxiety and insomnia (Ashton, 2002). Valerian is thought to work in a similar way and research shows that it can reduce insomnia. It can be indicated during the withdrawal of benzodiazepines as it can help to keep symptoms under control while the body gets used to the withdrawal (Lopez-Peig et al., 2012; Patocka & Jackl, 2010).

In Europe today it is the most common non-prescription sedative, as research has confirmed that it “‘encourages sleep, improves sleep quality and lowers blood pressure”’ (Chevallier, 2001, p. 148). In Germany, in particular, it is more likely to be prescribed than Valium.. The key ingredient here is valerenic acid that has been shown to inhibit the neurotransmitter gamma-Aminobutyric acid (GABA) from breaking down. GABA has a calming effect on the mind, which is why valerian is particularly prized for its ability to “reduce mental over-activity and nervous excitability, helping people who find it hard to ‘switch off’” (Chevallier, 2001, p.148). Its effect is calming to the mind rather than the body (Chevallier, 1996) and it can be used without the drowsiness or addiction attributed to many pharmaceutical sedatives.

As well as relieving the many symptoms of anxiety such as “tremors, panic, palpitations and sweating” (Chevallier, 2001, p. 148), research has shown that valerian can reduce blood pressure and heart rate as physiological responses to stress (Cropley et al., 2002). It also has a relaxing effect on over-contracted muscles, and is therefore helpful for “shoulder and neck tension, asthma, colic, IBS, period pain and muscle spasms” (Chevallier, 2001, p. 148). Hoffmann (1990) says that as a pain reliever “it is most indicated where that pain is associated with tension” ( p. 238). In clinical practice we can use this herb to good effect where there are systemic flare-ups that cause pain, like irritable bowel syndrome (IBS), as we discussed in the first video. By choosing valerian in that situation we can begin to make an impact on the negative feedback loop—stress causing pain, causing anxiety, reducing sleep capacity, and causing further stress. This will help us to break the pattern and give an opportunity for relief and resolution

Borage (Borago officinalis)

Back in 1597, herbalist John Gerard quoted a saying that was old even in his time: “Ego borago gaudia semper ago” which means “I, Borage, always bring courage” and I absolutely have found that to be true even in this modern day. Borage is an excellent remedy for people who have found that their boundaries have collapsed—that they cannot say “no” but keep piling on the pressure and doing things for other people while they become more and more exhausted. It is an important herb for adrenal support and certainly one of my first choices. It is restorative to the adrenal cortex (Hoffmann, 1990) and this will almost always be of use when a client is dealing with issues caused by stress.

Borage contains pyrrolizidine alkaloids which, in isolation, are toxic to the liver (Chevallier, 2001). As such it is recommended that it be used in small doses (10ml of borage tincture per week would be my maximum) and only for a short period of time—like 6 weeks. I don’t find that any more than this is necessary in any case. It would also be inappropriate to use borage with children or with a person who had any liver impairment as a precaution.

When a person begins to use borage, he or she often starts saying “no”—to people and to situations. I often warn clients that those people who are used to them always saying “yes” may not like this! When working with borage I often explain to people that it is a bit like you are sitting on your front porch, looking out at your garden and the white picket fence. Borage makes sure your fence is intact; it creates the space you need from other people and their problems. However, there is a gate in the fence—borage allows it to open for the people you want it to open for. It does not shut out the world, but it does reinforce your personal boundaries.

If borage is not available, or if it is contraindicated, my next go-to herb for adrenal health is Withania somnifera (ashwagandha).

Ashwagandha (Withania somnifera)

A wonderful adrenal tonic for exhaustion with anxiety, ashwagandha comes from the Ayurvedic tradition but is becoming equally prized in the West. Ashwagandha is particularly good for anxious Vata types who are really unable to function because of nervousness. In fact it is thought to be the best rejuvenative herb for the Vata constitution (Frawley & Lad, 2001). It can help to give a better quality of sleep, reduce over-thinking, and allow a sense of calm to return. It has been called “Indian Ginseng” because in the Ayurvedic tradition it is used for its similar ability to improve vitality as other ginsengs are used in Chinese and American traditional medicine. Indeed the name ashwagandha translates as “horse smell”—not only because of the smell of the plant but also alluding to a horse’s strength (Chevallier, 2001).

The species name somnifera also reminds us that ashwagandha can be used to “promote deep, dreamless sleep” (Frawley & Lad, 2001, p. 161). Part of the reason that the plant can affect our feeling of calm is that it increases the availability of dopamine, a neurotransmitter known to elicit feelings of calm and contentment and it appears to mimic the action of the neurotransmitter GABA, which relaxes the body (Mars, 2007). It never ceases to amaze me how modern science proves the chemical interactions behind traditions that are thousands of years old. In this case there is evidence of use over 3,000 years (Mars, 2007).

Skullcap (Scutellaria lateriflora)

Skullcap is one of my all-time favorite nervines and I always know when it is going to be necessary because it works so well with people who get all “hot and bothered” and irritable under pressure. If I hear a person talking about being “nippy,” shouting, and getting annoyed with the tiniest things, then I know that skullcap is a must in that client’s mix.

It is also a very nourishing herb—I think of it as feeding the nervous system. This is particularly important if someone has been under stress for a long time. Hoffmann (1990) explains this as “relaxing states of nervous tension whilst at the same time renewing and revivifying the central nervous system” (p. 233). This ability to reduce excitability while improving overall function makes this a true tonic to the nervous system. Mabey (1988) suggests it is “good for treating anxiety, depression, insomnia, and nervous headaches. Its bitter taste is also strengthening and stimulating to the digestion” (p. 73). As we know from the first video, states of anxiety and stress will have a detrimental effect on the digestion, so an herb that can calm nervous agitation while improving digestion is very useful.

Remember that people who lean toward fiery anger and irritation during stressful times will tend to be Pitta types, or people with Pitta derangement. One notable Ayurvedic resource explains that skullcap

…has special properties for lowering high Pitta, and for helping reduce the fiery emotions of anger, jealousy and hatred. It calms the heart and dispels excessive desire…promotes awareness, clarity, and detachment. It allays excitability and restores control over deranged sensory and motor functions (Frawley & Lad, 2001, p. 147).

This is certainly the kind of emotional effect I have seen skullcap exert and viewing it within the Ayurvedic model really helps me to see how this is possible.

California Poppy (Eschscholzia californica)

This delightful herb is always my choice when a person is unable to stop overthinking and worrying about things. I always ask my clients whether their internal radio station is already on and chattering away when they first wake up, or whether they lie awake at night playing scenarios over and over. If they do, then California poppy is the herb for them.

A traditional remedy for children, this herb brings a lot of light-heartedness to the situation. It is really useful for people who need to snap out of their internal dialogue and who need help to see issues clearly and take focused action. It can also help with pain that is getting in the way of rest and is excellent for people who get anxious about their pain (Kress, 2011). A study in France confirmed the traditional usage of Californian poppy showing that extracts were indeed sedative, reduced anxiety levels, and were non-toxic (Hanus, et al., 2004). Although closely related to the opium poppy (Papaver somniferum) the Californian poppy contains no opiates and rather than disorienting the user, like its much stronger cousin, it tends to normalize psychological function (Chevallier, 2001). It does contain a number of alkaloids that have sedative and hypnotic effects but they belong to a group of alkaloids that are much less potent than morphine and codeine. This safe, gentle remedy can be a useful addition to a protocol for someone who just cannot switch off.

Conclusion

There is a vast array of nervines available to us and these are just some of my own favorites. They will usually be the grounding force in a protocol for a person dealing with feeling overwhelmed and stress. They can be put together to produce the full protocol—although in my experience that is not often the case—or they can be partnered with herbs to soothe the other systems in the body that have been pulled out of balance by the situation the client is facing.

As we know, herbs can rarely be labeled as having one singular function, or relating to one single body system. They are wonderfully complex in their action and affinities. Therefore, when choosing herbs to complement nervines in other systems in the body, it is important to ensure that they are compatible. For example, if I have someone with very bad itchy eczema caused by stress, I would not choose to give them ephedra as an antihistamine, which can cause feelings of anxiety and palpitations, but I would give them calming chamomile instead. Chamomile can be used to reduce anxiety while working hard to improve digestion and reduce itchy skin conditions. So for each system for which you are suggesting herbs, ensure that, where you can, the herbs you choose either have a calming element to them, or at the least will not work against the overall calming effect that you want to help create.

We must never forget the complexity of herbs, or of human beings. Although we can theorize about the best herbs to create a balanced mix, it is rarely so straightforward in our clinics. For further insight, the next session, the final one in this series, will look at real life case histories. I will be sharing with you the real scenarios I have faced, what worked—and what did not—so that you can get a better idea of just how your learning can help you deliver the information needed to the people who come to you for help.
