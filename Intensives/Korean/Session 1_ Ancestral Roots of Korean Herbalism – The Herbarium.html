Session 1: Ancestral Roots of Korean Herbalism

60 minutes · Easy

South Korea, referred to as the “Land of the Morning Calm,” features unique and diverse geography that has played a significant role in shaping the nation’s history and culture. Nestled on the eastern part of the Asian continent, South Korea shares its borders with North Korea to the north, China to the west, and Japan to the east, with the Yellow Sea to the west and the East China Sea to the south. The Korean Peninsula is characterized by mountainous terrain, with approximately 70% of the land covered by mountains. These rugged landscapes have not only influenced the country’s traditional agricultural practices but have also served as natural barriers that have shaped the regional identities within the peninsula.

A Geography of Health

The history of Korea traces back to ancient times, dating back 700,000 years to Manchuria and the Korean Peninsula. Over the centuries, the Korean people developed a rich and distinctive culture influenced by their geographic surroundings. The country’s position between China and Japan has not only led to cultural exchanges but has also at times made it a battleground for regional powers.

South Korea’s unique geography has played a pivotal role in shaping the country’s vibrant and health-conscious culinary and medicinal traditions. The mountainous terrain, diverse ecosystems, and coastal areas have all contributed to the availability of a wide variety of herbal ingredients that form the foundation of Korean cuisine and herbalism.

One remarkable aspect of South Korea’s geography is the presence of Jeju Island, located off the southern coast. Designated as one of the New 7 Wonders of Nature, Jeju is a volcanic island known for its stunning landscapes, including abundant waterfalls, craters, and unique rock formations. 

The Korean Peninsula is home to a diverse range of flora, including numerous beneficial plants that have been utilized for centuries in traditional Korean tradition, referred to as Hanbang. The rich biodiversity of the mountains and forests has provided herbalists with a vast array of plants to incorporate into herbal preparations promoting a holistic approach to health and wellbeing.

Climate’s Impact on Culinary Traditions

South Korea experiences distinct seasons due to its temperate climate, including cold winters and hot summers. This climate diversity, along with fertile soils, has allowed for the cultivation of a wide range of crops. Traditional Korean cuisine emphasizes the use of seasonal and locally sourced ingredients, promoting a connection to nature and an awareness of the benefits of consuming foods in harmony with the environment.

The mountainous landscape of South Korea historically posed challenges for agriculture, leading to creative methods of food preservation. Fermentation became a key technique, not only for preserving food but also for enhancing its nutritional value. Kimchi, a staple in Korean cuisine, is a prime example. This fermented dish, typically made from cabbage and radishes, not only imparts a unique flavor but also introduces beneficial probiotics to the diet, contributing to digestive health.

Coastal and Cultural Influences 

With extensive coastlines along the Yellow Sea and the East China Sea, Korea boasts a cuisine in which seafood plays a crucial role. The availability of fresh fish and other marine products has led to the development of a variety of seafood-based dishes, rich in omega-3 fatty acids and other essential nutrients. The incorporation of seafood contributes to the overall health-conscious nature of Korean culinary traditions.

South Korea’s geographic location between China and Japan has facilitated cultural reciprocity, including the exchange of culinary and medicinal knowledge. This has led to the integration of diverse ingredients and practices into Korean traditions, creating a unique blend of influences that contribute to the country’s health-conscious lifestyle.

South Korea’s geography has provided the raw materials and environmental conditions necessary for the development of culinary and herbalism traditions that emphasize the use of natural, seasonal, and locally sourced ingredients. One example of South Korea’s rich culinary and herbalism traditions deeply influenced by its geography is the universal use of ginseng (Panax ginseng) root. South Korea’s favorable climate and mountainous terrain provide an ideal environment for cultivating this beneficial herb, and the country has become renowned for producing the best quality ginseng in the world. Ginseng, which promotes vitality and immunity, has been a staple in Korean tradition for centuries.

The geographical conditions, including the fertile soil and temperate climate, contribute to the unique flavor profile and potent beneficial properties of Korean ginseng. Cultivated in the southern regions of the Korean Peninsula, ginseng has become a symbol of health and longevity in Korean culture.

In Korean cuisine, ginseng is not only used in traditional herbal teas but is also incorporated into various dishes, such as chicken-ginseng soup (Samgyetang). This dish replenishes energy and boosts stamina. The cultural significance of ginseng in South Korea not only highlights the importance of natural and locally sourced ingredients but also reflects the deep connection between geography, traditional herbalism, and culinary practices in shaping the country’s rich cultural heritage. The integration of these elements into daily life reflects a holistic approach to health that has been passed down through generations, contributing to the vibrancy and uniqueness of South Korea’s cultural heritage.

In exploring Korea’s rich history, it is essential to delve into its traditional practices, including the profound influence of herbalism. The use of healthful foods and plants has deep roots in Korean culture, reflecting a holistic approach to wellbeing. From the preparation of the iconic dish kimchi, with its fermented vegetables and spices, to the use of herbal preparations passed down through generations, South Korea’s unique geography has contributed to the development of a vibrant and health-conscious culinary and medicinal tradition.

Symbolism of South Korea’s National Flag

An herbalist’s understanding of the meaning of the Korean flag can deepen their appreciation for the cultural, philosophical, and symbolic underpinnings that influence traditional herbal practices in Korea. This knowledge not only adds a layer of cultural richness to the herbalist’s approach but also fosters a more holistic and culturally sensitive perspective in the practice of herbalism.

The South Korean flag, known as Taegukgi, is a rich tapestry of symbolism that intricately weaves together the nation’s cultural, philosophical, and historical elements. Its design is a reflection of deep-rooted principles and values held by the Korean people. The flag’s field, predominantly white, not only serves as a canvas for the vibrant symbols but also holds cultural significance, representing peace and purity in Korean tradition.

At the heart of the Taegukgi is a circle, divided into two equal parts of red and blue, embodying the yin and yang philosophy. This symbolizes the eternal dance of opposites—light and darkness, positive and negative—illustrating the interconnectedness and balance of dual cosmic forces. The constant movement within the circle signifies the dynamic nature of the universe and the perpetual pursuit of harmony.

The four trigrams adorning each corner of the flag add layers of meaning and depth to its symbolism. These trigrams, rooted in ancient Chinese cosmology, encapsulate a multitude of concepts. They represent the natural elements of heaven, earth, water, and fire, highlighting the interconnectedness between the physical and metaphysical realms. Additionally, they embody celestial bodies, such as heaven, sun, moon, and earth, symbolizing the cosmic order and the cyclical nature of life.

Further enriching the symbolism, the trigrams signify the four seasons—spring, summer, autumn, and winter—acknowledging the cyclical rhythm of nature and the changing phases of life. They also encapsulate the four virtues of humanity, justice, courtesy, and intelligence, reflecting the moral and ethical foundations of Korean society. The trigrams extend their symbolism to encompass the familial unit, representing father, mother, daughter, and son, underlining the importance of family values in Korean culture.

Moreover, the cardinal directions—north, south, east, and west—find representation in the trigrams, emphasizing the comprehensive nature of the symbolism. This intricate fusion of elements encapsulates the essence of Korea’s cultural identity, philosophies, and historical values, making the Taegukgi more than simply a flag, but a visual narrative of the nation’s collective consciousness. The South Korean flag, with its nuanced symbolism, stands as a visual representation of the country’s profound cultural heritage and its commitment to harmony, balance, and unity.

For an herbalist, understanding the meaning of the Korean flag and the cultural and symbolic significance it holds can provide a deeper contextual understanding of the traditional herbal practices and medicinal traditions in Korea. Next, we go into several reasons why such a perspective is important.

Korean Herbalism in Context

The symbolism embedded in the Korean flag is deeply rooted in the country’s cultural and historical context. Understanding these symbols helps an herbalist appreciate the interconnectedness of nature, humanity, and the cosmos in Korean culture. This cultural awareness can inform the herbalist’s approach to traditional healing practices and enhance their ability to integrate holistic principles into herbalism.

  • Philosophical Foundations: The yin and yang philosophy represented by the red and blue circle at the center of the flag embodies principles of balance, harmony, and continual movement. Herbalists can draw parallels between this philosophy and the principles of balance in traditional herbalism, emphasizing the importance of harmonizing opposing forces in the body for overall wellbeing.
  • Symbolism of Natural Elements: The trigrams on the flag symbolize natural elements, celestial bodies, seasons, virtues, family roles, and cardinal directions. Herbalists can use this symbolism to deepen their understanding of the interconnectedness of these elements in traditional Korean herbalism. For instance, the representation of natural elements may guide the herbalist in selecting herbs that align with specific elemental qualities for a more balanced approach to healing.

Approaches to Holistic Healing: The four virtues highlighted in the trigrams—humanity, justice, courtesy, and intelligence—can inspire herbalists to approach healing not just as a physical process but as a holistic endeavor that encompasses mental, emotional, and spiritual wellbeing. This broader perspective aligns with traditional Korean herbalism, which often emphasizes a comprehensive approach to health.

Cultural Sensitivity: Understanding the traditional symbolism of the Korean flag allows herbalists to approach their practice with sensitivity and respect. This awareness can foster stronger connections with clients and patients, especially those from Korean backgrounds, as the herbalist demonstrates an appreciation for the cultural context that shapes their approach to healing.

Incorporating Traditional Wisdom: The Korean flag represents a visual manifestation of traditional wisdom and values. Herbalists can draw inspiration from this symbolism to enrich their practice with traditional knowledge, incorporating elements that have been respected and revered in Korean culture for centuries.

A Legacy of Health

Korean herbalism has a rich and storied history that spans more than 2,000 years, with its roots extending back to the Three Kingdoms period (57 BCE – 668 CE). Initially influenced by China’s traditional medicine system, known as Hanbang, Korean herbalism evolved over time to forge its distinctive path. During the Three Kingdoms period, the use of herbalism was entwined with the broader context of East Asian traditional medicine, and knowledge of beneficial plants and their applications flourished. What distinguishes Korean herbalism is its incorporation of native herbs and plants unique to the Korean peninsula. This amalgamation of Indigenous flora with the principles of Hanbang set the stage for a holistic and culturally nuanced approach to herbalism. 

Korean herbalism is not confined solely to the use of herbs; it integrates other traditional healing modalities such as acupuncture and moxibustion. Moxibustion involves the burning of herbs like mugwort (Artemisia princeps) either above or on the skin, enhancing the effects of other herbal approaches. Throughout Korean history, traditional herbalism has been deeply intertwined with the pursuit of wellness and longevity. It has long been customary for individuals to incorporate herbal preparations into their daily lives, reflecting a commitment to proactive healthcare and the intrinsic belief in the health-giving properties of nature. Today, Korean herbalism continues to thrive, blending ancient wisdom with contemporary practices to provide a holistic approach to health and wellbeing.

Korean Folktales

Korean folktales reflect our beliefs about nature, the relationship between humanity and nature, and how our connections within family and community impact Mother Earth. These traditional narratives, passed down through generations, serve as repositories of cultural heritage, moral and ethical lessons, and cultural resilience and adaptability.

Though in folktales Mother Earth sometimes brings disasters to humanity, She is also the source of our nourishment. Therefore, humans must learn ways to harmonize with nature as we are an integral part of the ecosystem and must do our part to preserve and respect Mother Earth. Below I’ll share a folktale about the founding of the first Korean kingdom that also features two essential herbs used in Korean herbalism.

Folktale of the Bear, Tiger, Garlic, and Mugwort

Long ago, the God of the Heavens, Hwanin (환인), had a son, Hwanung (환웅), who had a strong desire to rule Earth. As he gazed down at the mountains of Earth from the Heavens, Hwanung asked for his father’s permission to descend upon Earth and rule over it, which his father granted.

Hwanung led a group of 3,000 people and brought the gods of rain, wind, and clouds with him to Earth. They descended to Shindansu (the Holy Tree of Life) on Mount Taebaek where he began overseeing human world affairs like food, life, disease, punishment, and morality. 

​After Hwanung was established as the leader of Earth, a bear and a tiger prayed to Hwanung with their desire to become humans. Upon hearing their wishes, Hwanung gave them herbs—mugwort (Artemisia princeps) and garlic (Allium sativum)—and told the bear and the tiger, “If you avoid sunlight for 100 days and only eat garlic and mugwort, I will grant your wish.” After agreeing, the bear and the tiger took up residence in a cave.

Many days passed and the tiger was feeling deprived and fled the cave while the bear persevered and survived in the cave for 100 days. Hwanung kept his promise and transformed the bear, who was named Ungnyeo (Bear Woman).

Ungnyeo became sad that she did not have a companion and prayed again to Hwanung, this time for a son. Moved by her prayers, Hwanung transformed himself into a human and married Ungnyeo. The pair had a son, who was named Dangun.

As the first human prince of the land, Dangun eventually established his own kingdom. This kingdom was called Gojoseon (Ancient Joseon) and became the first Korean kingdom. The Dangun folktale represents the union of nature—the God of Heaven and Mother Earth—and the importance of nature and the cosmos.

Folktale adapted from German (2020).

Koreans have traditionally used herbs since ancient times not only for health and dietary reasons but also in rituals for restoring balance. The plants in the folktale, mugwort and garlic, are essential herbs that have many health benefits such as improving sleep and immunity, as well as easing inflammation and supporting blood flow. In the next sections, we’ll discuss both of these herbs and some of their traditional uses.

Mugwort (Artemisia princeps): Ssuk

Korean name: 쑥 [ssuk]

Family: Asteraceae

Binomial: Artemisia princeps

Other Names: Korean mugwort, Japanese mugwort, Korean wormwood

Parts Used: leaf, root

Taste and Energetics: bitter, pungent, aromatic, cool, dry

Properties: antimicrobial, aromatic, bitter tonic, mild choleretic, and emmenagogue

Indicated uses: Internally, mugwort is commonly used as a flavoring ingredient as well as a side dish in Korean cuisine. It can also be used as a tea. Externally, mugwort is used in moxibustion and skin care, as a bath soak, or for its essential oil.

Nutrients: calcium, magnesium, vitamin C, amino acids (Trendafilova et al., 2021).

Korean mugwort (Artemisia princeps) or ssuk in Korean, holds profound cultural, culinary, and beneficial importance in Korea. This aromatic herb has been an integral part of Korean traditional medicine for centuries, celebrated for its purported health benefits. Rich in antioxidants and nutrients, mugwort is believed to have anti-inflammatory and immune-boosting properties. It’s commonly used for calming nerves, soothing menstrual pains, and promoting sleep. The herb is quite complex with over 75 unique chemicals that have been identified (Skenderi, 2004). When consumed, its bitter and choleretic actions support digestion and its aromatic profile has relaxing properties. Due to its emmenagogue action, it should be avoided during pregnancy. It should not be used long-term or at high doses. 

Beyond these uses, mugwort plays a significant role in Korean cuisine, imparting a distinctive flavor to various dishes. One of the most iconic applications is in the preparation of ssukcha, a type of tea made from dried mugwort leaves. This tea is enjoyed for its earthy, slightly bitter taste and is often consumed for its potential digestive and relaxation benefits. In Korean cultural practices, mugwort also features prominently in festivals and rituals, symbolizing purity and protection against evil spirits. The multifaceted importance of Korean mugwort underscores its significance as a versatile herb deeply ingrained in the country’s cultural, culinary, and healing traditions.

In Eastern traditions, mugwort is used in a practice called moxibustion, which uses the dried herb on particular points of the body to stimulate the flow of energy, qi, which we will discuss in more detail below. The herb is placed directly on the skin, attached to acupuncture needles, or rolled into sticks and waved gently over specific areas of the body. In all instances, the herb is ignited and releases heat as it burns. Not only is the herb itself believed to have beneficial properties when used in this manner, but the heat released from the herb in a precise area is also a key aspect of this approach. 

Mugwort is also considered a dream herb and is often used to address sleep issues. It has a sweet and floral scent and is excellent for use in aromatherapy and teas. Its aromatic qualities are used during ceremonies and meditation practices. But mugwort alone is unlikely to make you a lucid dreamer overnight. This is a practice that requires consistent effort, including mindset-managing and intention-setting practices before bed, such as breathwork, visualization, and meditation. Having an evening ritual will help track your progress. Below are a simple mugwort tea recipe and a bath ritual to try.

Korean Mugwort (Ssukcha) Tea Recipe

Ssukcha, a tea made from roasted Korean mugwort leaves, but if you do not have access to Korean mugwort (Artemisia princeps) leaves, then Artemisia vulgaris is an alternative. Ssukcha tea, with an earthy aroma and unique flavor profile, is not only cherished for its taste but also its health benefits. Rich in antioxidants and anti-inflammatory properties, Ssukcha tea is often consumed to promote overall well-being and boost the immune system. Additionally, it is known for its calming effects, making it a popular choice for relaxation and stress reduction.

Ingredients

2 tablespoons dried Korean mugwort (Artemisia princeps or Artemisia vulgaris) leaves (ssuk)
3 cups water
Honey or sweetener of choice (optional)
Sliced ginger (Zingiber officinale) rhizome
Fresh mint (Mentha x piperita) leaves for garnish (optional)

Directions

  • Prepare the mugwort by rinsing the dried Korean mugwort leaves under cold water to remove any impurities.
  • In a pot, bring 3 cups of water to a boil. Once boiling, add the dried mugwort leaves to the pot.
  • Reduce the heat to low, cover the pot, and let the mugwort leaves simmer for approximately 10-15 minutes. This allows the flavors and beneficial properties of the mugwort to infuse into the water.
  • After simmering, turn off the heat and let the tea cool for a few minutes. Use a fine mesh strainer or cheesecloth to strain the tea, separating the liquid from the mugwort leaves.
  • If desired, add honey or your preferred sweetener to the strained tea. Stir until the sweetener is fully dissolved. Adjust the sweetness according to your taste preferences.
  • For added flavor, consider adding thin slices of fresh ginger during the simmering process. Ginger complements the earthy notes of mugwort and adds a subtle warmth to the tea.
  • Pour the ssukcha tea into cups or mugs. Garnish with fresh mint leaves for a refreshing touch.

Mugwort Bath Ritual

One of my favorite ways to enjoy mugwort is creating a herbal bath ritual. When taking an herbal bath, set a time in the evening when you are alone, light candles around the tub in a dimly lit bathroom, play meditative music, and set an intention for the night.

Ingredients

1 cup fresh mugwort (Artemisia princeps) leaf
2 cups dried mugwort (Artemisia princeps) leaf

Directions

  • Fill a quart-sized canning jar full of fresh and dried mugwort. Using just dried mugwort is fine if fresh mugwort is not available.
  • Add boiling water, cover, and steep the herbs for at least 30 minutes before preparing your bath.
  • Once the mugwort has been steeped, strain the mugwort tea into the bath before getting in. Soak in your own intuition and enjoy the flow of a cleansing bath.

What is Qi?

Qi is an energetic life force that is inside each of us, an ancient healing concept rooted in Taoist philosophy from 400 BCE. Similar to how Americans versus the British pronounce herb (or “erb”) differently, qi (or chi) is pronounced differently in various parts of Asia. There is no precise word for qi in English. It is the foundation of traditional Eastern medicine and predates Newtonian physics. 

In this ancient concept, everything under the sun and moon is composed of qi, and time has a way of showing the alchemy of life through qi. The life of a tree is qi—a cyclical thread connecting all beings that governs all things in heaven and earth. Qi is the common denominator of all things from an acorn seed to the total energy of the universe. The qi of the sun, rain, and soil resonate with the qi of an acorn to bring forth a tree that already contains the germ of the seedling and qualities that the sun, rain, and soil touch. Qi is the fundamental quality of being and becoming.

We cannot and should not try to explain or define qi. We simply experience qi. 

This ancient healing concept leads to methods for bringing the body into balance. Balance stems both from the internal processes of the body and external factors, such as environment and lifestyle habits. In order to avoid imbalance, Eastern medicine focuses on implementing preventative health as the first step of defense. In Korea in particular, stress is taken very seriously. When Koreans feel that their digestion isn’t aligned or that they have slight aches and pains, they implement modalities such as acupuncture and herbs. Efforts are taken to manage emotional and mental stress, as well. 

In order to be mindful of qi, techniques like meditation, breathwork, stretching, exercising, and eating consciously bring balance to the body.

Garlic (Allium sativum) Bulb: Maneul

Korean name: 마늘 [maneul]

Family: Amaryllidaceae

Binomial: Allium sativum

Parts Used: bulb

Taste and Energetics: sour, pungent, hot

Properties: alterative, antimicrobial, cardioprotective, expectorant

Indicated Uses: Garlic is a well-known antiseptic known for its lung-warming properties and ability to promote expectoration, resolve phlegm, energize the stomach, and support the immune system and the cardiovascular system. 

Nutrients: vitamins B and C, manganese, selenium, iron, copper, and potassium (Skenderi, 2004).

Sour and heating, garlic is a universal herb and a nutrient powerhouse used to cleanse the blood, among other things. Garlic, known as “maneul” in Korean, holds a position of great importance in Korean culture, both in the culinary arts and traditional health practices. Garlic stimulates the lymph system. It’s an antimicrobial and expectorant, making it capable of supporting colds and moving mucus out of the body. Stimulating garlic wards off congestion and chills by warming the interior and dispelling cold. Garlic may be small, but its benefits are mighty. 

Garlic’s significant antibacterial properties not only help protect against certain infections, but also promote heart health. By supporting healthy cholesterol and blood pressure levels, this herb provides overall protection for the heart. Research demonstrates that  those who have lower blood pressure are more likely to consume garlic in their diet (Qidwai et al., 2000). 

Garlic is often associated with warding off evil spirits and promoting vitality. The annual “Maneulnoli” festival, dedicated to garlic, highlights its cultural importance, bringing communities together to celebrate the garlic harvest and share in the multifaceted significance of this aromatic bulb. The enduring presence of garlic in Korean culture underscores its indispensable role, not only in the culinary realm but also in the broader cultural and health practices of the Korean people.

Fermented Garlic Cloves Recipe

Koreans use garlic in almost all their cuisine. Below is a delicious and healthy fermented garlic-centric side dish that can be stored in the refrigerator for months. In Korean herbalism, fermented garlic contains twice as many antioxidants as unfermented garlic and is rich in amino acids, the building blocks of protein in the body (Cleveland Clinic, 2021).

Ingredients

5 garlic (Allium sativum) bulbs
2 tablespoons rice vinegar
2 tablespoons soy sauce
1 cup water
2 tablespoons honey
2 tablespoons cooking wine
Pinch of non-iodized salt

Directions

  • Combine all ingredients in a saucepan.
  • Heat the mixture until liquid is boiling and allow to cool.
  • Transfer mixture to a pint-sized canning jar and cover lightly with a lid.
  • Let it sit in a dark pantry or cupboard for at least a week to ferment, then transfer to the refrigerator.
  • Enjoy as a side dish with a bed of rice or wrap it in seaweed paper with rice.
