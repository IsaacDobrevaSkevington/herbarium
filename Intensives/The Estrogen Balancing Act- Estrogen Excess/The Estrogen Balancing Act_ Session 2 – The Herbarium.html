The Estrogen Balancing Act: Session 2

Session 2: Managing High Estrogen Levels

Estrogen excess is becoming increasingly common and many people feel confused, overwhelmed, and bewildered about how to identify it and more importantly, how to start addressing this hormonal imbalance.

In my clinical experience, I’ve observed that estrogen excess can create a rollercoaster of emotional and physical symptoms that frequently feed into each other. Estrogen excess often disrupts metabolic processes promoting harmful biochemical cycles, like insulin resistance and increased aromatization of estrogens in adipose tissue.  The physical and emotional manifestations of estrogen excess can cause weight gain, premenstrual tension, anxiety, and fatigue, which can compound the situation and exacerbate hormonal imbalance.

Estrogen’s Bad Reputation

In normal ratios, estrogen is not a bad hormone. In fact, all endogenous human hormones have a host of benefits. Like most other body components, estrogen behaves differently in homeostatic and non-homeostatic situations. When homeostasis prevails, estrogen protects against all invaders and stressors, maintaining health (Patel et al., 2018). When these complex ratios between hormones fall out of balance and become disrupted, symptoms will arise from the dominant or excessive hormone. This will initiate a series of effects on hormonal pathways including a rise in potentially harmful metabolites that the body is unable to detoxify efficiently. As documented in Session 1, estrogen excess can have consequential, serious health implications, and chronic estrogen excess has been linked to various serious pathologies such as reproductive and breast cancers (Dossus et al., 2010; Travis & Key, 2003).

Natural Approaches to Managing Estrogen Levels

The positive news is that estrogen and overall hormonal balance can be very responsive to protocols incorporating natural approaches.  However, taking an integrative approach is vital-—there is no silver bullet. Consistent use of nutritional and lifestyle changes alongside targeted herbal support not only reduces excess estrogen levels but also substantially supports future health and wellbeing. 

This aspect is fundamental. Lifestyle and nutritional changes can make significant differences in hormonal health, experience of perimenopause and menopause (and, indeed, andropause) and ultimately substantially lower the risk of certain serious diseases. Another benefit of this integrated approach is the possible avoidance of using artificial hormones of all varieties, which all come with potential side effects.

Six Main Therapeutic Targets

When addressing estrogen excess clinically, I believe there are six main areas to target in order to maintain hormone balance, lower elevated estrogen and estrogen metabolites, and improve progesterone levels.

1) Support liver health 

As one of the main organs of estrogen detoxification, it’s important that the liver is working efficiently and is not overloaded. Thinking of the liver as a filter for potentially harmful metabolites from excess estrogen can be a useful way to envision this. If the filter is overloaded or busy processing a host of other toxins, it will be unable to deal with the estrogen metabolites as quickly and effectively. 

Dietary support:

  • Reduce alcohol intake: try to moderate drinking to a maximum of twice weekly and limit intake to a maximum of 2 standard drinks to reduce the impact on phase II liver detoxification. Drinking water between alcoholic drinks will also help the detoxification process. Moderating alcohol will also help maintain healthy blood sugar balance and therefore help to regulate insulin levels and reduce aromatization.
  • Reduce excessive coffee intake: moderating to 1 cup daily can help in situations of estrogen excess. I believe that this both reduces the demand on the detoxification pathways of the liver and also reduces cortisol spikes which can affect hormone production and overall hormone balance. However, if dealing with an extremely estrogen-dominant situation, I would suggest avoiding both coffee and alcohol until estrogen levels stabilize. Green tea has a host of antioxidant benefits and may be a good alternative to coffee. Research on Japanese women showed that one cup of green tea per day reduced estradiol and estrone from the 16-hydroxylated pathway (connected with breast cancer) by 40% in post-menopausal women (Patel et al., 2018). Mushroom coffees are also a good alternative; in particular, lion’s mane (Hericium erinaceus) can bring a similar mental clarity effect as caffeine without the detrimental effects on cortisol levels. 
  • Eat lots of cruciferous (Brassica family) vegetables: Kale, broccoli, cauliflower, cabbage, and Brussels sprouts all contain anti-carcinogenic compounds called indole-3-carbinol (Chamovitz et al., 2018).  Dietary consumption of broccoli actively promotes the breakdown of estrogen via the beneficial 2-OH pathway (Patel et al., 2018). A serving or two a day (½ – 1 cup) is recommended.
  • Improve liver methylation support: this can improve estrogen elimination through catechol-O-methyltransferase (COMT), the more protective estrogen pathway. Ellagic acid, a compound derived from blackberries, blueberries, and raspberries, has been shown to stimulate CYP1A1 (protective enzymes that assist in estrogen elimination) (Patel et al., 2018). Sulfur-rich foods, such as eggs, brazil nuts, onions, leeks, garlic, and cabbage also support methylation (Neil, 2010).

Herbal support: 

  • Milk thistle (Silybum marianum) seed improves liver detoxification and protects liver cells from damage. 
  • Schisandra (Schisandra chinensis) fruit increases phase I and II detoxification pathways and acts as a supportive adaptogen. 
  • Herbal bitters have a stimulating effect on the digestive system, increasing bile flow and consequently liver detoxification of estrogen metabolites. Examples of bitter herbs include barberry (Berberis vulgaris) root bark, gentian (Gentiana lutea) root, chamomile (Matricaria chamomilla) flower, and dandelion (Taraxacum officinale) root. Small doses of 5 to 10 drops of a 1:3 tincture are enough and should be taken before meals. (Bone & Mills, 2013). However, these are contraindicated in duodenal ulcers. Bitter foods such as arugula and endive have a similar but less potent effect.

2) Support gut health 

The gut is a key site of estrogen detoxification. Good gut health is fundamental to a huge range of bodily processes and overall health and wellbeing. Unfortunately, the rise of the Standard American Diet (SAD) has had a huge impact on gut health. I’ve found that as much as 90% of my clients struggling with estrogen excess had signs of a gut imbalance such as bloating and constipation, even though the majority presented with hormonal complaints.

Dietary suggestions:

  • Eat fermented foods: The volume of research regarding the estrobolome and gut health in relation to hormone balance is rapidly gaining significant clinical importance. I believe the exclusion of fermented food in the SAD has been a detrimental factor in gut health. Fermentation, a tradition used in many cultures as a way of storing and preserving food products, has been lost with the rise of freezers, processing, chemical preservatives, and plastics. Fermented food such as sauerkraut, kimchi, full-fat organic yogurt (without added sugar), kombucha, and kefir all provide a wealth of lactic acid bacteria (LAB). During fermentation, these bacteria synthesize vitamins and minerals, produce biologically active peptides with enzymes such as proteinase and peptidase, and remove some non-nutrients (Patel et al., 2018).

The Herbal Academy has an excellent short course on the Craft of Herbal Fermentation for those wishing to make their own herbal ferments.

In my experience, once balanced gut health has been achieved with a course of probiotics, a tablespoon of fermented food can have an equivalent effect as taking a daily probiotic supplement. I would advise anyone with any kind of hormonal imbalance to address gut health and regulate dysbiosis. If making these ferments at home is not possible, most health food shops now offer live-fermented food such as sauerkraut and kimchi.

    • Ensure regular consumption of prebiotics: These are nondigestible carbohydrates that feed the good bacteria in the gut, including oats, whole soybeans, onions, garlic, steamed asparagus, bananas (just ripe), and Jerusalem artichokes.
    • Lots of fiber: Dietary fiber, especially lignin, binds unconjugated estrogens in the digestive tract, which are then excreted in the feces. It can also beneficially affect the composition of intestinal bacteria and reduce intestinal β-glucuronidase activity, resulting in a lowered deconjugation of estrogen and reduced reabsorption. Dietary fiber intake also increases serum concentrations of sex hormona binding globulin (SHBG), thus reducing levels of free estradiol (Hall, 2001).
  • Support healthy bowel transit and prevent constipation: Drink adequate water, ensure plenty of daily movement and activity, avoid processed food and “white foods” such as white bread, muffins, pasta, wheat crackers, and white rice.
  • Avoid unnecessary antibiotic use: Antibiotics should only be used when absolutely necessary and when discussed with a health provider. Antibiotics destroy endogenous gut bacteria, so if taking antibiotics, ensure beneficial bacteria are replaced by supplementing with probiotics during and after use.
  • B-vitamin complex and magnesium may be indicated: To support nervous system function and healthy bowel transit.

Herbal support: 

  • Berberine, a constituent in barberry and a number of other herbs, is a very effective antimicrobial. I have also found it effective in addressing small intestine bacterial overgrowth (SIBO) and hormone imbalance due to gut dysbiosis.  It is very bitter, so is best taken as a tincture or in capsule form.

3) Reduce stress

Stress is a major cause of hormone imbalance. Increased cortisol production depletes progesterone levels, initiating imbalanced hormone ratios and estrogen excess. Self-care must be a prioritized routine rather than something that slips when we get busy. This is especially important as we get older and our body really needs the extra exercise, sleep, good nutrition, relaxation, and time to laugh in order to perform well and feel good. The topic of stress management is covered in much more depth in the Herbal Self-Care for Stress Management short course by the Herbal Academy.

It is also addressed in the Supporting Clients with Stress intensive here in The Herbarium. 

Two clinical observations: Stress is not just environmental!

I encourage many of my clients to stop people-pleasing and putting others’ wants and expectations before their own needs. Saying no respectfully is an important life skill that is often hard to develop.  Additionally, stress and pressure may be created internally by our own expectations of ourselves and by setting ambitious (and often unrealistic) goals to achieve everything simultaneously. Teach clients to be mindful of this and prioritize their most valuable activities and time. 

Herbal support: 

  • Adaptogens like ashwagandha (Withania somnifera) root, verbena (Verbena officinalis) aerial parts, and nutritive nervines like oat (Avena sativa) milky seed, linden (Tilia spp.) flower, and chamomile (Matricaria chamomilla) flower may also be supportive here.

4) Maintain a healthy weight and decrease adipose tissue

Adipose tissue has more estrogen receptors and can produce more estrogen through the aromatase pathway. If overweight, reducing adipose tissue can significantly reduce the amount of estrogens produced. This will not only reduce estrogen excess but is a major step towards the prevention of estrogen-driven pathologies. Abdominal weight gain has been recognized as a factor in estrogen-driven cancers (Keum et al., 2015). 

Dietary and lifestyle suggestions: 

  • Low to moderate carbohydrate intake if there is a history of PCOS or blood sugar regulation issues: Excess consumption of simple carbohydrates raises blood glucose and insulin levels, resulting in adverse influences on sex hormone balance (Hall, 2001).
  • Incorporate both regular aerobic and weight-bearing exercise: Regular exercise will help with building muscle and losing fat.
  • Increase overall physical activity: This will reduce the risk of reproductive tract and estrogen-driven cancers (Gonçalves et al. 2014; Schmid et al., 2015). Research has indicated that physically active women have about a 20 to 30 percent reduction in risk of having breast cancer compared with inactive women (Gonçalves et al., 2014).
  •  Be mindful of how much time is spent sitting in front of a computer or at a desk. Get moving! I recommend a target of at least 10,000-15,000 steps daily.

5) Minimize exposure to endocrine disruptors

With increasing evidence pointing towards the huge impact of endocrine-disrupting chemicals (EDCs) on hormone balance, it is crucial to minimize their effect by reducing EDCs in our personal environments, homes, and diets. 

Dietary and lifestyle suggestions:

  • Eat organic foods, grass-fed meat, and small fish: Avoid high consumption of large fish such as tuna and swordfish, which have higher levels of mercury. Smaller fish, such as sardines, herring, and mackerel, contain less mercury. Organic produce is produced without contamination from endocrine-disrupting chemicals in  fertilizers and pesticides. If consuming meat and dairy, choose pastured organic meat and dairy, which is grazed in a natural outdoor environment and therefore contains more natural nutrients and is free of genetically modified organisms (GMO) from GMO feed grain.
  • Swap plastic bottles and food containers for glass: This includes eliminating bottled water. Use a carbon filter as a cheap and effective way to filter drinking water. 
  • Switch household detergents containing parabens and other harmful chemicals to more natural alternatives: For example, white wine vinegar and essential oils make an excellent fabric softener. Avoid unnecessary chemical fragrance products, which can be replaced with essential oils. A simple spray with a small amount of alcohol, distilled water, and essential oils can make a great air freshener.
  • Use natural skin care products, preferably stored in a glass container: The skin is our largest organ, so anything we put on it will be absorbed and can end up in our bodies or become stored in fat. Many homemade skin care products are simple and gratifying to make. For a thorough exploration into skin health and many inspiring skin care recipes, see the excellent Botanical Skin Care short course from Herbal Academy on natural skin care.

6) Adopt an estrogen-lowering nutritional protocol

An anti-inflammatory diet involves consuming low sugar, high fiber, balanced healthy fats, and other nutrient-dense, whole foods that are rich in antioxidants. A well-researched example is the Mediterranean diet. However, there are a few specifics to consider in terms of reducing estrogen excess.

Dietary suggestions:

  • Reduce or exclude dairy: Clinically, I have observed significant reduction of estrogen excess symptoms when cow’s milk is replaced with plant milk alternatives. Cow milk consumption may increase urinary excretion of estrone (E1) in humans. Ingestion of large amounts (1 liter daily)  of semi-skimmed milk appears to raise estradiol (E2) and E3 (estriol) (Michels et al., 2019). It has been suggested that this is due to industrialization of farming and that now cows are milked while pregnant. This has led to higher levels of estrogens in milk now than in the past. 
  • Consume adequate protein: This is essential for the synthesis of CYP detoxification enzymes (Neil, 2010). The DRI (Dietary reference Intake) is 0.36 grams of protein per pound (or 0.8 grams per kilogram) of body weight—so someone who weighs 150 pounds should consume about 54 grams of protein daily. To put this into context, an average egg has 6 g protein, chickpeas have 19 g protein in 100 grams, and spirulina has 4 g protein in 1 teaspoon. 
  • Consume high amounts of dietary fiber: Fiber helps promote regular bowel movements and eliminate estrogen, preventing reabsorption and recirculation.
  • Consume dietary phytoestrogens: These include isoflavones from soy, beans, peas, clover, alfalfa, and kudzu, and lignans, especially from flaxseed, rye, wheat, and sea vegetables. Certain flavonoids (e.g., rutin, resveratrol, quercetin), especially from citrus fruits and grapes, also act as phytoestrogens. Phytoestrogens like lignan, isoflavones, and coumestan act as estrogen agonists, and on ligation with the estrogen receptors, initiate estrogen signaling. It is debated whether the phytoestrogens might be acting as antagonists, blocking estrogen from binding to the receptors, thus might hold potential as selective estrogen receptor modulators (SERMs) (Patel et al., 2018). In terms of evidence, it remains widely debated yet inconclusive. In my opinion, phytoestrogens seem to confer protective benefits in nutritional amounts and have been consumed for thousands of years. However, I would not recommend them in high isolated extract strengths (such as genistein or soy extracts) unless under the guidance of a health professional until further evidence becomes conclusive. Long-term studies are necessary to ascertain whether phytoestrogens can be recommended for people with estrogen-responsive cancers.
  • Consume soy: Soy has been shown to reduce 17β-estradiol in women (Lu et al.,  1996). There is considerable evidence that soy intake encourages estrogen detoxification (Xu et al., 2000), however, there is no definite conclusion on its overall benefit yet. If using soy, non-GMO, organic, and fermented sources are advised. Fermented sources of soy include miso, tempeh, soy sauce, and natto. The process of fermentation destroys lectins and phytic acid making soy easier to digest.Tofu and organic edamame beans are also good sources of whole bean soy with good levels of  protein, calcium, and beneficial phytoestrogens.
  • Add flax seed to diet daily: Research showed that women consuming 10 g (1 teaspoon) of flaxseed per day experienced longer luteal phase during the menstrual cycle, increased progesterone-to-estrogen ratios, and fewer anovulatory cycles, all of which were considered to reflect improved ovarian function (Phipps et al., 1993). Flaxseed also contains up to 40% oil rich in α-linolenic acid (i.e., n-3 polyunsaturated fatty acid) (Chen et al., 2006). Flaxseed also inhibits the aromatase enzyme (Bone & Mills, 2013). I suggest adding a teaspoon a day of fresh-milled (to release oils fresh and avoid rancidity) flaxseed added to foods.
  • Include white button mushrooms: White mushrooms have an anti-aromatase effect that reduces conversion of androgens to estrogens and is protective against breast cancer (Patel et al., 2018)
  • Include citrus: D-limonene, a naturally occurring monoterpene found in the oils of citrus fruits, promotes the detoxification of estrogen by inducing phase I and phase II enzymes in the liver (Patel et al., 2018). A simple and tasty way to do this is have a slice of organic, unwaxed lemon in hot water or flavor cool water with lemon or orange slices.

Always Support the Individual

When considering an approach for a particular individual, it is important to take into account the individual’s genetic predisposition, current symptoms, and blood and hormone test results. After taking a full case history, some very targeted questions in relation to estrogen excess may be useful to try to determine the main underlying cause and then target this with the protocol.

Some specific questions I like to ask clients in relation to identifying estrogen excess are:

Question Remark
Have you always had heavy periods and/or problem periods, or did this develop later in life?

If client has always had heavy periods, this indicates that the client has had a higher menstruating lifetime exposure to endogenous estrogens. Could suggest genetic high estrogens or  detoxification issues from puberty, rather than something that develops later due to increased aromatisation with PCOS or exposure to endocrine disruptors.

  • Primarily address high endogenous estrogens and progesterone balance.
What age was your menarche? Have you reached menopause? (If not, ask mother’s age at menopause as there is a strong maternal genetic link.) Duration of menstruating years can be indicative of higher lifetime estrogen levels and therefore increased risk of estrogen detoxification issues or high endogenous estrogens. 

  • Focus on estrogen clearance and effective detoxification.
Did you take the oral contraceptive pill (OCP) or other artificial hormones for over 5 years? If so, how did you react to taking them, and to discontinuing them?  

There is a potential for reduced progesterone production from long-term use. May be a higher risk for gut dysbiosis.

  • Ensure good gut health and increase endogenous progesterone production.
Do you have a history of PCOS? Is maintaining a healthy weight or waist to hip ratio a struggle?

This is indicative of increased aromatization of testosterone to estrogen, especially in the late thirties onwards. 

  • Focus on decreasing aromatization and reducing blood sugar levels.
Have you been through recent stressful or challenging events? Can you give me an idea of your current stress levels? (Using a 1-10 scale can be useful and allows changes to be monitored over time).

Chronic stress can be implicated in hormone imbalance, contributing to low progesterone levels relative to estrogen.

  • Consider adaptogen support and nervines to reduce cortisol and support progesterone production.
Have you been in contact with high levels of endocrine-disrupting chemicals (EDCs)? Occupations like hairdressing, dry cleaning, and custodial work with high exposure to cleaning products may increase risk.

If there are issues with detoxification of estrogen metabolites, adipose tissues may  store EDCs.

  •  May require additional metabolism and detoxification support.
  • Adipose tissue loss if indicated.
Is there a family history of estrogen-driven cancer or estrogen excess conditions like fibroids or endometriosis?

This may indicate epigenetic links due to methylation or detoxification issues.

  • Support effective detoxification, reduce aromatization, and increase phytoestrogens.
Is there a history of thyroid issues?

Excess estrogen can affect the thyroid’s ability to produce hormones, while progesterone stimulates thyroid hormone production. Could indicate low relative progesterone.

  • Support progesterone production and increase phytoestrogens to modulate the effect of endogenous estrogens on thyroid.

Of course, it could be a combination of all of these factors, but I find trying to investigate a bit deeper and find underlying causes can help formulate an intial targeted and directional approach. This can be very useful to enhance the effectiveness of the protocol and produce a quicker response. The client’s response can be monitored and the protocol adapted as practitioner support continues.

Top Five Herbs to Consider for Estrogen Excess

There are a wide variety of herbs that may be used to assist with restoring healthy estrogen balance. These herbs have arange of useful actions such as increasing estrogen metabolism, enhancing progesterone production, reducing cortisol and the stress response, decreasing excessive aromatisation, and supporting optimum liver function. Below are the herbs that I use most often, and find most effective in clinical practice.

Rosemary (Rosmarinus officinalis) aerial parts

Rosemary contains antioxidants to support liver function (Ho, 1994). It has traditionally been used to regulate periods, improve memory, and support circulation. I use it as a specific for those with fibroids, PMS, and cyclical breast tenderness, all caused by high estrogen ratios and decreased estrogen metabolism. I find rosemary very effective when combined with vitex (Vitex agnus-castus) berry in a tincture formula for estrogen excess.

Ways to take it: Tincture, tea, vinegar, or glycerite

Safety: Use caution during pregnancy, breastfeeding, or in those with high blood pressure. Use under professional supervision when taking medications and with digestive health conditions.

Chaste tree (Vitex agnus castus) berry 

Vitex can help modulate hormone balance, balancing progesterone and estrogen ratios. Vitex contains diterpenes that have dopamine-modulating qualities which inhibit prolactin. In turn, prolactin inhibits corpus luteum development, thereby reducing progesterone in the luteal phase. Consequently, by inhibiting prolactin, chaste tree may increase progesterone (Bone & Mills, 2013). The flavonoid apigenin, also found in vitex, has been observed to have phytoestrogenic qualities and binds with the β-estrogen receptor (Shoba et al., 1998) . Potentially, this could in effect work by blocking some of the stronger action of endogenous estrogens. 

I use vitex mainly in a tincture formulation with other herbs. However, it can also be taken as a capsule. Vitex may take 2-3 cycles to influence hormone balance and may not work for all those with estrogen excess, so it is important to monitor response and symptoms closely. Vitex is a complex herb, and dosage can vary so it is preferable to use it under professional supervision.

Ways to take it: Tincture, capsules

Safety: Use cautiously in young women whose cycle is still settling and thus is easily disrupted. Do not take at same time as hormonal medications or the oral contraceptive pill. 

Turmeric (Curcuma longa) rhizome 

A well-researched anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, blood sugar-balancing, and hepatic herb that is very useful in situations of estrogen excess. Research has shown curcumin in turmeric  can help prevent estrogen metabolites from turning into toxic quinones (Liska & Leupp, 2002). Turmeric also acts as a weak phytoestrogen, blocking the action of some of the stronger endogenous estrogens (Bachmeier et al., 2010). 

Ways to take it: Capsules, in foods; suggested dose 2 g daily. (A tasty way to enjoy is daily turmeric lattes with plant-based milk, coconut oil, and cinnamon.) Always combine with black pepper, as piperine dramatically improves bioavailability (Shoba et al., 1998). Turmeric is not extracted well in tinctures.

Safety: High doses should not be used long term with concurrent antiplatelet or anticoagulant medication. Contraindicated in biliary tract obstruction.

Milk thistle (Silybum marianum) seed 

A hepatoprotective and antioxidant, milk thistle also has insulin-stabilizing properties. Its role in reducing estrogen excess is through supporting the effective functioning of hepatocytes. Milk thistle appears to reduce the activity of the CYP enzymes in vitro and its role seems to be in protecting the liver cells and stimulating new growth of liver cells rather than enhancing xenobiotic clearance (Bone & Mills, 2013). 

Ways to take it: Tincture, capsules

Safety: Consult with a health care provider before taking milk thistle with prescribed medication as it may alter the metabolism of drugs.

Berberine-containing herbs

The most utilized berberine-containing herb in my dispensary is barberry (Berberis vulgaris) bark, but berberine is also found in Oregon grape (Berberis aquifolium) root, coptis (Coptis chinensis) root, and several other herbs. Berberine is antimicrobial, antitumor, anti-inflammatory and has blood glucose-lowering effects. I use it as a specific for gut dysbiosis linked to hormone imbalance. 

Ways to take it: Tincture, capsules

Safety: Consult with a health care provider if taking any medications. Berberine may interact with antibiotics so stop use while taking them.

Top Five Supplements to Consider for Estrogen Excess

Selected supplemental nutrients may be indicated alongside the herbs and nutritional changes discussed above. It is important to highlight that all of these may not be necessary; look at the individual’s signs and symptoms and, if relevant, blood work, and choose the supplements that are most appropriate. I am not a big fan of giving a lot of supplements simultaneously to clients, however, in certain situations, targeted supplementation can be very useful. Below are the supplements that I find most clinically relevant in situations of estrogen excess.

DIM with calcium glucarate: Diindolylmethane (DIM), a compound derived from cruciferous vegetables, has been found to stimulate cytochrome activity CYP1A1 and is also a mild aromatase inhibitor. It is often combined with calcium glucarate. The active component, glucarate (or glucaric acid), binds and deactivates estrogen in the liver and inhibits beta-glucuronidase, an enzyme made in gut bacteria that causes estrogen to be reabsorbed (Briden, 2017). 

B complex: Folate, B6, and B12 function as important cofactors for enzymes involved in estrogen detoxification; thus, decreased levels of B vitamins can lead to increased levels of circulating estrogens (Hall, 2001). A good quality B complex is a useful way to take these vitamins.

Zinc: Zinc helps increase progesterone levels and lower estrogen levels. About 10 percent of the United States population is deficient in zinc (Wuehler et al., 2005) However, this number may be much higher in vegetarians and vegans because meat is high in bioavailable zinc and may enhance zinc absorption. In addition, vegetarians typically eat high levels of legumes and whole grains, which contain phytates that bind zinc and inhibit its absorption. Vegetarians sometimes require as much as 50% more of the RDA for zinc than non-vegetarians (National Institute of Health, 2019). Therefore, supplementation may be indicated. 

Probiotics: Good gut health is vital to the healthy detoxification of estrogens. An initial course of probiotics may be indicated alongside dietary changes to restore gut health. Symprove is a liquid probiotic from barley that I use most often in my practice; it seems to be most effective as more of the viable bacteria may be able to reach the gut. The bacterial strains it contains include Lactobacillus rhamnosus, Enterococcus faecium, L. acidophilus, and L. plantarum.

Magnesium: Magnesium is an essential cofactor for the COMT enzyme, and therefore optimizes the methylation and excretion of catechol estrogens (Bolton, 1998). Magnesium chelate or magnesium glycinate are highly absorbable forms with low laxative side effects at 300-400 mg per day. For those with constipation, magnesium citrate is more effective at 200-400 mg per day (depending on bowel tolerance).

Considering Pharmaceuticals?

There are a range of options available when addressing estrogen excess and hormonal imbalance and below is a review of pharmaceutical options available. As a supporter of functional medicine, I believe that there may be a place for both pharmaceuticals and natural approaches, in certain cases, within an integrated health system. However, it is important to discuss both the benefit and potential side effects of these approaches with a health care provider.

Hormonal medications and bioidentical options: For those who choose to use pharmaceutical treatments under the guidance of a medical provider, there are some things to keep in mind.  A skilled practitioner will closely monitor hormone, thyroid function, and insulin levels throughout treatment, and should provide you with information about potential side effects or health implications of artificial hormones.

 Hormone replacement therapy (HRT): Giving HRT for symptoms of estrogen excess does not address the root cause of the issue, as estrogen excess as is not caused by low estrogen levels.  Estrogen-dependent conditions such as fibroids and endometriosis can be aggravated by HRT and may require cessation of treatment (Trickey, 1998). Additionally, HRT carries a higher risk of blood clotting and certain cancers.

Prescribed bioidentical hormonal treatment: The use of bioidentical hormones can potentially displace cortisol from cortisol binding globulin (CBG), which can then lead to an excess of free-circulating cortisol, which perpetuates a stress response cascade (Neil, 2010). In effect, this implies that the bioidentical progesterone may cause more stress responses and consequently reduce endogenous progesterone production. 

Oral Contraceptive Pill (OCP): OCP depletes many nutrients and disrupts gut flora. It carries additional cardiovascular risks and potential psychological symptoms for some people (Briden, 2017).  In my experience, it is not advisable for those who intend to conceive in the near future as it can be challenging to try and restore normal ovulatory cycles after taking for any length of time. Taking the OCP does not address the root cause of the hormonal imbalance. 

Progesterone cream synthesized from wild yam: This is widely available over the counter, and is often marketed as a natural product. In fact, a naturally occurring constituent in wild yam (Dioscorea villosa) is actually synthesized to produce pharmaceutical-grade micronized progesterone, and many so-called “natural wild yam” creams actually include synthetic, micronized progesterone. This is a synthesized progesterone cream.

Synthesized Progesterone Creams- a few cautions

Dr. Jolene Brighten warns that it is very hard to get dosage right with over-the-counter progesterone creams, as they vary so much in concentration and absorption, and using them may even mask symptoms of serious endocrine pathologies, even cancer (Brighten, n.d.).

I have had many women present with increased symptoms of headaches, anxiety, premenstrual tension, and insomnia after using progesterone cream for more than a few months. Again, progesterone cream does not address the root cause and may further disrupt delicate hormonal balance.

Pure natural wild yam cream: This does not contain any natural progesterone that can be utilized by the body. There is much confusion and dubious marketing around this. The steroidal saponin found in wild yam, dioscin, is marketed as being converted to progesterone in the body. However, as Ruth Trickey (1998) highlights:

“Chemically this assumption is quite illogical, especially considering the starting compound for progesterone in the body is cholesterol. In fact, if transdermal applications of a starting compound could be reasonably expected to produce progesterone, rubbing the body with butter should have a greater (and far cheaper) effect on progesterone levels” ( p.315).

To reiterate, wild yam cream contains no biologically active progesterone or progesterone precursors that can be utilized by the human body.

It is always important to take each case in perspective. For those who have not had success with natural approaches, there may be a case for considering progesterone creams or bioidentical hormone therapy along with appropriate nutritional, dietary, and other changes. In my clinical opinion and experience there is no method to resolve estrogen excess without primarily addressing dietary and lifestyle changes. 

Conclusion

The symptoms of estrogen excess are an alarm bell signaling that the body is out of balance and there are fundamental health issues to address. In this way, I feel it can be a very useful sign, and when addressed early on, there are numerous impactful changes and proactive preventative steps that can be taken to improve hormonal balance and overall wellbeing in the long term. It is my opinion that artificial hormones and medication should only be utilized once fundamental lifestyle and nutritional changes have been tried without improvement. Artificial hormones are not a panacea, they are powerful drugs. Of course, sometimes these pharmacological approaches are indicated in certain cases and may need to be undertaken with professional supervision, but the integrated and functional approach should be the first approach.

I’ll illustrate how individualized protocols can be tailored for the client and examine in detail the herbs utilized for each person. I’ll demonstrate how stress, family history, poor diet, and lifestyle choices can all be causes of estrogen excess, and then I’ll present the effective protocols and herbs that I used to help these four clients regain hormonal balance and, ultimately, good health.
