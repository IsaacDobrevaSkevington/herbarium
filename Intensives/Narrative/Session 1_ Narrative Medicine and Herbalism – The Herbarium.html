Session 1: Narrative Medicine and Herbalism

10 minutes · Easy

What Is Narrative Medicine?

“What kind of medicine?” is the typical first response I receive when telling people at herbal cocktail parties, my embittered elderberry martini in hand, that I studied narrative medicine. “What’s that?” they ask again, leaning into the conversation to ensure that they heard me correctly. 

I enunciate slowly the second time around. “Narr-uh-tive…medicine,” I drawl, stretching the syllables, sure to emphasize the first word. 

“Ahh,” they echo back to me, nodding as if suddenly enlightened. “Tell me more about that.”

When defining narrative medicine, I often begin with a memorable catchphrase, as in, “It’s the de-clinicalization and re-humanizing of care,” or, “It’s humanity in medicine,” or, “It’s acknowledging the power of the stories we are told and tell ourselves about our bodies.” These are simple ways to get one’s foot in the door of understanding the complex labyrinth of narrative medicine, a term used interchangeably with narrative healthcare. Dr. Rita Charon (2007), maven of the narrative health program at Columbia University, translates the term to mean clinical practice fortified by the knowledge of what to do with story.

Hippocrates first used the term “patient-centered medicine” which succinctly encapsulates the concept of viewing individuals as holographic life stories rather than walking pathologies reduced to a set of symptoms on a checklist (Srinivasan, 2012). Stories in the caregiver-client interaction have a sensitizing effect on both parties in the face of a desensitized landscape of care.

Goals and Benefits

The overarching goals of narrative medicine are to attend to, interpret, be moved by, and metabolize stories as they pertain to health. Dr. Rita Charon (2008), godmother of narrative medicine, labels affiliative care—in which provider, patient, and story are all inextricably intertwined—as the number one achievement of the narrative model. She describes affiliation as connecting seemingly disconnected realities—between someone’s autoimmune flare, for example, and their job loss, and recognizing the unrecognized as a way of increasing empathy, because empathy heals (Charon, 2008).

Personal narratives in diagnostics and therapeutics are intrinsically beneficial as a form within which patients experience illness, foster empathy and understanding, construct holistic meaning, and contextualize and shed light on aspects of health (Dwyer, 2017). Simply put, the stories we tell ourselves about ourselves greatly impact our health status. 

Client narratives may frame health agendas and ground medical data in their context of life experience. For the care provider, listening to these stories may lead to new and helpful hypotheses and offer reflective potential which leads to optimized health outcomes. 

Just as disease never occurs in a vacuum, neither does healing. Contextualizing illness within the chapters of someone’s life allows dots to connect, diagnoses to be malleable, and health to be an open-ended process rather than an end goal.

The adage “you are your own healer” is a common mantra in alternative health. But an additional layer of empirically proven empowerment is that care fueled by affiliation is more effective. The intersection of active listening and the allowance of story as an intentional way of co-creating health, in practice:

  • reduces provider burnout and malpractice and increases workplace resilience,
  • increases diagnostic accuracy,
  • optimizes patient health outcomes and improves efficacy of care, and
  • increases satisfaction of all parties involved in the care interaction (Charon, 2008).

Narrative Care in Practice

What do we really mean by narrative medicine in practice? On a day-to-day basis, facilitating this type of empathy in the healthcare interaction can include exercises like these:

  • incorporation of plenty of blank space into the intake form;
  • time and attentiveness to possibility, nuance, and multiple subjective truths within the intake;
  • diagnosis and treatment protocols based on unique individuals (story-inclusive);
  • poetic medicine, “prescriptive” literature, writing to heal, and other meaning-making expressions of medical humanities as part of a treatment plan;
  • use of pathography (a story of illness) by client or provider as a way of cultivating empathy;
  • narrative medicine practitioner-facilitated groups involving a close reading of a text followed by a reflective writing exercise (for client or provider);
  • power and agency-sharing within the care provider/client relationship (client as hero, care provider as human); and
  • a mediated patient-provider relationship with a “medical humanist” or narrative medicine facilitator bridging the communication gap.

Narrative medicine can be as simple as the seasoned nurse assuring the nervous patient, “I hear you,” or the verbal invitation of the inquisitive intern, “What was that like for you?” or the oncologist offering a space of silence after the moment of diagnosis. One tagline within the field is that inquiry creates intimacy. Asking questions about what a particular symptom or sensation, diagnosis or detail, might mean to a patient and teasing story out of them is both art and science. 

Narrative Medicine on the Rise

Issues in communication—as opposed to an actual concern about a provider’s level of measurable proficiency—are responsible for more than 70% of major negative healthcare outcomes in hospitals (Joshi, 2015). Holistic communication skills and an emphasis on story as medicine go beyond “soft science,” placebo, or “woo-woo” into the realm of life and death. 

Medical school students experience a documented steep decline in empathy during their third year of schooling, a phenomenon known as “the devil is in the third year” (Hojat et al., 2009, p. 1182). Accordingly, 6-week medical humanities courses are now being required within the core syllabi of terminal degrees for many third-year medical school students. Narrative medicine as a paradigm and practice is experiencing a massive spike in popularity in the face of a sterile, sometimes problematic system of managed care. Many clinics and hospitals are now hiring staff “medical humanists,” “patient-provider liaisons,” and other narrative medicine practitioners to infuse deep listening and empathetic skills into patient-centric care. 

As marginalization of voices and health disparities grow, the importance of story as normalizer and equalizer becomes increasingly evident. In the last two decades, more than 300 pathographical books—or written stories of illness—have come out in support of the power of words to navigate health challenges (Aronson, 2000).

Some Examples of Story-Informed Traditions in Herbalism

There are several distinguishing factors that make herbalism one of the oldest health traditions, including ease of access, cost effectiveness, and general efficacy with a lack of side effects when used properly. Herbalism has been practiced since prehistory, and until recently, was a form of primary healthcare globally. 

Herbalism encompasses holism by acting as a closed-loop cycle involving the whole planet. By harvesting and cultivating herbs from the earth and using these in and on our bodies and sharing them with our human and animal families, we facilitate a relationship of reciprocity and respect with all botanical and animal life, which in turn improves our human lives. 

The tools in the herbalist’s toolbox are endless, and may include diet and nutrition, exercise, therapeutic touch, lifestyle shifts, use of stones and gems, nature-based therapy, meditation, yoga, various forms of mental health counseling, and narrative medicine. Appreciation for story is tightly wound into many traditions of herbalism, two specifically that we will explore here. 

Narrative in the Wise Woman Tradition

The wise woman tradition (which includes those assigned male at birth as well as those assigned female at birth) believes that medicine is everywhere and in everything, and that defining it as one thing is impossible. One primary value of this belief system is the perception of health journeys as wholistic, nourishing, building, accepting, and empathizing rather than based on fear, deprivation, and heroic cleansing. 

The wise woman tradition stands on flexibly integrating multiple wellness modalities and upholding the trio of science, intuition, and tradition as equally important standards. It’s built on the foundation of the self as responsible, accountable, informed, and self-healing. This heritage acknowledges that much of healing and maintaining health is silent and invisible. 

In the wise woman tradition, there are seven medicines, or main areas of focus which may transform one’s health; one of these is story medicine (Weed, 2020). Disease and illness are seen as gateways of metamorphosis and symptoms are framed as gifts. This movement delineates diagnosis as the finding of story and healing as the telling of story (Weed, 2020). 

Stories Within Southern Appalachian Folk Medicine

Southern folk medicine also boasts a long history of incorporating different forms of story into the wellness journey, some of which are touched on below. I was trained in southern Appalachian folk medicine by Phyllis Light (personal communication, March 2015), a fifth-generation practitioner of this tradition of the mountains of the southeastern United States, a lineage informed by engaging in bioregional relationships with local plants. Southern folk medicine evolved into a system of identifying and countering physical ailments of all kinds without restriction to where someone originated from or lived. 

The energetics of Southern folk medicine are divided into the four elements: earth, water, air, and fire, which translate into constitutions. The stories that we are told and tell ourselves about our health or illness go through the filters of our constitution as we process them. 

In this tradition, the lines between the metaphorical and the literal, the physical and the metaphysical, are blurred. Someone with a fiery constitution might have an overabundance of digestive fire that speeds nutrient assimilation and leaves them unable to properly absorb nutrition. An airy individual out of balance might find themselves unable to concentrate because they have a lack of grounded mental clarity and are in need of earth energy to anchor them firmly into focus.

The cornerstones of Southern folk medicine (many of which it shares with other natural health ideologies) include health as a continually evolving process, the body as a self-healing instrument given the right tools, the self as a collaboration between mind, body, and spirit, and symptoms as the body’s brilliant messengers which should not be suppressed (P.D. Light, personal communication, March 2015). 

Disease is multifaceted and may occur as a result of combining energetically unclean forces, pathological viruses and bacteria, spiritual transgressions, magic, and negative energy, or it can be brought onto oneself through perceived wrongdoing. Trauma, emotional distress, and stress are major causes of illness (Light, 2018).

In Southern folk medicine, illness patterns are as singular as the people they affect. Chronic illness is based on complex patterns and breaks in the synergy of a multi-organ system, and an individual with a long-term disorder may not exhibit the same symptoms as another individual with the same disorder due to differences in constitution. Health protocols ought to be as individual and customized as the individuals they’re targeting. 

Zooming out to gauge the whole picture of health, including genetic predisposition, constitution, diet, mental health, environment, relationships, and attitudes is just as, if not more, important than independent lab values, diagnoses, or isolated symptoms. The stories we tell ourselves via these factors directly impact our daily health status. 

Cultural influences play a major role in our individual health. Individuals should take an active role of accountability in maintaining their own health. None of us are separate from the earth. What heals our bodies, heals the earth. What heals the earth, heals our bodies (P.D. Light, personal communication, March 2015).

The meaning and symbolism of blood, the “river of life” and most important body part in this tradition, is of utmost significance. Throughout one’s life, the quality—thick or thin, hot or cold, high or low—and flavor of one’s blood shifts according to surroundings, beliefs, spiritual connections, and choices (Light, 2008). 

What Is Narrative Herbalism?

There is a synergistic effect when the two modalities we’ve examined combine together seamlessly. Narrative medicine and herbalism are two of the oldest health traditions worldwide, and both individually and together, they are valid, accessible, safe, and effective forms of complementary care today. 

“Storytelling may not have the biological effects of an antibiotic or offer the physical transformation of surgery, but it enables patients to take control of their illness in a way that pharmaceuticals can’t” (Mohammed, 2018, para. 5), notes one journalist. The power of narrative is potentiated when combined with physiologically transformative herbs. 

Narrative herbalism, the crossroads where infusions of story and herbal infusions of plants which have the ability to shape story meet, is a unique lens through which to view healing. We will examine the overlap of these two perspectives and the ways in which they tune to and catalyze each other. 

Narrative medicine, which embraces multiple truths being held at the same time and goes beyond rigid, depersonalized diagnoses and treatment plans in a clinical setting, creates a space of subjectivity which herbalists can smoothly enter into. Both narrative medicine and herbalism blend the objective markers of health, from vital signs to endocrine blood work, with the subjective stories of wellness, from one’s grandmother’s history with a cardiovascular condition to one’s relationship with joint tension and spinal discomfort as a reflection of emotional flexibility.

Herbalists value the innate presence of subconscious story within all people; the narratives we carry about our bodies are part of our cellular structure and these shape our thoughts and vice versa. Our cells are the embodiment of our stories. 

Perhaps before even naming this understanding, herbalists share a value around story being a vital part of a client’s ability to heal. Herbalists are educators and vessels of empowerment, believing that education is agency and that the delicate balance between both kingdoms of health and of illness is nuanced and complex.

Conditions vs. People

One tenet which glues narrative medicine to herbalism is the distinction between diseases and people. Both story-centric care and herbalism focus on the person, rather than the “chief complaint.” Working toward health goals and empowered systems of self care is distinct from working to overcome an illness.

There is no one universal pathway to health for everyone. Even Panax ginseng, often thought of as a panacea, is not a magic bullet for every human’s health. It’s too drying for some and not stimulating enough for others. This is a basic principle of herbalism, and one that allows herbalists to sneak in the back door to narrative medicine. Herbalists prize individualized formulas for individual clients and view people as complex amalgams of biology, genetics, choice, belief, and physiology. 

Likewise, narrative medicine can be the gateway to opening up to the possibilities of herbal practice, a truth which goes both ways. These two toolboxes are dual ends of the long drawbridge between story and science. Both herbalism traditions introduced above teach that the storied way of knowing is equally as important as the scientific way.

Plants as Teachers

Holistic herbalists possess an ingrained understanding of the sacred intelligence of plants and an essential perception of plants as healers. This fundamental tenet of nature as relationship and reciprocal dance—rather than simply just place, environment, or resource—is built into the strong foundation of this approach. 

Just as the therapeutic success of client and mental health therapist is well known to be largely based in the subjective rapport and quality of regard within the relationship, rather than objective reflections offered or lifestyle changes made, in herbalism there is high value to be found in one’s relationship dynamic with a plant rather than simply the material impact of ingesting the constituents themselves. Deconstructing the power of herbs into nothing more than the various alkaloids, terpenes, volatile oils, and minerals that make up a leaf or blossom does not take into account the energetics of a plant and the relational journey that has coexisted between plants and humans as long as the two have coexisted. 

The spectrum of plants as teachers has a vast range, from the big teachers which can catalyze major shifts in patterns of addiction, thought, and behavior, such as psilocybin mushrooms and ayahuasca, to the lesser-known instructors of just-as-critical lessons in liver health, resilience, and digestive tenacity, like the common fierce dandelion (Taraxacum officinale). 

Plants as teachers represent opportunities for interdependence where we humans can explore active listening, attentiveness, and intuition. The complexities of health and illness are the icebergs of which we recognize only the surfaces, a lesson which both plants and narrative teach freely. St. John’s wort (Hypericum perforatum), for example, when used as a “natural mood enhancer,” might come with a range of unexpected “side effects,” such as destroying bacteria, fending off viruses, easing nerve damage, supporting liver health, and aiding sore muscles. These are just a handful of the possibilities this plant carries. 

Herbal practitioners have much to learn from herbs about how to hold space for healing without allowing ego to dominate the interaction. Plants have much to share about humility, lack of ego, and the ability to be comfortable in the ambiguity of the unknown. For example, it’s not always possible to pinpoint the exact mechanism of action of a plant on the body, mind, and spirit, as seen in the case of mimosa. 

Mimosa (Albizia julibrissin), the tree of happiness, has a long history of being used to uplift the human heart and bring joy and light to the griever. We know from a scientific perspective that mimosa flower appears to have an effect on mood through the serotonin pathways (proven in animal studies with potential to work similarly in humans), but this empirical explanation doesn’t do justice to mimosa’s remarkable ability to flood people with happiness or unblock emotional congestion just by looking at or smelling the blossoms (Kim et al., 2004).

The intangible qualities of herbs that most herbalists witness on some level are partially a byproduct of our relationships with the plants with which we choose to work. The simple power of creating a ritual involving an herb, for instance, keeping a bottle of mimosa bark and flower elixir near you during a time of grief and taking drop doses while echoing a mantra, can be a powerful story shifter and way of reclaiming authority over one’s experience. 

Narrative Herbalism in Practice

How might narrative herbalism show up when moved out of a theoretical framework and into the day-to-day? There are an unlimited number of ways to interpret what’s possible within this field. Below you will find a few ideas to kickstart your imagination in the clinic or one’s personal life: 

  • encouraging clients to connect with the natural world through intentional unstructured time outside, forest bathing, outdoor exercise, allying with a particular plant and doing a focused meditation with it, etc.; 
  • emphasizing the importance of developing a relationship with a primary plant in a client’s herbal protocol (by cooking with, tasting, or growing the herb, or creating art inspired by the plant, etc.); 
  • asking clients to write (or draw, document, sing, verbally narrate, or otherwise express) their stories of health and illness, including any plants they have worked with on their wellness journey;  
  • employing methods of intake, traditional energetics, constitutional insights, and other frameworks of understanding from herbal traditions (e.g., Western herbalism, Ayurveda, Chinese medicine, and/or other bioregional and cultural traditions) that the herbalist is connected with and using these various languages of the body as a way of interpreting and tending to the individual experience;
  • engaging with the language of the plants—from environmental factors to the Doctrine of Signatures—as a tool to understand the complexity of herbalism and matchmake certain plants with people and conditions; and 
  • increasing one’s own capacity as caregiver to be a model of empathy, attention, awareness, humility, and ability to tolerate ambiguity. This could mean deciding to use burdock (Arctium lappa) instead of yellow dock (Rumex crispus) in a bitters formula because burdock’s spiny, cloying qualities more closely match the complaints of the recipient or incorporating flower essences for anxiety into an herbal regimen as a way of reaching the emotional root of an individual’s stress.

Narrative Medicine for the Clinical Herbalist

Clinical herbalists find themselves in a niche within narrative medicine because of a pattern that is all too common. Clients often turn to a variety of natural health practitioners, including herbalists, for support after finding themselves not “seen” or listened to by conventional caregivers after pursuing that route to its fullest extent. 

For the herbal practitioner incorporating narrative into their practice, one key element is recognizing the importance of intake for clients who may never have been asked to tell their story before. Putting the spotlight on the value of client narratives in the intake process necessitates recognizing patients as multidimensional humans with a storied past that may hold clues to their current presentation of illness. 

The physical and energetic blank space on a page or comfortable, open silence within a room allows clients to envision themselves as a potent force in their own journey to health. Through this initial act of story sharing, clients are able to identify themselves as authors (derived from the word “authority”) in their own lives, sometimes for the first time. 

Narrative Medicine for the Community and Family Herbalist

Narrative medicine in the home and community is so intertwined and par for the healing course that it is innately understood and self explanatory. “Tell me where it hurts,” and, “How do you feel?” are easy entry points into the story of home health, which still today remains the most common type of healthcare practiced worldwide. 

A steaming bowl of savory chicken soup dotted with garlic (Allium sativum) is history, tradition, comfort, and science all wrapped up in one package. Narrative herbalism is steeped into the cup of chamomile (Matricaria chamomilla) tea placed beside the bed of the feverish child, and woven into the matted nest of astragalus (Astragalus mongholicus) roots the harvester brings in from the garden, ready to untangle, wash, and prepare into broth, where they will provide immune support for the winter ahead. 

Story medicine is the first ingredient in every community care interaction, from lending an open ear while brewing linden (Tilia americana) infusion to lift the heart and moisten the sinuses to throwing petals from grandmother’s roses (Rosa spp.) in a postpartum sitz bath to soothe tender tissues. 

Tools for Practicing Narrative Herbalism 

Making concrete recommendations to clients as to how they might integrate narrative into their holistic health protocol is a wonderful place to begin bringing awareness to the power of these practices, as is trying them on yourself at home and in your community. Try using the following sample suggestions and expanding upon these to suit your own approach:

  • Spend a few minutes a day with the plant (fresh or dried, essential oil, or whatever part is easiest to access, even a photo or artistic representation of it) that represents the main part of your current adaptogenic or mood-regulating formula (reishi mushroom (Ganoderma spp.), for example) and write down what you are feeling before and after. You may consider something like expressing gratitude and appreciation to the plant for how it helps bring you back into balance after a stressful day.
  • Take a dropper of motherwort (Leonurus cardiaca) extract and notice how your sense of your heartbeat and manifestation of anxiety changes. Journal about that for 5 minutes.
  • Make a cup of tea from a plant that calls to you and notice what memories it brings up for you.
  • Write a story about your own health or illness state from the perspective of one of your favorite plants that has been a part of your healing.
  • Select a plant ally and apply it to your body (ingesting it if appropriate) or sit with it in some form and observe what happens.
  • Soak in a luxurious lavender (Lavandula spp.) or calming chamomile (Matricaria chamomilla) bath twice a week while listening to a story or reading. Notice how your body responds.
  • Write the narrative of your chronic pain and include as characters three herbs that you are finding helpful in your processing of it.
