Session 3: Pitta-Type Digestive Imbalances

60 minutes · Easy

Getting at the Roots

There is a saying amongst Ayurvedic practitioners: “Ten years into the woods; ten years out of the woods.” This essentially means that illness (and wellness) do not happen overnight. If someone has a serious disease or ailment, it typically took a long time for that state of imbalance to develop. Therefore, it is unreasonable to expect a quick fix for an illness that took many years to accumulate. Renowned Ayurvedic practitioner and teacher Dr. Vasant Lad is fond of saying, “Ayurveda is not a quick fix.” Ayurveda does not mask our problems or offer a bandaid; rather it resolves ill health at the most foundational level.

Pitta dosha is one of the three doshas, or major energetic forces, in Ayurveda and is comprised of both fire and water elements. When you picture pitta dosha, think primarily of fire and just a little bit of water. In its pure form, pitta’s energy is illuminative, and when pitta types are balanced they have the capacity to be remarkably logical, perceptive, and courageous. Some of the qualities that describe pitta dosha are hot, sharp, oily, sour, and intense.

Anyone, regardless of their inherent constitution, can develop pitta-type digestive disturbances. However, those with a predominant pitta constitution are simply much more likely to have an imbalance of a pitta nature. Regarding pitta dosha, Ayurvedic practitioner and author Sebastian Pole (2013) writes, “[Pitta] is pungent, hot, penetrating, greasy, oily, sharp, liquid, spreading and sour. Its primary function is transformation. It is the force of metabolic activity in the body associated with the endocrine function, hormone levels, digestion, body temperature, visual perception, hunger, thirst and skin quality” (p. 26). This is an apt description of the qualities and functions of pitta and a concise summary of the types of disturbances that may reflect a pitta imbalance:  the imbalances in the digestive system that are driven by pitta dosha reflect this hot, oily, sour, sharp, intense nature. Some examples of digestive disturbances that are often associated with excessive pitta dosha include: ulcerative colitis, diarrhea, burning indigestion, acid reflux, and celiac disease.

The Five Pitta Subdoshas

There are five subdoshas of pitta.

Alochaka pitta resides in the eyes and relates to our sense of vision.

Sadhaka pitta is centered in the heart and relates to our ability to digest intellectual information.

Ranjaka pitta is centered in the liver and spleen and gives color to our blood.

Bhrajaka pitta resides in the skin, metabolizes light, processes touch, and is responsible for the health of our complexion.

Pachaka pitta is located in the small intestine and is largely responsible for the breakdown and absorption of our food at the level of the small intestine (Pole, 2013). It is also the basis for the other forms of pitta, is a source of heat and metabolism for the body, and paramount to consider whenever there is a pitta imbalance of any kind (Frawley, 2000). In terms of digestion, pachaka pitta is inarguably central.

Qualities of Pitta Dosha

Those with a strong pitta nature tend to have a sharp, strong appetite and the need to eat regular meals. The modern colloquial term “hangry” is an apt description of what happens to a pitta type when they have to wait too long for a meal. Whereas a vata type may forget to eat or become light-headed and dizzy when their blood sugar gets low, pittas are more likely to get so wrapped up in their work that they neglect to make proper time for a meal. This often results in agitation, crankiness, and potentially burning indigestion or similar issues. This can become a very problematic habit for those with a strong pitta nature. Skipping or postponing meals only fans pitta’s hot digestive fire and increases the intense, sharp pitta nature.

The benefits of a strong pitta digestion are a robust metabolism and the ability to digest a wide range of foods. However, when the flames of digestion burn too hot, that fire can begin to attack the body’s own tissues. Pittas can literally burn themselves up. Initial signs of a pitta imbalance may include a sour taste in the mouth and burning indigestion (Frawley, 2000). If these initial indicators are left without remediation, more severe states of illness will likely develop down the road. Thus, it is critical that individuals with a pitta nature and accompanying pitta digestion learn to keep their digestive fires balanced so that assimilation is optimal, the mood stays balanced, the gut lining and tissues of the digestive system remain healthy, and elimination of wastes is performed at an optimal level. Timing and quantity of food consumption are as important as the foods and herbs consumed. However, when dealing with a particular doshic imbalance, such as excess pitta, the approach must be multi-pronged. For those with a pitta imbalance, immense benefit can be derived by adopting a pitta pacifying diet, taking pitta pacifying herbs, and adopting a pitta pacifying lifestyle.

Pitta Lifestyle Suggestions


Although food is incredibly important, it certainly isn’t the only component in cultivating optimal health and balance. Factors such as sleep, work habits, exercise, and social and familial connections have an immense impact on health and overall wellbeing. Also, Ayurveda teaches that how we eat is as important as what we eat, and that it’s not just what we eat; it’s what we digest. 

For those with a predominant pitta constitution, reminders to cool down and slow down are generally helpful. In terms of eating habits,  it is especially important for pitta types not to skip meals or work through meal times, thus ignoring hunger signals. Also, choosing surroundings and practices that encourage a sense of mental rest and ease is a good idea. This can be done both during and outside of meal times.

Pitta-types may benefit from setting reminders to take computer or work breaks, and from making a deliberate effort to take time off their work, duties, and responsibilities, not only for self-care but also to connect with loved ones and simply enjoy life. Engaging in non-competitive activities done purely for relaxation, enjoyment, and connection will help bring balance for pitta-types. Activities (and non-activities) that encourage both physical and mental coolness are supportive. This can take the form of avoiding hot temperatures and exposure to intense sunlight, spending time near cool bodies of water, and taking time to relax, laugh, play, and be spontaneous.

Beyond spending time in conducive natural environments, in terms of color choices, green, blue, white, lavender, and silver are colors that calm and balance the fires of pitta. 

Foods for Cooling Pitta 

As discussed in Session 1, Ayurveda teaches that there are six tastes: sweet, sour, salty, bitter, pungent, and astringent. Due to pitta’s excessive heat, sharpness, and oiliness, the basis of a pitta pacifying food program is foods that are bitter, sweet, and astringent in nature. The bitter and astringent tastes help to clear the blood, liver, and gallbladder of excess pitta. The sweet taste is both cooling and nutritive, which is especially helpful for pitta types with a very fast metabolism and a strong appetite. In fact, the sweet, bitter, and astringent tastes are all cooling and thus calm down the overly hot pitta digestion, known as tikshnagni (Frawley, 2000).

Examples of the sweet taste are rice, oats, dates, cow’s milk, and natural sweeteners such as maple syrup and coconut sugar. The bitter and astringent tastes are found abundantly in vegetables. Dark leafy greens such as kale, mustard greens, beet greens, and collards all contain the bitter taste. Integrative doctor Akil Palanisamy (2015) teaches that beet greens are one of the greatest liver cleansers available. The liver, being associated closely with pitta, is often disrupted in pitta type imbalances. Pitta types who suffer from acne, skin rashes, premenstrual syndrome (PMS), and irritability will likely benefit from a little extra work clearing out the liver. Beet greens are one of the best foods to incorporate in one’s regular diet in order to assist in healthy liver detoxification (Palanisamy, 2015).

The astringent taste is found abundantly in cruciferous vegetables, such as broccoli, cauliflower, Brussels sprouts, and cabbage. Pitta types do well to incorporate abundant amounts of dark leafy greens and cruciferous vegetables in their diet on a regular basis. During the fall, winter, and spring these vegetables should be cooked most of the time. However, pitta types who have strong digestion can have salad and raw vegetables, especially in the warmer months. If you experience excessive gas or bloating and are eating a lot of raw foods, it is advisable to focus more on cooked vegetables with pitta pacifying spices.

Cooling Down with Spices

Spices not only offer flavor, but are also incredibly nutrient dense, and have the ability to profoundly affect the energetics of a dish. Cooling spices are best for pitta types. Pungent (hot) spices, such as cayenne (Capsicum annuum), garlic (Allium sativum), and clove (Syzygium aromaticum) are very aggravating for pitta dosha, so should be avoided. Herbs and spices with a cool and bitter taste will calm the fires of pitta dosha, and have traditionally been applied to balance a hot agni and burning sensations in the digestive system.

Some favorable culinary spices for pitta dosha include dil​l (Anethum graveolens), cumin (Cuminum cyminum), rose (Rosa spp.) petals, turmeric (Curcuma longa), cardamom (Elettaria cardamomum), and saffron (Crocus sativus). Saffron is in fact a wonderful spice for cleansing the blood, detoxing the liver, and tonifying the nerves (Lad, 2009). A lovely way to take saffron is to use it as a garnish over mildly spiced warm milk or rice pudding. You can also add a little saffron to your rice during the cooking process. Coconut is also a very beneficial pitta reducing food, and can be taken in the form of coconut water, coconut milk, fresh coconut meat, and dried shredded coconut. One way to enjoy cooling pitta-pacifying herbs and spices is to make chutney. 

Cooling Pitta-Pacifying Cilantro Mint Chutney

Chutneys are a fabulous way to bring a little more flavor and excitement to common dishes. I often find that I forget to prepare chutneys at home, but when I do, I remember how delightful and easy they are. The mint and cilantro in this chutney is cooling, and thus beneficial for pitta dosha. It is best suited for consumption in the summer months. Chutneys such as this one are also nice as a condiment served on the side of hot, spicy dishes to help cool down while eating.

Ingredients

½ cup lime juice, freshly squeezed (can substitute lemon juice)

¼ cup water or coconut water

2 cups fresh cilantro, roughly chopped

1 cup fresh mint leaves

½ cup unsweetened dried coconut flakes

¼ cup green peas

⅓ avocado 

3 tablespoons ginger (Zingiber officinale) rhizome, peeled and diced

2 teaspoon maple syrup

½  teaspoon sea salt

½ teaspoon black pepper, freshly ground

Directions

  • Simply blend all ingredients in a food processor or blender until smooth.
  • Keep in a jar in the refrigerator for up to a week.

Understanding Nuance

It is important to note that sometimes digestive ailments can be quite complex. Excess heat generally indicates a pitta imbalance, but it is always important to take the whole person into account. For instance, acid reflux, though burning in nature, may involve a vata imbalance as well as excess pitta. Therefore, if you are dealing with a chronic or serious condition, it is always best to get a proper evaluation by an Ayurvedic practitioner, as well as a medical specialist.

Regardless of your particular condition, there are some spices that are broadly safe and beneficial. Coriander (Coriandrum sativum) is a fabulous spice for pitta types, used as either the powdered seed or as fresh cilantro leaves. It is sweet, astringent, and cooling, and has a delightful and versatile flavor that goes well in many savory dishes. It is commonly used in both Mexican and Indian cooking in order to balance the heat of spicy dishes (Dass, 2013). Fennel (Foeniculum vulgare) is also a fabulous spice for soothing and cooling digestion. Fennel tea works very effectively at pacifying excessive gas, as well as soothing signs of overly hot digestion. It strengthens agni without overheating pitta (Dass, 2013). In my Ayurveda practice, I have personally experienced great success in pacifying pitta digestive disturbances using herbal formulas that incorporate fennel with demulcents such as marshmallow (Althaea officinalis) and shatavari (Asparagus racemosus).

Herbs for Calming Pitta 

There are a number of helpful herbs and herbal preparations that target an imbalanced pitta gut. Aloe juice or pure aloe gel, called kumari in Ayurveda, is incredibly balancing for pitta dosha on the whole (Dass, 2013). If a pitta individual presents with acne, a reddish complexion, PMS, or other signs of a congested liver, incorporating pure aloe juice or aloe gel is traditionally recommended. Aloe (Aloe vera) is soothing to ulcerated tissues as well, and as Ayurvedic practitioner Vishnu Dass (2013) writes, “Aloe vera gel or juice…has a purifying and rejuvenating action upon the blood, liver, spleen, gastrointestinal tract and reproductive tissues” (p. 135). As a practitioner, I typically suggest 2-4 ounces per day of aloe juice or gel to those who present with certain types of pitta imbalances. It is always best to start out with a smaller dose to see how the body receives it, and this can be increased if no apparent discomfort results.

When looking for an aloe juice or aloe gel to purchase for internal use, it is best to get the inner filet only. The whole leaf may contain a natural form of latex, which can be an irritant for some people. It is also important to note that dried aloe is actually a very strong laxative, and quite different from aloe juice or aloe gel. Powdered aloe is considered to be a purgative and is very depleting when taken for an extended time period (Dass, 2013).

Due to pitta’s overly hot nature, pitta types require digestive herbs that are moist, sweet, and soothing, because the overly strong pachaka pitta and high agni have a way of irritating the internal tissues of the gastrointestinal tract. Bitter herbs may also be appropriate, and will be discussed shortly. Herbs that are moist and sweet include shatavari, licorice (Glycyrrhiza glabra), and marshmallow. Licorice tea is quite soothing for pitta individuals. It not only has the effect of soothing digestion; it is also a rejuvenative for the adrenals (Dass, 2013). This is an added benefit, especially for pitta types who are prone to overwork, and thus adrenal fatigue.

Shatavari is a wonderful rejuvenative, especially for women (Pole, 2013). However, it can also be incorporated into herbal formulas meant to soothe the digestive tract. Marshmallow is particularly moist and soothing and is great for helping to alleviate problematic digestive disturbances, such as ulcerated tissues and diarrhea (Dass, 2013). Shatavari, licorice, and marshmallow can all be taken in various modes of preparation. For supporting the digestive system it is generally best to either take them as a whole powder, mixed into a hot liquid, or in capsule form. You can also make teas and decoctions with the whole bark or root of the plant. Herbal ghees are also a lovely, very nourishing way to receive the herbs. For those who run dry or who are prone to depletion, ghee has the added benefit of being nutritive and moistening.

As mentioned previously, bitter herbs may also be considered when dealing with excess pitta. The bitter taste is cool and very pacifying to the raging fires of pitta. Examples of bitter herbs include dandelion (Taraxacum officinale), chicory (Cichorium intybus), and kutki (Picrorhiza kurroa). These herbs are often employed to cleanse and cool the liver and blood. Since the liver plays a powerful role in digestion, these bitter herbs may be useful in cooling down pitta type digestive troubles (Dass, 2013).

A great way to take bitter herbs on a regular basis is in the form of a root “coffee.” Various commercial root coffee substitutes are widely available today in health food stores. These are typically made out of dandelion or chicory, and possibly grains such as rye and barley. While they won’t give you a caffeine boost, they do have a rich, roasted flavor, and can be taken with milk or cream, just like coffee. Some ayurvedic herbal suppliers make special ayurvedic “coffee blends” that incorporate herbs such as ashwagandha (Withania somnifera) and kapi kacchu (Mucuna pruriens), offering added health benefits. Or, you can make your own!

It is important to mention that although foods, herbs, and spices have profound benefits when chosen wisely, Ayurveda always considers the entire context of a person’s life. Ayurveda seeks to address the whole person, not just an external signal. Therefore, lifestyle changes may be required to truly rectify a health imbalance. This could mean a change in eating habits, sleep habits, communication habits, stress management practices, or even a change in location or occupation.

Conclusion

Due to changes in season, life phase, and innumerable other circumstances, our present balance of the doshas changes, too. Thus, balance is in many ways a moving target. However, if one is able to understand the nature (dosha/s) of the individual and the energetics of the foods and herbs consumed, Ayurveda serves as a remarkable guide that is expansive, specific, and flexible. If a digestive imbalance is characterized by heat, sharpness, inflammation, and/or burning sensations, then the likelihood of pitta dosha playing a role is high. Therefore, choosing herbs and foods that balance pitta dosha will produce favorable results. Foods and herbs that are sweet, bitter, astringent, and cool in nature will generally have pitta-balancing effects. Think cilantro, mint, coconut, rice, bitter greens, cooling herbal teas, and mellow afternoons—preferably enjoyed in the shade by a river, lake, or stream!
