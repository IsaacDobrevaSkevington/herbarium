Session 2: Vata-Type Digestive Imbalances

60 minutes · Easy

From the Cosmos to Earth

It is a well-known theory of modern science that we are made of stardust. As Carl Sagan put it so famously in the PBS television series, Cosmos: “The nitrogen in our DNA, the calcium in our teeth, the iron in our blood, the carbon in our apple pies were made in the interiors of collapsing stars. We are made of starstuff” (Sagan, 1980). These elements, as well as oxygen, phosphorus, potassium, sulfur, sodium, chlorine, magnesium and a few others, combine to form human matter (Herlihy, 2011).

Now let’s take this one step further. Both modern science and Ayurveda teach us that what appears to be solid matter is actually tiny particles in a state of constant motion. “As modern science confirms, matter is energy and what seems solid is a static appearance of innumerable, subtle moving currents” (Frawley, 2000, p. 7). Both yoga and Ayurveda teach us that our life force is something called prana, and the force behind all motion is something called vata.

Furthermore, according to Ayurveda and yoga, all matter (including humans and all that we take in through our five senses) can be classified into five elements. These are the elements of nature, and more macroscopic than carbon, nitrogen and oxygen. The five elements are earth, water, fire, air, and ether, and they not only exist in a very real way, they also represent qualities. Thus, we are each a unique and brilliant combination of stardust, space, wind, water, earth, and fire.

These five elements then combine in various ways to make up the three doshas in Ayurveda: vata, pitta, and kapha. The doshas are sometimes referred to as “cosmic forces.” “Ayurveda recognizes three primary life-forces in the body, or three biological humors called vata, pitta, and kapha, which correspond to the elements of air, fire, and water….vata is the biological humor, also translated as wind. It means ‘that which moves things’” (Frawley, 2000, p. 9). Vata is necessary as it is the driving force behind all bodily and mental movements, including the movement of thought and the natural contractions of the gut and intestines known as peristalsis, circulation, and nerve impulses.

Learn more about the three doshas here: https://theherbalacademy.com/introduction-to-ayurveda/

In terms of the digestive system, a healthy level of vata gives rise to timely digestion and elimination. Vata governs motion, so anywhere there is movement in the body, vata is responsible. Thus, when vata is balanced, there is a healthy amount of movement in the digestive tract. Food is digested in a timely manner without excessive gas or bloating, and elimination happens properly.

However, when vata becomes excessive and out of balance, a number of symptoms may ensue. Some indicators of imbalanced vata are: excessive gas, constipation, bloating, erratic appetite, nervous stomach, acid reflux, and herniated tissues.

The Five Vata Subdoshas

Beneath the umbrella of vata exists the vata subdoshas, also known as the five vayus. The vayus are prana vayu, samana vayu, vyana vayu, udana vayu, and apana vayu. The five vayus exist in the subtle body. However, they also govern key physiological functions, such as those involved in healthy digestion.

Prana vayu governs the intake of sensory impressions. It primarily deals with information transmitted from one’s environment and into the sensory sphere. “Prana vayu draws the energy we call prana into the body through each of the five senses” (Halpern, 2012, p. 60). Prana is carried on the breath, as well as in food and water.

Samana vayu means “balancing air” and it moves from the periphery to the center of the body (Halpern, 2012). It also carries information across cells and membranes, as in the case of carrying nutrients from the intestines to the bloodstream. Samana vayu is key in digestion as it governs absorption, and its main site is in the small intestine (Halpern 2012). If someone is having trouble absorbing nutrients, this means that samana vayu is compromised.

While samana vayu moves from outside in, vyana vayu moves from the inside out. Vyana vayu governs circulation and means “diffusing air” (Halpern, 2012). Vyana vayu plays an important role in the movement of nerve energy, the circulation of thought, sweating, blood flow, heartbeat, and the movement of joints (Halpern, 2012). Vyana vayu is not as directly involved in digestion as the samana, apana, and udana vayus. However, it is important for circulating nutrients throughout the bloodstream and to the organs. Also, all the vayus must be working in healthy conjunction for optimal digestion and elimination to occur.

Udana vayu is the upward and outward moving air. It is related to muscular action, exhalation, and expression, as it is “responsible for taking the prana and putting it to work” (Halpern, 2012, p. 63). In terms of digestion, udana vayu governs the actions of vomiting as well as regurgitation of stomach acid, as in the case of acid reflux.

Apana vayu, being the downward and outward moving air, plays a critical role in digestion, namely in that it governs all eliminatory processes. Apana vayu is responsible for eliminating all waste products, including feces, urine, and menstrual flow. When apana vayu is healthy, bowel movements are formed and happen with ease one to three times a day. If apana vayu is disturbed, one may experience constipation or diarrhea.

All of the vayus need to be functioning in healthy synchronicity for vata to remain balanced in the body. When one of the vayus becomes derailed, vata as a whole is vitiated, and various health problems will manifest sooner or later.

Qualities of Vata Dosha


It is critical to understand how vata gets out of balance. Ayurveda teaches us that harmony equals health, and disharmony leads to disease. Thus, all healthy imbalances are the result of living out of balance with our true nature and the rhythms of the natural world.

Vata is light, cold, dry, and rough in nature, so excessive amounts of light, dry, cold foods, as well as irregular eating habits will cause an increase in vata over time. Furthermore, those with more vata in their constitution will naturally be more likely to develop a vata imbalance. Again, some of the indicators of a vata imbalance in the digestive system are gas, bloating, constipation, and an erratic appetite. Acid reflux and diarrhea can also be driven by vata. However, pitta dosha typically plays a role in these imbalances as well, as pitta is connected to excess heat and acidity.

Generally speaking, warmth, oil, and regular routines balance vata. Thus, foods that are warm, heavy, and eaten at regular times will bring calm to a derailed vata dosha. Conversely, the classical texts warn us that eating foods which our incompatible for our constitutional type will have grave results: “Persons who indulge in partaking incompatible foods, overeating, and eating of uncooked foods develop the dreaded Amadosa which is similar to poison” (Murthy, 2013, p. 126). “Amadosa” likely refers to an extreme version of ama, or undigested food matter in Ayurveda.

According to renowned Ayurvedic practitioner and scholar, Dr. David Frawley (2000), “[a]ll anti-vata therapies are nurturing, warming, moistening, calming, and grounding” (p. 68). Furthermore, Frawley states that food should be nutritive and strengthening, emphasizing the sweet, sour, and salty tastes (Frawley, 2000).

Balancing Vata with Lifestyle

Ayurveda teaches that “how you eat is as important as what you eat.” Another common Ayurvedic aphorism is “it’s not what you eat, it’s what you digest.” Even if your food choices are perfect, if you are not eating with awareness, the benefits of your food will only carry your body so far.

Since our nervous system is intimately involved in digestion, when one sits down to eat, it is important to put the nervous system in “rest and digest” mode rather than “fight or flight” mode. This way your body will be able to put its blood flow and resources toward digesting, rather than other activities.

Here are some practical tips for creating the perfect internal digestive environment (adapted from the California College of Ayurveda Guidelines for Healthy Eating, 2012):

  1. Eat without distraction and in a peaceful setting. This means no TV, phone, or computer. It is best not to even read a book or a newspaper while eating. Paying attention to what you eat and taking the time to truly enjoy your food will greatly contribute to better digestion, as well as greater appreciation for the meal!
  2. Begin each meal with a few slow breaths and/or an expression of gratitude. Our breath is intimately linked to the state of our nervous system, so slowing your breath will help bring you into a more present and receptive state. The start of a meal is also a great time to express a simple gratitude or two, either silently or aloud. The significance of gratitude expression is the crux of much of the current positive psychology research, and the results are encouraging: “After 10 weeks, those who wrote about gratitude were more optimistic and felt better about their lives. Surprisingly, they also exercised more and had fewer visits to physicians than those who focused on sources of aggravation” (Miller, 2012).
  3. Chew your food to an even consistency before swallowing. A practical tip is to put your utensil down between bites. Chewing properly will ensure that your salivary enzymes can work on your food before it reaches your gut, thus making the digestive process more efficient.
  4. Eat at a moderate pace until 75% full. I have seen drastic improvements in digestive health and weight reduction in those who have been successful at implementing this guideline. One trick to being able to tell when you are 75% full is what I have coined “the burp of honesty.” If you are paying good attention to your meal, for most people there will be a point in your meal where you have a small burp. This is an excellent indicator that it is time to put the fork down. In my experience, about 90% of the people I have worked with experience the burp. For those who do not, a sigh of satisfaction is a good indicator that the meal is done. You want to end your meal sated, not stuffed.
  5. Don’t rush into work or heavy physical exertion directly following a meal. Give yourself at least 10-15 minutes to digest. During this time it is fine to take a leisurely stroll or to read a light book or engage in pleasant conversation. However, stressing your body and mind will detract from your digestive capacity.

Balancing Vata with Taste

The taste of food is the cornerstone of implementing a vata-pacifying food program. In Ayurveda, importance is placed on the taste of the food. As mentioned in Session 1, there are six tastes: sweet, sour, salty, bitter, pungent, and astringent. Ayurveda teaches that we need all six tastes in order to have a balanced diet. However, the recommended proportion of the tastes varies from person to person, depending on their dosha.

The sweet, salty, and sour tastes are the most heavy, moist, and nourishing tastes and therefore are the most vata-balancing. Examples of sweet foods include ghee, dates, whole milk dairy, whole grains (especially white basmati rice), wheat, oats, and nuts. Salty foods include seaweed, soy sauce, miso, and all forms of salt, such as rock and sea salt. The sour taste is found in fermented foods, such as kimchee, sauerkraut, and yogurt. It is also found in citrus fruits and vinegars. The sour taste has the benefit of being warming and is also the best taste for strengthening the digestion long-term. This is important, as those with vata-type digestion often experience weak or erratic digestion, as well as excessive coldness.

Examples of vata-pacifying dishes are baked or stewed apples; oatmeal with vata-pacifying spices; rice pudding; the classic Ayurvedic dish, kitchari; and root vegetable stews. In my Ayurveda practice,, I have seen incredible results from people simply eating more mindfully and switching to mostly cooked foods as opposed to raw.

Spicing is a crucial component in all Ayurvedic cooking. Not only do spices add flavor and enjoyment to food, they are also profoundly nutrient rich, and have the capacity to sway the qualities of a particular dish. In general, spicing for vata should be warm, but not hot. Cold by nature, vata types require a gentle warming of the digestive system. However, food that is spicy hot will deplete, dry, and overstimulate delicate vata dosha, resulting in more dryness and depletion.

If you think of chai spices and pumpkin pie spices—that is the spice profile that is suitable for vata. Cinnamon (Cinnamomum spp., cardamom (Elettaria cardamomum) , fresh ginger (Zingiber officinale), nutmeg (Myristica fragrans), and fennel (Foeniculum vulgare) are all ideal spices for vata. Cumin (Cuminum cyminum), turmeric (Curcuma longa), and coriander (Coriandrum sativum) also work well. Vata can take small amounts of black pepper (Piper nigrum), clove (Syzygium aromaticum), and even cayenne (Capsicum annuum), but those hot spices should be used in small amounts and with sweeter, milder spices, such as cinnamon, cardamom, fennel, and coriander.

Here is an example of a great vata friendly spice blend. You can easily mix this spice blend at home and keep it on hand to use in a number of dishes.

Vata Spice Mix

Get pleasantly warm and cozy with this Vata Spice Mix. This blend is savory with just a bit of spicy kick, perfect for keeping vata-type digestion balanced and also for stoking the fires of agni particularly in the fall and winter. This recipe specifies the amounts in parts. A part can be an measure you choose, e.g., 1 tsp, 1 tbsp, ¼ cup, etc., depending on the batch size you’d like to make. 

Ingredients

2 parts ginger (Zingiber officinale) rhizome, powdered

2 parts fennel (Foeniculum vulgare) seed, powdered

1 part coriander (Coriandrum sativum) seed, powdered

1 part cumin (Cuminum cyminum) seed, powdered

1 part turmeric (Curcuma longa) rhizome, powdered

1 part black pepper (Piper nigrum) fruit, powdered

Directions

  • Combine all ingredients into a glass jar or spice shaker.
  • Shake well with the cap firmly closed, and use as seasoning for veggies, grains, legumes, and even stewed or baked fruit.
  • Store out of direct sunlight. 

Excessive and uncomfortable gas is one signal that vata is imbalanced in the digestive system. When this happens, one should use the above-mentioned vata-pacifying spices often in cooking. Furthermore, cinnamon (Cinnamomum spp.), cardamom (Elettaria cardamomum), fennel (Foeniculum vulgare), and ginger (Zingiber officinale) have specific carminative qualities, meaning they quell excess gas. For those who can tolerate caffeine, a cup of chai between meals is an appealing way to take a cup full of carminatives. If you are avoiding or minimizing caffeine, you can just prepare a cup of warm milk or water using chai spices. Or, substitute tulsi (Ocimum tenuiflorum) or decaffeinated black tea for regular black tea leaves. 

Here is an easy to follow chai recipe. Goat milk or vegan milk, such as coconut or almond, can be substituted for whole cow’s milk. However, for vata types who can tolerate whole cow’s milk, it is preferred as its qualities are cool, sweet, moist, and heavy.

Greta’s Masala Chai

The chai in India is truly incomparable. Served in small ceramic or glass teacups, it is creamy, sweet, strong, and aromatic. I don’t know that I will ever be able to fully replicate its aromatic magnificence, but this is my humble attempt at creating a good cup of flavorful and delicious masala chai. I have learned through trial and error that the success in making a good cup of chai lies largely in the process. Also, good quality spices make a big difference. CTC Assam is my preference for black tea. It is inexpensive and comes in fine, pellet-like curls. It can be found at specialty tea shops, Indian markets, or online. Or substitute strong loose-leaf black tea or a black tea bag.

Yield: 1 medium-sized mug or 2 small teacups.

Ingredients

1 cup water
3/4 cup milk (full fat dairy milk, oat, coconut, or almond milk work well)
1 tablespoon CTC (cut, torn, curled) black CTC Assam tea or tulsi (Ocimum tenuiflorum) leaf
1/2 teaspoon ginger (Zingiber officinale) rhizome, powdered
1/4 teaspoon cinnamon  (Cinnamomum spp.) bark, powdered
1/4 teaspoon cardamom (Elettaria cardamomum) seed, powdered
1/8 teaspoon black pepper (Piper nigrum) fruit, powdered
A pinch of clove (Syzygium aromaticum) fruit powder
Raw sugar, honey, or maple syrup to taste

Directions

  • Add the water to a small saucepan and bring to a boil. 
  • Add the black tea or tulsi leaf (for a caffeine-free version), cover, and remove from heat. Steep for 2-5 minutes.
  • Add the milk and spices and bring to the very edge of boiling. Small bubbles will start to appear. Careful not to let the milky tea boil over—you may end up with a big mess on your stove if the milk boils!
  • Once the milk just barely starts to boil, remove from heat and steep, covered, for another 2-5 minutes. 
  • Strain, sweeten, and optionally, dust with a pinch of cardamom. 

Balancing the Upward Energy: Udana Vayu

Acid reflux is an interesting condition in that it is an expression of both derailed pitta and vata doshas. Pitta dosha is associated with our stomach acids and digestive enzymes. However, vata, being “that which moves” governs the directional motion of our stomach contents. Thus, when food and stomach acids are coming up rather than going down, this is an expression of udana vayu, the upward and outward moving vayu.

The pacification of acid reflux or GERD (gastroesophageal reflux disease) involves calming both vata and pitta doshas. It is, as always, important for the Ayurvedic practitioner to assess the overall state of agni (digestive power) in the afflicted individual. If the appetite is generally very strong and stools tend to be loose, cool foods, herbs, and spices should be employed. The management of pitta dosha is necessary in such cases. If, on the other hand, the appetite is erratic or weak, and digestion and bowel movements are sluggish, dry, or irregular, vata must be reduced. A warm, moist diet emphasizing the sweet, sour, and salty tastes should be adopted, as well as an appropriate spice and herb protocol.

Sour foods, such as lemons, apple cider vinegar, kimchee, and other fermented foods have the capacity to strengthen agni long-term. It may seem a bit of an oxymoron, but digestion is often weak in cases of acid reflux. Slowed digestion can actually cause stomach acids to wreak havoc on the gut lining. “Impaired oesophageal clearance is responsible for prolonged acid exposure of the mucosa. Delayed gastric emptying, resulting in gastric distension, can significantly increase the rate of transient lower oesophageal sphincter relaxations, contributing to postprandial gastro-oesophageal reflux disease” (Giorgi et al., 2006). Medical jargon aside, essentially the researchers found  that when our stomach acids and food sit around for too long, stomach acids burn the esophagus, causing all manner of digestive discomfort.

Furthermore, in cases of acid reflux, one should always consider the role of the nervous system, both in the brain and in the gut. As doctor and author Giulia Enders (2015) explains,“The nerves of the digestive tract ensure that the esophagus moves things downward in a harmonious stadium-style wave…Practical tips to help with heartburn and reflux are based on trying to get those two nervous systems back on the right path” (p. 94). Enders goes on to list some practical tips for retraining the nervous system of the gut, such as sipping tea and chewing gum between meals. The idea is that you are retraining the nerves involved in swallowing and digesting. It is interesting to note that in the Ayurvedic paradigm, vata rules nerve impulses, so these methods are tools for pacifying and redirecting vata dosha.

In working with acid reflux and GERD, all practices need to encourage vata to move downward. Udana, samana, and apana vayus need to be normalized. If the individual is experiencing constipation, that is one of the first problems that needs to be addressed, which is elucidated further along in this session.

Ginger is a commonly available spice that increases the rate with which the stomach empties its contents, so is a useful daily seasoning for those with weak or sluggish digestion. Ginger can be used as the fresh rhizome or in dried and powdered form, and can be used in cooking or taken as a supplement. The digestive benefits of ginger are not only known to Ayurvedic practitioners and those in holistic circles, there are now current scientific studies to back it up: “One ingenious double-blind placebo-controlled study used ultrasound imaging to show that ginger was effective at accelerating the emptying of the stomach and gently stimulating stomach contractions, thereby improving gastrointestinal motility” (Palanisamy, 2015, p. 117).

On the slightly cooler side, avipattikar churna is a classic Ayurvedic formula indicated in vata-pitta type digestive imbalances. Ayurvedic practitioners have classically used this herb and spice blend to treat excess pitta in the digestive system (Dass, 2013). However, it has the effect of normalizing the direction of vata in the gastro-intestinal tract and soothing hyperacidity. Therefore, avipattikar churna can be helpful when both vata and pitta are vitiated. I have personally seen it to be an effective aid in soothing spasms of the esophagus accompanied by acid-reflux type discomfort. However, there is no single solution in Ayurveda, so as always, it is important to consult with a practitioner, particularly if you have chronic digestive complaints. Avipattikar churna is made of a number of herbs, including amalaki (Emblica officinalis) fruit, haritaki (Terminalia chebula) fruit, and bibhitaki (Terminalia belerica) fruit, which make up triphala, trivrut/nishoth (Operculina turpethum) root, clove (Syzygium aromaticum) bud, black pepper (Piper nigrum) fruit, long pepper (Piper longum) fruit, ginger (Zingiber officinale) rhizome, musta (Cyperus rotundus) rhizome, vidanga (Embelia ribes) fruit, cardamom (Elettaria cardamomum) seed, and Indian cassia leaf/Indian bay leaf/tejapatara (Cinnamomum tamala) in a base of raw sugar (Pole, 2013).

Balancing the Descending Energy: Apana Vayu

Whereas acid reflux and GERD are signals that udana vayu has gone awry, constipation is a classic example of a disturbance in apana vayu, the downward moving energy. Due to vata’s light, dry, erratic nature, constipation is a fairly common occurrence for those with a vata constitution. Dryness in the colon, as well as irregular eating habits, will inevitably lead to constipation.

Nervousness and travel are also likely culprits when looking at the root cause of constipation. Since all of these causes are light, dry, and irregular by nature, the solution is to bring in warmth, oil, and regular routine. Eating foods that are warm, moist, and appropriately spiced will go a long way in soothing vata and helping to normalize bowel movements. Eating and sleeping at regular times will also help.

If constipation is not resolved by changes to diet and lifestyle, there are a number of herbal supports  that may prove beneficial. One of the safest and most effective choices is triphala, a wonderful, simple classical Ayurvedic formula that means “three fruits.” The three fruits that comprise triphala are amalaki, haritaki, and bibhitaki. Traditionally, triphala is made up of an equal proportion of each of these three fruits. “It is tridoshic, deep cleansing and rejuvenating tonic for the entire body” (Dass, 2013, p. 315).

Not enough can be said about triphala’s benefits and uses. However, as is the case with all herbs, making sure your triphala comes from a source that is organic and free of heavy metal contamination is important. Triphala can be taken in capsule, tablet, loose powder, or ghee form. It is generally a safe herb, but it is best to consult with your health practitioner for appropriate dosage and any potential contraindications.

Whereas all individuals with a predominant vata constitution should take precautions to pacify vata dosha with food, spices, herbs, and lifestyle year round, autumn is the season most closely associated with vata dosha. Therefore, all of us should pay close attention to managing vata during autumn, as well as the weeks preceding. Autumn is a cool, dry season, and like vata, is associated with shedding, lightening, and letting go. Thus, it is important for anyone prone to vata-type imbalances to consider adopting a vata-pacifying regime in the fall.

Here are a few useful hints:

  • As summer draws to a close, get back into grounding routines. Eating, sleeping, and exercising at routine times will help keep vata at bay.
  • Since vata is pacified by warmth, oil, and regular routine, autumn is a great time to incorporate soups, stews, kitchari, and roasted root vegetables. Most of your diet should consist of cooked and warmly spiced food.
  • Practicing what Ayurveda calls abhyanga, or oil massage, is an enjoyable and calming practice that is especially important in fall and winter. Abhyanga can be as simple or luxurious as time allows. Essentially, it is the practice of applying a layer of warm oil to the entire body, classically done before bathing. Sesame oil is the most vata-pacifying choice, as it has a warming and unctuous quality.

Autumn is the perfect time of year to get cozy with a cup of chai and a good book, or take a leisurely stroll in the crisp autumn air. By taming vata dosha during this season, you will position yourself well to remain vibrant and healthy throughout fall, winter, and all year long.

Conclusion

Though autumn is the time of year in which vata dosha is most prominent, for those with a vata constitution or vata imbalance, it is a good idea to take measures to tame and ground this windy, mobile dosha all year round. Remember, vata dosha is light, dry, cool, and mobile by nature, so in order to balance vata, focus on foods, herbs, and lifestyle practices that are characterized by warmth and moisture and that encourage regularity and routine. Enjoy a hot, spicy cup of chai, baked apples, roasted root veggies, or a steaming bowl of oatmeal any time of year to balance and calm vata dosha.
