Session 4: Kapha-Type Digestive Imbalances + Dual Doshas

60 minutes · Easy

Kapha Season

As temperatures drop, days become shorter, and we move into deep winter, it is the perfect time to consider balancing excess kapha dosha and any corresponding kapha digestive issues. Some of the characteristics of kapha dosha are moist, heavy, slow, dense, cool, cloudy, and sticky–qualities that all tend to get exacerbated in the midst of winter and early spring. For predominantly pitta or vata types, excess kapha may rarely be an issue. However, winter and spring is the kapha time of year and the season in which kapha issues tend to surface. Therefore, it is useful for everyone to be aware of the influence of kapha during the cooler, darker months of winter and early spring.

The Five Kapha Subdoshas

Like vata and pitta, kapha has five subdoshas, which may also be referred to as the “five forms of kapha.” 

Tarpaka kapha is seated in the brain and relates to contentment, calm, stability, and memory. 

Bodhaka kapha is located in the mouth and tongue and its physical manifestation is saliva, which is a key factor in taste and digestion. 

Avalambaka kapha is located in the heart and lungs; it corresponds to plasma and phlegm. 

Kledaka kapha is located in the stomach in the form of lubrication for the mucosal lining. Its function is to protect the mucosal lining from stomach acids, which relate to pitta dosha. 

Sleshaka kapha is located in the joints as; it corresponds to synovial fluid and the moisture that protects joints from friction and wear and tear (Frawley, 2000; Halpern, 2012). 

Qualities of Kapha Dosha

Since the focus of this session is kapha digestive imbalances and how to palliate such conditions, it is important to understand what a kapha digestive imbalance looks like. In general, kapha types tend to have a slower digestion and metabolism compared to their vata and pitta counterparts. Mandagni is low digestive fire and is most common in kapha types or those with a kapha imbalance. Whereas a pitta type may require three square meals a day, and a vata type several small meals a day, often those with a kapha constitution do bestwith one to two meals per day. This is neither good nor bad, and can certainly make a kapha type’s grocery bill a little easier on their bank account! However, it is especially important for kapha types not to overeat, particularly late at night, and to strictly avoid overburdening their slower digestion and metabolism.

For kapha types, balance typically means strengthening agni and sticking to relatively lighter, warmer foods. Symptoms of low digestive power include nausea, a thick coating on the tongue, weight gain even when caloric intake is low or moderate, and feeling heavy, sleepy or full after meals. Excess mucus is also associated with vitiated (excessive) kapha.

This is how ayurvedic practitioner, author, and scholar David Frawley (2000) sums up low agni and its relationship to kapha: “Agni is usually low in kapha types, with poor appetite, low metabolism and tendency to gain weight even without excess food consumption. There will be excess mucus and congestion” (p. 137). All of these symptoms are a reflection of how excess kapha dampens agni with its heavy, moist, sticky qualities.

Ama

Excess weight and feeling a little sluggish are not where the problems of mandagni, or low digestive fire, end. There is a deeper, consequential issue known as ama. “Most of us suffer from an accumulation of toxins, undigested food particles or waste materials, called ama in Sanskrit” (Frawley, 2000, p. 120). Ayurveda teaches that low or irregular digestive fire results in ama, and ama clogs both our physical and subtle channels, leading to a multitude of health imbalances. Even brain fog is considered to be a reflection of accumulated ama. In the case of brain fog, the mental channels that allow for clarity of thought are literally clogged!

Stoking Agni

Ama is problematic because it interferes with effective absorption of nutrients, the clear communication of hormones and neurotransmitters, and optimal organic function. As I alluded to earlier, there is a direct relationship between agni and ama. According to Frawley (2000), ama weakens the immune system, and therefore most health issues are related to ama buildup in some way.  Dr. Frawley goes on to explain how agni and ama are essentially opposite in qualities, and that when agni is strong and metabolism is balanced, ama is absent.

In the case of low agni, which is the result of aggravated kapha, the answer is to stoke and strengthen the digestive fire. “Sounds great!”, you may be thinking, “but how?” I’ll give you a hint…it all comes back to what’s on your plate, specifically the balance of the six tastes.

Agni and Lifestyle Considerations

As mentioned earlier, diet and lifestyle always form the basis of any ayurvedic protocol. You can take all the herbs in the world, but without making changes to the way you live, little improvement will come. One very important lifestyle consideration when it comes to balancing agni is the timing of meals. Ayurveda teaches that there is a correlation between the patterns of the sun and our agni. When the sun is at its daily peak, typically around noon, this is the time that digestion is usually strongest.

Ayurveda teaches that high noon is the best time to eat the largest meal of the day. Conversely, in the early morning and after sundown, the power of the sun is a little weaker and the same is true of our agni. During these times it is best to stick to lighter meals. For kapha, a light meal might even mean nothing more than a spicy tea or a thin vegetable soup. As always, it’s good to listen to your gut feelings, and if there is an absence of true hunger, it is best to abstain from eating.

Exercise, sweat, and heat exposure are also helpful ways to stimulate agni and to aid the body’s natural detoxification pathways. Strenuous exercise helps stimulate the appetite, and when done several times a week can help re-adjust the body’s fat ideal, or set weight (Svoboda, 1999). Saunas and sunbathing are also classic ayurvedic practices for stimulating agni and removing ama, as these are ways to promote sweating and to invoke the fire element. (Remember that fire characterizes agni, and by increasing the fire element we increase agni.)

In closing, those with kapha-afflicted digestion will benefit from adopting a light, warm, bitter, pungent, astringent diet, and should be careful not to overeat or to eat at odd times of day. Huge strides can be made in balancing agni when you consistently abide by this 75% full recommendation. The 75% rule is especially helpful for those concerned with weight management. In more classical terms, the ancient ayurvedic text the Charaka Samhita states, “The amount of food which, without disturbing the equilibrium (of dhatus and doshas) in the body gets digested as well as metabolized in proper time, is to be regarded as the proper quantity” (Dash, 2014, p. 106). For those with a strong digestive fire it is also important to remember to get enough calories and nutrients. Being underfed can be as problematic as being overfed. 

Those with a predominant kapha constitution should take measures to stimulate agni on all levels, and to remove excess waste, weight, and ama. It is helpful to remember that agni is our ability to digest food and remove wastes on a physical level and subtle level. Agni is also our ability to make sense of ideas and experiences and to do away with notions and emotions that don’t serve us. Kapha types are generally blessed with a hearty and resilient constitution. By keeping their agni balanced, and removing ama on all levels, they can enhance their positive attributes, and allow those qualities of strength, steadiness, and abundance to shine through more cleanly.

The Kapha Food Plan: Bitter, Astringent, and Pungent

The crux of a kapha-balancing food plan is to incorporate the bitter, pungent, and astringent tastes. “[Kapha] people need to concentrate on bitter, pungent, and astringent foods which invigorate their bodies and minds, and should avoid sweet, sour, and salty substances which help them remain set in their ways” (Svoboda, 1999, p. 62). In Ayurveda, it is always helpful to return to the qualities of the doshic imbalance, and this will point the way to the solution. Since kapha is heavy, cool, and moist, a kapha balancing diet is hot, dry, and light. The tastes that embody those qualities are the bitter, pungent, and astringent tastes.

A diet that emphasizes cooked vegetables is one of the best ways to highlight foods that are pungent, bitter, and astringent. Vegetables are low in calories and most possess some combination of light, bitter, and astringent qualities—tastes that alleviate excess kapha. Leafy green vegetables such as kale, collards, beet greens, dandelion greens, and lettuces are especially beneficial for kapha dosha. Cruciferous vegetables, such as cauliflower, broccoli, and Brussels sprouts are also beneficial due to their astringent and bitter nature. Kapha types should be careful not to overdo very earthy or juicy vegetables, such as sweet potatoes, squash, and beets (Svoboda, 1999). Heavy, dense, and very moist foods only increase kapha’s already heavy and dense qualities. In addition, particularly cool and moist vegetables such as asparagus and cucumber should take a back seat to leafy greens and cruciferous vegetables.

The bitter taste supports the body’s natural filtration process and promotes lightness in both the mind and bodily tissues (Frawley, 2000).  Since kapha dosha is heavy, moist, and dense in nature, it stands to reason that the light, purifying, drying effects of the bitter taste would be beneficial for kapha dosha. In fact, the bitter and pungent tastes are considered to be most effective at reducing excess bodily tissues. Ayurveda teaches that the bitter taste dries up excess tissue, and the pungent taste burns excesses away.

Speaking of the pungent taste, chili peppers and hot spices are an emphatic yes on the kapha menu. Kapha types should use spices freely in their cooking. Of the three doshas,  kapha types most benefit from fasting and occasionally skipping meals. Hot spices such as dry ginger (Zingiber officinale), black pepper (Piper nigrum), cayenne (Capsicum annuum), garlic (Allium sativum), and clove (Syzygium aromaticum) all warm the cool dampness of kapha. These pungent spices stimulate and energize, which benefits kapha’s slow, steady constitution. Trikatu is a classic digestive blend that consists of equal parts dry ginger (Zingiber officinale) rhizome, black pepper (Piper nigrum) fruit, and pippali (Piper longum), and is indicated for many kapha-type digestive issues.

Refreshing Grapefruit Berry Smoothie

This refreshing grapefruit berry smoothie is a great example of a tart and refreshing beverage that can be taken by kapha-types on occasion in  lieu of a full meal.

Ingredients

2 large whole grapefruits, fresh and peeled 

¼ cup mixed berries (any combination of blueberries, black berries, raspberries), frozen

1 tablespoon lemon or lime juice

1 1/2 inch piece raw ginger (Zingiber officinale) rhizome, peeled

Directions

  • Simply place all ingredients into a blender and blend until smooth.

I have personally made this beverage many times over, and find it to be amazingly delicious and beneficial, particularly if you are looking for something tart, refreshing, and enlivening.

Hearty Foods for Kapha

As you have probably figured out by now, the kapha food plan should not have an overabundance of hearty fare. Anyone who is suffering from sluggish, kapha-type digestion would do well to stick to lighter, warmer foods. However, that doesn’t mean that more hearty foods need to be entirely eliminated. The grains, legumes, and oils that are recommended for kapha should ideally be eaten in smaller amounts compared to vegetables, and special attention should be paid to the energetics of these foods.

Grains that are warming and drying are best for kapha, such as millet and buckwheat. Barley, corn, and rice are recommended secondarily. Wheat is too heavy and oily, so should be avoided. Oats and oatmeal are in the same camp as wheat. In general, grains that are roasted and toasted are best, and this applies to bread as well, which should only be eaten toasted, if at all (Svoboda, 1999). For kapha dosha, make porridge from buckwheat, rice, or cornmeal rather than oatmeal, as these grains are lighter and drier.  

In terms of legumes, black beans, mung beans, pinto beans, and well-cooked tofu are best for kapha. It is best not to overburden kapha-type digestion with lots of legumes, as they are generally difficult to digest (Svoboda, 1999). In general, kapha types do not require large amounts of protein. Small amounts of legumes that are well cooked and well spiced are suitable. Adding the spice asafoetida or hing, which is actually a resin, to beans and legumes during the cooking process makes them easier to digest.

Nuts, oils, and dairy should also be used lightly by anyone with kapha-type digestion. These are all heavy, oily, and sweet in nature, and thus kapha-aggravating. Seeds are better than nuts, and roasted pumpkin and sunflower seeds can be eaten on occasion (not by the handful, but in small amounts as a garnish). The best oils are safflower or very small amounts of ghee. In terms of dairy, goat’s milk is better than cow’s milk, as it has a lighter, warmer quality to it (Svoboda, 1999). Dairy should not be eaten cold or without spice. A bit of heated goat milk with warming spices, such as turmeric, ginger, and black pepper is acceptable for kapha.

Herbs for Clearing Kapha

While the basis of any Ayurvedic protocol is diet and lifestyle, herbal supplements can go a long way in helping the individual regain health and balance. Here are some special herbs and spices that are particularly effective for clearing excess kapha from the digestive tract.

A simple and classical formula that is ideal for kapha digestive disturbances is trikatu. This is a formula made from just three spices: dried ginger, black pepper, and pippali (long pepper). Trikatu is indicated for strengthening agni, burning ama, and generally boosting metabolism and digestion (Dass, 2013). The suggested dosage is 1-3 g, 2-3 times per day (Dass, 2013). This pungent blend is is generally best taken as a powder, or churna, in a bit of hot water.

The classic formula, hingwashtak churna, is also quite nice for stimulating kapha digestion. This formula is a little heavier and oilier than trikatu, so it is also useful for many vata-type digestive issues.  Hingwashtak churna contains cumin (Cuminum cyminum), black cumin (Nigella sativa), rock salt, ginger (Zingiber officinale), ajwan (Trachyapermum copticum), black pepper (Piper nigrum), pippali (Piper longum), and asafoetida (Ferula asafoetida). The suggested dose is 1-3 g twice daily (Dass, 2013). .

Another great herb for stimulating agni is chitrak (Plumbago zeylanica).  This pungent root is known for its ability to boost metabolism; it possesses what Ayurveda refers to as a “scraping” quality and may be employed for its ability to reduce fat tissue (Dass, 2013). Fat-reducing herbs are important to consider when addressing a kapha imbalance within the digestive system, as excess fat tissue may be linked to sluggish digestion. 

Of course, the root issues that are causing the excess weight need to be addressed, but adding some pungent, fat-burning herbs can help stimulate and motivate the individual as they make lifestyle and dietary shifts. I often find that once a client starts to shed some excess weight and feel a little lighter, this increases the motivation to continue to make lifestyle shifts.

Diet for Dual Doshas: Putting Principles Into Practice

So what happens if you are a vata-kapha type or a pitta-vata type? Or perhaps you are strongly one dosha but have a dual dosha imbalance? Things can start to become confusing. However, if you return to the foundational concepts of the six tastes, it will all become clear! Just remember that the tastes are made of elements, and that the elements are the building blocks for the three doshas. Also, the six tastes, the five elements, and the three doshas all have inherent qualities. Understanding which qualities you need to increase and decrease will help guide the way. Let’s take a look at how this plays out.

Vata-Kapha: Reduce cold, dry, heavy qualities. Emphasize the pungent and sour tastes.

Vata-Pitta: Reduce hot, light, dry qualities. Emphasize the sweet taste.

Pitta-Kapha: Reduce hot, heavy, moist qualities. Emphasize the bitter and astringent tastes

(California College of Ayurveda, 2012). 

Now let’s take a more detailed look at a vata-kapha food plan, a pitta-kapha food plan, and a vata-kapha food plan.

Vata-Kapha Food Plan: Warm and Light

In some ways vata and kapha are polar opposites. Vata dosha is light and dry; kapha dosha is heavy and moist. Vata types tend to be light framed and quick moving, whereas kapha types typically have a solid build and move slowly, gracefully, and methodically. However, one attribute that vata and kapha dosha share in common is coolness. Both these doshas lack the fiery heat of pitta dosha.

When it comes to food for this dual doshic type, it is helpful to remember that vata is cool, dry, and light and that kapha is cool, moist, and heavy. A diet that is generally warm, moist, and on the lighter side will help balance the converging qualities of vata and kapha (California College of Ayurveda, 2012). This means favoring foods with pungent and sour tastes. Salty and astringent foods can be eaten in moderation, while the sweet and bitter tastes should be minimized (California College of Ayurveda, 2012). It is worth clarifying that everyone needs some balance of all six tastes in the diet. However, if you are working on decreasing a doshic imbalance, such as a vata-kapha excess, it is helpful to emphasize certain tastes.

Tiwari (1995) offers some examples of foods that pacify, or are balancing for, vata-kapha types; these are foods that can be eaten in abundance by vata-kapha types or those with a vata-kapha imbalance:

Grains: Barley, buckwheat, millet, quinoa

Legumes: Aduki beans, black beans, chickpeas, red lentils, navy beans 

Vegetables: Beets, bok choy, broccoli and broccoli rabe, cabbage, carrots, celery, arugula, jicama, kale, kohlrabi, collards, eggplant, rutabaga

Fruits: Apples, apricots, cherries, cranberries

This is by no means an exhaustive list, but rather a sketch of foods that are vata-kapha pacifying. Also, a few foods that are best avoided by vata-kapha types are: dates, oats, wheat, kidney beans, and watermelon. These foods tend to be too cool, heavy, or sweet (Tiwari, 1995).

Vata-Pitta Food Plan: Nourishing and Heavy 

If you prefer foods that are heavy, moist, and somewhat sweet, then you are in luck if you’re a vata-pitta type! When vata and pitta combine, light, mobile, and hot qualities predominate; the heat of pitta will tend to overpower the coolness of vata. So, you will likely end up with a constitution that is light, dry, mobile, and warm. In fact, lightness and intensity are two common features of vata and pitta that will show up strongly in a vata-pitta constitution (Svoboda, 2010). 

In terms of diet, the sweet taste, with its cool, heavy qualities helps to balance the heat and mobility of this dual dosha blend. Foods with an astringent or sour taste are second in line, while the bitter and pungent tastes are the most aggravating to both vata and pitta (California College of Ayurveda, 2012). Tiwari (1995) offers some examples of foods that benefit vata-pitta types:

Grains: Barley, oats, white or brown basmati rice

Legumes: Aduki beans, yellow split mung dal, urad dal

Veggies: Bell pepper, collards, carrots, asparagus, broccoli and broccoli rabe, celery, lettuce, mustard greens, okra 

Fruits: Dates, coconut, figs, grapes, lemons, mango, pears, pineapple

Also, spices should be aromatic but not too heating. Examples of spices to favor are cardamom (Elettaria cardamomum), coriander (Coriandrum sativum), dill (Anethum graveolens), orange (Citrus x sinensis) peel, saffron (Crocus sativus), turmeric (Curcuma longa), and peppermint (Mentha x piperita) (Tiwari, 1995).

Foods to minimize include mushrooms, eggplant, turnip greens, raw onion, cranberries, chili pepper, and raw garlic (Tiwari, 1995). These foods are generally too light or heating for vata-pitta types. 

Pitta-Kapha Food Plan: Cool and Light 

When you combine pitta and kapha doshas, you are essentially merging earth, water, and fire. The result is a predominance of hot, moist, and heavy qualities. This is because pitta dosha is hot, oily, intense, and fluid or mobile. Kapha dosha is also oily, but cool, heavy, dense, and stable (Tiwari, 1995). 

In terms of the best foods for pitta-kapha types, it is ideal to emphasize the bitter and astringent tastes. The pungent and sweet tastes are fine in moderation, while it is best to minimize the sour and salty tastes (California College of Ayurveda, 2012). Since pitta and kapha combined are hot, moist (or oily), and heavy, bitter and astringent foods are best as they tend to be cool, drying, and light. The sour and salty tastes should be minimized, as foods that exhibit those tastes tend to be hot, heavy, and moist—all qualities that one wants to decrease with a pitta-kapha dual constitution. 

Tiwari (1995) offers some examples of foods that are balancing for pitta-kapha types or a pitta-kapha imbalance:

Grains: Barley, wheat, white or brown basmati rice 

Legumes: Lima beans, urad dal, chickpeas, aduki beans, black-eyed peas 

Veggies: Endive, green beans, cabbage, asparagus, collards, dandelion greens, turnip greens, parsnips, plantain

Fruits: Apples, apricots, cherries, mango, quince, watermelon 

Some foods that should be avoided by pitta-kapha types are raw garlic, fenugreek, raw onion, pickles, soy sauce, miso, and horseradish (Tiwari, 1995). These foods are generally considered too sour, salty, and heating for pitta-kapha. 

Conclusion

At first, understanding the principles of ayurvedic food selection can seem complex. When you consider dual dosha food programs, the plan can become even more nuanced. However, understanding the elements and qualities that characterize the six tastes and the three doshas will serve as a road map to guide the way. And if needed, seek out a good ayurvedic recipe book or an ayurvedic practitioner to provide additional support! Just remember that the wisdom of Ayurveda exists within you; it simply needs to be uncovered.
