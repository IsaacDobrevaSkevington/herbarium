Session 1: Understanding Menopause

60 minutes

Introduction

Menopause is a profound and natural passage in a woman’s life that marks the end of the reproductive years. But far from being an endpoint, it’s also a vibrant transition–a time for reflection and a renewed focus on wellbeing. Approaching menopause with wisdom and understanding will make this transition easier and more comfortable. While herbs can be very helpful, a healthy diet and lifestyle are still the primary way of managing symptoms and maintaining wellness during this time of change. Taking control of one’s health during the menopausal transition requires knowledge about the various anatomical and physiological factors at play, which we’ll be exploring in this first session of the Menopause Intensive. We’ll also cover what to expect, what is “normal,” and if or when to consider hormonal replacement therapy (HRT). 

Navigating the Stages of Menopause

To understand this transformative period, let’s explore the distinct stages of spontaneous (natural) menopause: this is the unhurried, natural unfolding of menopause, typically arriving around age 51-52. 

Premenopause is the span from menarche (beginning of menses) until the subtle shifts heralding perimenopause. Menstrual cycles are generally regular, and the body is in its reproductive prime. Health concerns at this time might be premenstrual syndrome (PMS), dysmenorrhea, or difficulty conceiving. An imbalance in progesterone and estrogen (typically too much estrogen and a lack of progesterone) can lead to fibroids, endometriosis, and fibrocystic breast disease. Imbalances in the reproductive hormones, along with insulin resistance, can result in polycystic ovarian syndrome (PCOS). 

Perimenopause (the menopause transition) is a phase that often commences with irregular menstrual cycles or other symptoms. It extends through the very last period, lasting up to a year beyond the final menses (Menopause Society, 2025). During this stage, ovarian function is gently slowing down its production of vital hormones like estrogen and progesterone (Peacock et al., 2025). Perimenopause lasts 2-8 years, although 4 years is the average (Office on Women’s Health [OASH], 2025)—so typically occurs during one’s forties. This can be thought of as the body adjusting to new rhythms, which might bring a diverse array of experiences: shifting periods (longer, shorter, missed, or heavier), hot flashes and night sweats due to changes in hormone levels that impact the hypothalamus, disrupted sleep, emotional ebbs and flows, or newfound dryness of vagina, hair, and skin. 

Menopause is the definitive moment marked by a 12-month period-free benchmark (Menopause Society, 2025). The ovaries have completed their work, resulting in significantly lower levels of estrogen and progesterone. It’s a permanent shift, leading into the next phase of life. 

Postmenopause is the long, expansive season of life that follows the final menstrual period (Menopause Society, 2025). With consistently lower hormone levels, particularly of estrogen, the body finds a new equilibrium. It’s also a time to be especially mindful of certain health considerations, like maintaining bone density to prevent osteoporosis and supporting heart health, as cardiac risks become similar to those of males due to a new lack of protective estrogen (OASH, 2025). 

These stages illustrate the typical menopausal route for most women. Sometimes, however, life’s path takes a different turn, leading to menopause through medical intervention. Induced and surgical menopause are examples of this type of menopause.

Induced menopause occurs when menopause arrives earlier than expected, often when both ovaries are surgically removed or permanently affected by treatments like chemotherapy or radiation for cancer (Menopause Society, 2025). 

Surgical menopause is a specific form of induced menopause, resulting from the surgical removal of both ovaries (bilateral oophorectomy) for medical reasons (Menopause Society, 2025). This can happen at any age before natural menopause would spontaneously occur. 

Hormones in Flux

Menopause is an intricate hormonal symphony conducted by the endocrine system. The ovaries gradually reduce their output of estrogen and progesterone, with wide-ranging effects on the body. Let’s look at each of these hormones and their respective roles in maintaining health.

Three steroid hormones are collectively known as estrogens. These are estrone (E1), beta estradiol (E2), and estriol (E3). Of them, E2 is the most active estrogen. Estrogen plays a pivotal role in conception, pregnancy, and childbirth. In non-pregnant persons, estrogens are produced in the ovaries and adrenal cortex, and during pregnancy they are produced in the placenta. Estrogen is responsible for female reproductive organ maturation and secondary sexual characteristics (breast development and fat deposition in the buttocks and thighs) and is a multifaceted hormone that impacts every organ system. Its decline influences not just the reproductive system, but also the skin and the musculoskeletal, cardiovascular, and nervous systems. A lack of estrogen also causes thinning of the delicate tissues of the urogenital system. Low levels of estrogen during menopause can notably cause vaginal dryness and skin changes, such as increased thinness and wrinkling. 

Progesterone is produced in the ovaries and adrenal cortex outside of pregnancy, and is additionally produced by the placenta during pregnancy. Once essential for cycle regulation and preparation of the endometrium during early pregnancy, its levels recede as ovulation becomes less frequent. Low progesterone can cause increased mood swings, sleeping difficulties, irregular cycles, fatigue, headaches, and many of the same symptoms as low estrogen.

The pituitary gland responds to lower levels of the sex hormones estrogen and progesterone by increasing its production of follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) and luteinizing hormone (LH), attempting to increase ovarian activity during this time. High FSH levels on a blood test often serve as confirmation of menopause’s arrival. 

Common Symptoms During Menopause

The widespread influence of the reproductive hormones means their graceful exit impacts far more than just the reproductive system. Changes in both estrogen and progesterone levels can be associated with symptoms that affect and transform the whole body. The symptoms experienced during perimenopause and menopause are unique to each person. These can arrive suddenly and intensely like a tsunami, or gently like a light rainstorm. They might be constant or come and go. The menopausal journey is marked by several common changes that most, but not all, will experience. These commonly experienced changes can include the following symptoms: 

Menstrual period changes: Cycles might become irregular—shorter, longer, or even intermittent with months between them, and periods may be lighter or heavier than usual. 

Hot flashes: Many experience sudden waves of warmth, often intense, usually flushing the face, neck, and chest, sometimes accompanied by sweating. 

Night sweats: These are disruptive hot flashes that occur during sleep. 

Sleep challenges: It is not uncommon to experience difficulty falling asleep or staying asleep. 

Emotional shifts: This may manifest as mood swings, heightened irritability, anxiety, or feelings of sadness. 

Vaginal dryness: This is caused by a thinning and drying of vaginal tissues, which can lead to discomfort during intimacy. 

Changes in libido: Due to hormonal shifts, changes in relationships and lessening sexual desire can occur. 

Urinary changes: This may include increased frequency or urgency, or a higher likelihood of urinary tract infections. 

Silent shifts in bone density: This is an important, often unseen change leading to the risk of osteoporosis (OASH, 2025). 

Weight shifts: Weight gain, especially around the abdomen, leads to a more “apple-shaped” rather than “pear-shaped” body in many. Along with this shift in weight, those in this phase of life also have an increased risk for type II diabetes and heart disease (Mayo Clinic Staff, 2023).

Hair and skin changes: Menopause can be accompanied by subtle thinning of hair or drier, less elastic skin. 

Cognitive changes: Many experience increased forgetfulness or difficulty concentrating. 

Joint pain and muscle aches: generalized stiffness or discomfort. 

Headaches: These may become more frequent or intense.

Pain: Shifts in estrogen and progesterone can be implicated in pain during menopause. Pain during menopause may include joint stiffness, osteoarthritis, migraines, and vulvovaginal pain (Strand et al., 2025).  

How Menopause Impacts Each Body System

As mentioned, the widespread influence of reproductive hormones means that menopause is a whole-body transformation, affecting many of the body’s systems.

Cardiovascular system: estrogen helps keep the heart strong through its protective effects on the cardiovascular system. As estrogen levels shift, there may be changes in blood cholesterol and other lipids, and an increased risk of high blood pressure, heart attack, and stroke, making heart-supportive lifestyle choices vitally important. 

Digestive system: While not a primary target, some women notice shifts in digestion, such as more bloating or changes in bowel habits, as the body adapts. For instance, postmenopausal women with irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) have more severe IBS symptoms than premenopausal women. This is thought to be due to the modulatory effect of female sex hormones on brain-gut interactions (Lenhart et al., 2020). 

Immune system: Research suggests a connection between hormonal shifts and subtle changes in immune function during this time. Menopause can bring with it a higher risk of autoimmune disease, due to lack of estrogen (Gameiro et al., 2010). 

Integumentary system (skin and hair): Estrogen contributes to plump, supple skin. Its decline can bring drier, thinner skin and subtle changes in hair texture or density. 

Nervous system: Fluctuating hormones can lead to mood swings, irritability, anxiety, feelings of sadness, or even a foggy mind. Sleep can become dysregulated. 

Musculoskeletal system: Estrogen has a protective effect on bones. With its decline, bone density can lessen, making osteopenia and osteoporosis a key consideration. 

Urogenital system: The delicate tissues of the vagina and urinary tract thrive on estrogen. Its decrease can lead to vaginal dryness, discomfort during intimacy, or changes in urinary patterns. 

The Timeline of Menopause

While the average age for menopause is 51 (Menopause Society, 2025), this age varies based on each individual’s unique genetics, lifestyle, and environmental influences. Understanding each of these factors can help with setting expectations, although each person’s menopausal journey will be unique to them. 

Premature menopause is the term used for menopause that occurs before the age of 40, and early menopause is defined as menopause that occurs in the age range of 40-44. 

Factors that might bring menopause earlier include: 

Family history: If someone’s mother experienced early menopause, genetics suggest they might, too (Steiner et al., 2008). 

Smoking: Actively smoking or even passive exposure to smoke can hasten menopause’s arrival by 1-2 years (Jiao et al., 2024). 

Medical interventions: Surgical removal of ovaries (as in a bilateral oophorectomy) induces immediate surgical menopause. Cancer treatments such as chemotherapy or radiation can also impact ovarian function. 

Chronic stress and socioeconomic factors: Prolonged stress, often linked to lower socioeconomic status, can sometimes influence the timing of menopause (Wang et al., 2021). 

Dietary choices: Some research suggests a link between frequent spicy food intake and earlier menopause (Wang et al., 2021).

Lower body mass index (BMI): Being underweight may be associated with an earlier menopause (Wang et al., 2020). 

Spontaneous abortions: Some studies suggest higher odds for early menopause in women who have experienced spontaneous abortions (Wang et al., 2021). 

Factors that might delay menopause include: 

Parity and breastfeeding: Those who have their first child later, have more live births, or breastfeed for longer periods often experience menopause later (Wang et al., 2021). 

Socioeconomic status: Higher socioeconomic status and education may correlate with later menopause, potentially due to better nutrition and reduced chronic stress (Schoenaker et al., 2014). 

Nourishing diets: Diets rich in fruits, vegetables, dairy, meat, tea, and even certain soybean products have been associated with a later onset of menopause (Wang et al., 2021) 

Active lifestyle: Regular physical activity has been linked to a later age at menopause (Jiao et al., 2024). 

Body weight: Healthy weight gain during adulthood has been inversely associated with premature and early menopause (Wang et al., 2021), while abdominal obesity is associated with late menopause (Jiao et al., 2024). 

Nutrient intake: Adequate intake of vitamins can also play a role in delaying menopause (Lay et al., 2020). 

Occasional alcohol intake: Some studies suggest this may be associated with a later age at menopause, though excessive alcohol consumption is detrimental to health (Wang et al., 2021). 

Oral contraceptives: Use of birth control pills has been associated with later menopause (Lay et al., 2020). 

Older age at menarche: A first period occurring at age 13 or later is associated with later menopause (Lay et al., 2020). 

Menopausal Support

Nutrition for Menopause

Food is a powerful tool during the menopausal transition and menopause. Thoughtful dietary choices can help manage symptoms, bolster bone health, support cardiovascular health, and promote general wellbeing during and after menopause. In general, the most well-studied and overall healthiest diet is the modified Mediterranean diet, a focus on foods that are high in extra virgin olive oil, abundant and colorful produce, whole grains, legumes, nuts, seeds, fish, and seafood. This applies during menopause as well. Consider the Mediterranean diet as foundational, and modify it in the following ways: 

  1. Eat unprocessed foods as much as possible, and organic when possible. 
  2. Eat more plants!
  3. Eat unprocessed carbohydrates that are slow-burning and high in fiber for steady blood sugar and energy. Avoid refined sugar, processed or refined flour products, pasta, etc. 
  4. Hydrate well. Avoid too much alcohol, coffee, and soft drinks. In addition to water, choose herbal teas and fermented drinks like kombucha, water kefir, and seltzer. Seltzer with a splash of 100% cranberry juice is refreshing and can help one avoid urinary infections. 

Aviva Romm, herbalist and physician, has written extensively on menopause. Her “Eating for Hormone Intelligence 101” plan is exceptionally helpful for hormonal balance. Here is Romm’s (2021) suggested plan: 

  • Every meal should include: one serving of protein (fish, poultry, eggs, legumes, or beans), one serving of healthy fats (avocado, olive oil, coconut oil, or ghee), and 1-2 servings of fruits and vegetables. 
  • Every day should include: 6-8 servings of fruits and vegetables, up to 2 servings of fruit daily, 1-2 slow-burning carbohydrates (whole grains, sweet potatoes, or winter squashes), nuts, seeds, and water. 
  • Include weekly portions of lacto-fermented foods (sauerkraut, kimchi, etc.).

Essential Nutrients for Menopause

During  menopause, there is an increased need for several nutrients. The following are considered the most important to ensure a healthy transition: B vitamins, calcium, magnesium, quality carbohydrates, protein, and vitamins C, D, and E. 

B vitamins: B vitamins are needed for energy, a calm nervous system, and hormone regulation. Focus on whole grains, leafy greens, lean proteins, and legumes. 

Calcium: The main mineral in bones is calcium phosphate, which forms a crystalline structure called hydroxyapatite, along with magnesium and trace minerals. Boosting intake through dietary sources is preferable to taking calcium supplements (Reid, 2014). Dairy, fortified plant milks, leafy greens, and some nuts are good sources. Nutritive herbs like sea vegetables, oat (Avena sativa) straw, and nettle (Urtica dioica) leaf can provide much needed trace minerals, along with calcium. 

Quality carbohydrates: Complex carbohydrates from legumes, nuts, whole grains, fruits, and vegetables are preferred over refined sugars. These provide steady energy and essential fiber for gut health and support stable blood sugar—all crucial during menopause (Eibol et al., 2025). 

Magnesium: Magnesium is a required cofactor in over 600 biochemical reactions in the body (Mazza et al., 2025). It is important for hormonal, neurological, cardiovascular, and reproductive health. Magnesium is the second most abundant mineral in bones. Lower serum magnesium concentrations are associated with a significantly higher risk of incident bone fractures (Dominguez et al., 2023). Leafy green vegetables (e.g., spinach, kale), legumes (e.g., beans, lentils), nuts and seeds (e.g., almonds, pumpkin seeds), whole grains (e.g., brown rice, quinoa), and certain fish (e.g., mackerel, salmon) are all excellent sources of magnesium. 

Protein: Protein is essential for maintaining muscle mass, which naturally declines with age. Protein also fortifies bones and helps with satiety. Lean meats, fish, eggs, dairy, legumes, nuts, and seeds are good sources of protein (Erdelyi et al., 2024). The intake of fish, milk, and soy products can help in alleviating the severity of menopausal symptoms. Studies have shown that fish may also play a role in delaying natural menopause (Yang et al., 2025). Soy products can be an important source of protein, especially for vegetarians, and can be an important food during the menopause transition. However, there is a controversy regarding phytoestrogens, and soy in particular, which is detailed below. 

Vitamin C: this nutrient supports collagen production for healthy skin and tissues. Vitamin C is a powerful antioxidant and keeps the immune system robust. Find it abundantly in citrus, berries, bell peppers, and broccoli. Herbal sources include amla (Phyllanthus emblica) berry, moringa (Moringa oleifera) leaf, rose (Rosa spp.) hip, acerola cherry (Malpighia emarginata), and many other fruits. 

Vitamin E: Studies on vitamin E supplements have shown that vitamin E is effective for alleviating many menopausal symptoms, such as hot flashes and vaginal dryness (Feduniw et al., 2023). Whether or not these beneficial effects are available from food sources of vitamin E is still up for debate. Nonetheless, vitamin E is an important antioxidant and nutrient for skin, cardiovascular, and immune health. Good food sources include almonds, sunflower seeds, hazelnuts, avocado, peanut butter, fatty fish, and green leafy vegetables. 

Vitamin D: The “sunshine vitamin” is crucial for absorbing calcium. As estrogen wanes, strong bones become paramount to prevent osteoporosis. Vitamin D can be found in fatty fish, fortified foods, and safe sun exposure. 

The Phytoestrogen Conversation: Soy, Equol, and the Microbiome

Phytoestrogens are remarkable plant compounds that gently mimic estrogen by binding to estrogen receptors in the body. Found in flax (Linum usitatissimum) seeds, sesame seeds, whole grains, and notably, soy products, they’ve been a topic of much discussion regarding menopausal support. Isoflavones are the primary phytoestrogens in soy and other legumes. Examples of well-studied isoflavones include genistein and daidzein. Some clinical studies suggest that soy isoflavones may help reduce the frequency and severity of hot flashes and may have beneficial effects on bone density and cardiovascular health in some individuals (Kenda et al., 2022). Herbs can also contain phytoestrogens like genistein and daidzein, like red clover (Trifolium pratense) aerial parts and kudzu (Pueraria montana) root, legumes that can provide support during menopause. 

Navigating the “Soy Debate”

Concerns sometimes arise about whether soy’s gentle estrogenic activity might stimulate certain cancers through estrogen mimicry. However, extensive research shows that moderate soy consumption does not increase breast cancer risk, and some research even suggests a protective effect. There is some evidence that soy can potentially negatively impact thyroid function, especially in those with existing thyroid conditions or diets that are low in iodine (Jargin, 2022). How soy is prepared (fermented vs. unfermented) can influence the availability of isoflavones and modulate any of its negative effects (Daniel, 2005). 

The Equol Connection 

A fascinating aspect of soy’s impact lies within the gut microbiome and its ability to produce a special metabolite called equol (Setchell & Clerici, 2010). This powerful compound is created by special beneficial gut bacteria when they break down daidzein (a soy isoflavone). Equol is thought to have stronger estrogenic effects and be more readily used by the body than its precursor (Lampe, 2009). However, not everyone’s gut hosts the specific bacteria needed to make equol. Only roughly 30-50% of the population are “equol producers,” while 80-90% harbor O-desmethylangolensin-producing bacteria, which produce a different daidzein metabolite (Lampe, 2009). Those who are producers of the equol bacteria might experience more significant benefits from soy, such as greater ease from hot flashes and better bone health (Lampe, 2009). Interestingly, the “equol producer” phenotype is more prevalent in Japanese adults and in Asian populations (e.g., 50-60%) who have historically consumed soy regularly compared to predominately Caucasian populations (e.g., 20-30%) (Song et al., 2006). This difference suggests a fascinating interplay of genetics, diet, and gut health in how different individuals respond to soy and highlights why generalizing soy’s effects across all populations needs careful consideration. 

Another legume that has shown benefit during the menopause transition is kudzu (Pueararia mirifica) root. Widely used in Thailand and in Chinese medicine, kudzu has been shown in clinical trials to be as effective as HRT (Chandeying & Sangthawan, 2007). Kudzu contains the isoflavones puerarin, daidzein, and genistein in addition to many other phytoestrogenic compounds (Wang et al., 2020). Whether or not kudzu’s effectiveness in an individual is dependent on being an “equol producer” has not yet been determined. 

Lifestyle Considerations

Beyond diet, lifestyle factors can profoundly shape the menopausal journey. 

The following lifestyle suggestions can be amazing allies:

These practices are shown to improve overall health during the menopausal transition (Aarshageetha et al., 2023). 

Embrace restorative sleep: Sleep often becomes elusive during menopause, especially with night sweats and anxiety. Prioritize creating a sacred sleep sanctuary: cool, dark, and quiet. Stick to a consistent sleep schedule, and ease into the evening by avoiding caffeine and heavy meals before bed. 

Incorporate relaxation: Gentle relaxation practices can also be profoundly helpful, as can relaxing herbal nervines and hypnotics like passionflower (Passiflora incarnata) aerial parts, hops (Humulus lupulus) strobile, and lemon balm (Melissa officinalis) aerial parts. Nervines should always be chosen according to the energetics of the individual. 

Release the smoke: For those who smoke, the menopausal transition offers a powerful impetus to quit. Smoking is linked to earlier menopause and can worsen symptoms like hot flashes (Smith & Schliager, 2015). It’s one of the most impactful choices to improve overall health and wellbeing. 

Joyful movement: Regular physical activity provides many important benefits during menopause. Find something enjoyable and stick to it! Think of movement, not exercise. Movement should be fun, not a chore. Try yoga, dancing, gardening, hiking, swimming, bicycle riding, or pilates. Movement can help ease hot flashes and uplift mood. All weight-bearing exercises (e.g., walking, dancing, jogging) and strength training are important for preserving bone density and preventing osteoporosis. Movement supports healthy weight management and cardiovascular health. Getting regular movement can also be a wonderful tonic for deeper sleep. 

In addition, the following can also be very beneficial practices at this time: 

Adrenal support: In addition to stress management, supporting the adrenals with adaptogenic herbs will help modulate the effects that stress may have during this transition. Choose adaptogens that are suited to the individual’s constitution. Maca (Lepidium meyenii) root is a well-studied herb that can ease hot flashes, vaginal dryness, depression, and night sweats, and increase libido (Meissner et al., 2006). 

Massage: Weekly or monthly massage can help with hot flashes, mood, and sleep. Make it part of your wellness routine, with or without aromatherapy essential oils. 

Chinese Medicine and Ayurveda Perspectives

Global herbal traditions offer profound insights into menopause, seeing it not as a medical condition, but a natural and celebrated progression through life. 

Chinese Medicine

In Chinese medicine, menopause is understood as a natural decline in Kidney yin and Kidney yang, ultimately due to a decline in jJing (essence). The Kidneys are seen as the root of vitality, governing growth, reproduction, and the very process of aging. 

Kidney yin provides the body’s cooling, moistening, and nourishing qualities, while Kidney Jing is a fundamental life force, akin to constitutional or genetic reserve. With age, Kidney yin naturally lessens, leading to an imbalance where yang (warm, active energy) can feel relatively dominant or rise upward. This rise of yang energy is considered “false heat,” since it is the lack of the watery yin energy that causes heat symptoms in the body. This pattern is called “kidney yin vacuity” and is characterized by the following symptoms: heat in the palms, chest, and soles of the feet; irritability and anger; ringing in the ears; a red tongue with little coating; and a thready, rapid pulse (Goh, 2003). Hot flashes and night sweats are attributed to this “empty heat” rising because deficient Kidney yin can no longer ground the yang. Vaginal dryness is a sign of the body’s increasing dryness from yin deficiency. Mood swings, anxiety, and insomnia can arise from a restless Shen (spirit) or Liver qi stagnation, both influenced by Kidney imbalance. 

Bone loss, as seen in both osteopenia and osteoporosis, is considered an imbalance due to a decline of Kidney Jing, as the Kidneys govern the health of the bones. Kidney yang deficiency can look like cold intolerance, a sore lower back or knees, loose stools, frequent urination, a pale tongue with a thin coat, and a deep, feeble pulse (Goh, 2003). 

Restoring balance during menopause in Chinese medicine focuses on nourishing Kidney yin, Kidney yang, or both. Herbs that tonify kidney yin include: processed rehmannia (Rehmannia glutinosa) root, goji (Lycium barbarum) berry, white peony (Paeonia lactiflora) root, and American ginseng (Panax quinquefolius) root. Herbs to nourish kidney yang include: eucommia (Eucommia ulmoides) bark,

morinda (Morinda officinalis) root, epimedium (Epimedium spp.) aerial parts, Chinese ginseng (Panax ginseng) root, and cordyceps (Cordyceps sinensis) mushroom. Lifestyle practices like stress reduction and restorative rest can also be helpful. 

Ayurveda

Ayurveda, the ancient Indian science of life, views menopause through the doshas—vata, pitta, and kapha—the fundamental energies that govern all bodily functions. Menopause is primarily seen as a shift into a vata-pitta phase of life, with vata dosha becoming more prominent with age. 

Vata dosha (air and ether) is characterized by qualities like dryness, lightness, coldness, and movement. As vata naturally increases with age, symptoms like vaginal dryness, joint aches, loss of bone density, anxiety, and insomnia can occur. 

Pitta dosha (fire and water) is characterized by heat, intensity, and transformation. While vata takes center stage in aging, an imbalance in pitta can amplify hot flashes, irritability, and anger due to excessive heat. 

Ayurvedic guidance during menopause centers on balancing these shifting doshas. This includes eating nourishing, grounding, and warming foods to soothe vata, while avoiding overly spicy foods that might aggravate pitta. Commonly used herbal allies include ashwagandha (Withania somnifera) root, shatavari (Asparagus racemosus) root, and bacopa (Bacopa monnieri) aerial parts. Lifestyle practices such as self-oil massage (abhyanga), gentle yoga, and meditation are also helpful Ayurvedic techniques for easing menopausal symptoms. 

Exploring Hormonal Support: HRT and Bioidentical Hormones

For some individuals, particularly those experiencing severe menopausal symptoms, conventional medical options like hormone replacement therapy (HRT), also known as menopausal hormone therapy (MHT), can offer significant relief. Hormone replacement therapy involves replacing the hormones (estrogen, often with progesterone) that decline during menopause. It’s considered the most clinically effective treatment for hot flashes and vaginal dryness, and it’s also highly effective in preventing bone loss and reducing the risk of osteoporosis-related fractures (The North American Menopause Society, 2022).

There are a number of different types of HRT available. The decision to use HRT and the type that is used should be decided in consultation with a qualified healthcare professional, like a gynecologist with a practice that focuses on menopausal health. The most common types of HRT are estrogen therapy (ET), in which estrogen alone is prescribed—typically for those who have had a hysterectomy—and estrogen-progesterone/progestin therapy (EPT), in which estrogen is combined with progesterone (or a synthetic progestin) and prescribed to those with a uterus to protect the uterine lining from thickening, which can increase the risk of uterine cancer. Hormone replacement therapy comes in various forms—pills, patches, gels, sprays, and vaginal rings or creams—allowing for a personalized approach. 

Weighing benefits and risks: The decision to use HRT is a deeply personal one. As herbalists, we typically want to try herbs first, but sometimes symptoms, health history, and individual risk factors can lead to the decision to do HRT. While HRT is effective, discussions about its safety gained prominence after the Women’s Health Initiative (WHI) study, which highlighted increased risks of breast cancer, heart disease, stroke, and blood clots in certain populations of women who received combined HRT (Writing Group for the Women’s Health Initiative Investigators, 2002). However, newer research and a deeper understanding suggest that for healthy women who start HRT close to the onset of menopause (generally within 10 years of their last period or before age 60), the benefits of symptom relief and bone protection often outweigh the risks. Risks tend to increase with age and duration of use (The North American Menopause Society, 2022). This theory is known as the “timing hypothesis” or “window of opportunity” and is the advice of many mainstream healthcare professionals (Harman et al., 2014). 

It’s important to note that the U.S. Preventive Services Task Force (USPSTF) (2022) advises against using systemic menopausal hormone therapy solely for the primary prevention of chronic conditions in postmenopausal women who are asymptomatic or have very mild symptoms. This recommendation focuses on preventive use, not on the use of HRT to treat moderate to severe, disruptive menopausal symptoms, where it remains a powerful and effective option under careful medical guidance (Crandall et al., 2023). The implications of menopausal hormone therapy beyond age 65 years vary by type, route of administration, and dosage strength. In general, the risk of HRT related adverse effects is reduced with low rather than medium or high doses, vaginal or transdermal rather than oral preparations, and with E2 rather than conjugated estrogen (Baik et al., 2025). 

Understanding Bioidentical Hormones

Bioidentical hormones are chemically identical to the hormones naturally produced by the body (e.g., estradiol, estrone, estriol, and progesterone). They can be formulated by pharmaceutical companies into FDA-approved medications or custom-compounded by pharmacies. 

Compounded bioidentical hormones are custom preparations, often marketed as more “natural” or “safer” alternatives. However, it’s crucial to understand that these compounded forms are not regulated by the FDA in the same rigorous way as approved medications, meaning their purity, potency, and consistent effectiveness aren’t always guaranteed. There are also FDA-approved bioidentical hormone products available from pharmaceutical companies, which offer the same natural chemical structure with the assurance of regulatory oversight and quality control. The decision to use bioidentical hormones ultimately lies with each individual and their physician or other qualified healthcare practitioner. 

Conclusion

Menopause is the ideal time to incorporate an integrated approach to wellness. For some, this might mean finding various supportive tools and creating a comprehensive health plan with the aid of an herbalist, nutritionist, naturopathic doctor, or other integrative practitioner. An individual using HRT for severe hot flashes might also turn to herbs to gently address other symptoms like anxiety or sleep disturbances, or to simply support overall vitality. Hormone replacement therapy can be combined with herbs, diet, and other modalities to achieve the health benefits desired. For those wishing to gradually reduce or discontinue HRT, herbal support, under proper medical and herbal guidance, can help ease the transition and manage lingering symptoms. For those with milder symptoms or those who prefer to explore non-pharmaceutical routes, herbal preparations such as those discussed in the following sessions, alongside dietary and lifestyle shifts, can be a primary choice and an important part of an overall comprehensive care plan. 

Most importantly, HRT should not be self-prescribed or discontinued without consulting with your physician. Open communication with a qualified healthcare professional is paramount to ensuring that menopause support choices are safe, effective, and truly aligned with an individual’s unique health needs. The journey through menopause is deeply personal, and the right combination of support requires an integrative, team approach to finding solutions.
