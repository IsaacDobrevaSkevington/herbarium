Session 1: Introduction to Summer Herbs

60 minutes

Introduction

Summer brings an undeniable sense of bounty—long days, warm sunshine, and gardens overflowing with plants and pollinators. Herbs thrive in this season, soaking up the sun and producing lush foliage, vibrant blooms, and richly aromatic oils. 

For herbalists and plant lovers, this is the perfect season to connect with herbs through the bevy of fresh plant material that is so readily available. Whether you’re harvesting fresh herbs for tea, infusing oils for body care, or adding garden greens to your meals, summer offers the opportunity to work with plants at their peak.

It’s also a season that invites us to sync with nature’s rhythms. The expansive, outward-moving energy of summer encourages activity, creativity, and celebration. By noticing what grows around us—and how our own energy shifts with the season—we can find simple, meaningful ways to support our wellness, gather joy, and stay grounded in the fullness of life.

In this Maker’s Summer Herbs DIY Intensive, we’ll explore how to work with the herbs of the season, from creating nourishing foods and body care to simple supports for vibrant living. Together, we’ll learn how to make the most of this abundant time, tuning in to both the gifts of the plants and the wisdom of the season itself.

As mentioned above, summer is not just a season of physical abundance—it’s a time when creative energy flows readily. This energy calls us to experiment, express, and engage in fresh ways. When we slow down to gather, create, and savor the gifts of summer, we’re not just making things—we’re tuning in to the vibrant life around us and strengthening the bond between ourselves and the earth.

Understanding the Body’s Needs in Summer

Summer brings a dynamic mix of heat, humidity, dryness, and increased sun exposure—all of which naturally influence what the body craves to feel balanced and vibrant. Longer days and higher temperatures often call for lighter foods, more hydration, and activities that keep us cool and grounded.

Many traditional systems of herbalism recognize how closely the rhythms of nature mirror the needs of the human body. They offer valuable insights into seasonal energetics—how the environment around us affects our internal balance. For example, in Ayurveda, summer is associated with pitta dosha, which is governed by the elements of fire and water. Pitta is sharp, hot, and intense—just like the season itself. When pitta becomes excessive, it can show up as irritability, overheating, inflammation, or restlessness. To stay balanced during summer, Ayurveda encourages cooling, calming, and moistening herbs and practices that help ease intensity and support a sense of harmony.

Incorporating herbs and foods that balance the season’s fiery nature can be deeply supportive. Plants with cooling, soothing, and hydrating qualities, such as hibiscus (Hibiscus sabdariffa), mint (Mentha spp.), and rose (Rosa spp.), can help ease the effects of summer heat and encourage a steady sense of vitality.

Understanding these seasonal shifts gives us a chance to work with nature, not against it, nourishing the body in a way that feels intuitive, restorative, and aligned with the rhythm of life around us.

The intensity of summer heat doesn’t just affect the body—it can also stir up restlessness, irritability, and emotional unease. When temperatures rise, it’s common to feel more agitated or uncomfortable. This is where nervines—herbs that support the nervous system—can be especially helpful. Gentle, cooling nervines like lemon balm (Melissa officinalis), chamomile (Matricaria chamomilla), and oat (Avena sativa) offer a sense of ease, helping to settle frazzled nerves, lift the mood, and support a more balanced response to seasonal stress. Sipping these herbs in teas, using them in tinctures, or incorporating them into simple daily rituals can help create a steady foundation of emotional support during the heat of summer.

Common Characteristics of Supportive Summer Herbs

Summer herbs tend to mirror the season’s needs, offering cooling, moistening, and skin-supporting qualities that help us stay comfortable and balanced as the temperature rises.

Cooling herbs like hibiscus (Hibiscus sabdariffa), mint (Mentha spp.), and lemon balm (Melissa officinalis) are natural allies during the warmer months. They help regulate body temperature, refresh the senses, and ease the heat that can build up both internally and externally. Enjoyed as teas, infusions, or in culinary dishes, these plants offer a simple, delicious way to stay cool and energized.

Moistening herbs such as rose (Rosa spp.), aloe (Aloe vera), and oat (Avena sativa) gently ease the dryness and irritation that often come with hot, sunny days. Whether sipped in a tea, applied in a skin preparation, or incorporated into food, these herbs help to hydrate, soothe, and restore the body’s natural moisture balance. 

Skin-supporting herbs like plantain (Plantago spp.), jewelweed (Impatiens capensis, I. pallida), and calendula (Calendula officinalis) shine during the summer months, when bites, rashes, sun exposure, and minor skin irritations are more common. These plants can be used in washes, salves, or simple poultices to calm and nurture the skin, offering a sense of comfort and restoration.

By incorporating these summer-friendly plants into daily routines, we can create a gentle rhythm of support that helps the body stay in harmony with the vibrant, sometimes intense energy of the season.

Summer Herbs to Know

Plants have an incredible way of meeting us where we are, especially in summer, when the intensity of the season calls for a little extra care. Many herbs naturally offer cooling, moistening, and soothing qualities that help balance the effects of heat, dryness, and long days spent outdoors.

In this section, we’ll take a closer look at some of the plants that shine brightest during the summer months. These herbs offer simple, accessible ways to support the body, lift the spirit, and stay connected to the rhythms of the season. Whether through teas, topical preparations, or nourishing rituals, these summer allies help weave a sense of ease and resilience into daily life.

Aloe (Aloe vera) is a cooling, succulent plant well known for its gel-filled leaves, which provide support for a variety of summer skin concerns. With a long tradition of use for easing sunburns, minor burns, rashes, and dry or irritated skin, aloe’s moistening nature has earned it a spot in many herbal first aid kits. Its energetics are cooling and moistening, making it especially helpful when the skin feels hot or dry after time in the sun. 

Aloe vera contains polysaccharides, enzymes, vitamins, and minerals that support skin integrity. Consider keeping a potted aloe vera plant in a sunny window for immediate summer first aid. To use, select a thick, mature outer leaf from the base of the plant that is well-established with several layers of plump leaves, and cut it with a clean knife. Then, split the leaf and scoop out the gel for immediate use or refrigerate it for later. Apply the cooling gel directly to sunburns, heat rash, or minor cuts and scrapes. Fresh aloe gel keeps in the fridge for about a week. 

Safety note: Aloe vera should not be used in cases of staph infection as it may seal in the infection and cause staph to grow (Gladstar, 2008). 

Bee balm (Monarda spp.) is a vibrant and aromatic member of the mint family, traditionally used to support the respiratory and digestive systems, while also providing gentle nervous system support. With both warming and cooling properties, it can help move heat out during fevers or overheated states, or bring warmth to a cold, sluggish digestive system. Bee balm helps to ease congestion, soothe sore throats, and calm irritated skin, making it especially useful as a tea, steam, or infused honey. Bee balm can help “draw out fire” from hot, inflamed skin conditions like sunburn, acne, and bug bites. A simple poultice made from the fresh flowers—even just chewed and applied—has traditionally been used to ease pain and inflammation, living up to its name as a balm for bee stings and sun-soaked skin.

The leaves and flowers are rich in volatile oils like thymol and carvacrol, giving bee balm strong antimicrobial and anti-inflammatory properties. It’s also a flavorful addition to herbal vinegars, culinary blends, and garden teas, although it’s a strong flavor, so a light hand is best. Harvest the aerial parts when the plant is in full bloom, avoiding any mildew-damaged leaves, and dry them in small bundles or spread them on screens out of direct sunlight.

Butterfly pea (Clitorea ternatea) is a cooling, slightly sweet-tasting flower best known for its brilliant blue hue and mood-supportive qualities. Traditionally used across Asia in both herbal and culinary applications, this plant is often seen as a symbol of beauty, transformation, and new beginnings. In Ayurveda, butterfly pea is often used to support calm energy, uplift mood, and promote clarity (Mukherjee et al., 2008), making it a lovely herb for this hot, busy season.

While the vivid blue flowers are most commonly used, the leaves, roots, seeds, and even the whole plant are valued for their diverse properties. The flower’s vibrant color comes from its rich content of ternatins—antioxidant anthocyanins that also change color when mixed with acidic ingredients like lemon juice (Saito et al., 1985). In the summer months, butterfly pea flower is a delightful addition to herbal teas, infused syrups, and cooling beverages. The flower is a popular natural colorant for food and drinks, adding not just beauty but also antioxidant and nootropic benefits. 

Butterfly pea blooms from early summer through fall. For optimal quality, harvest the flowers shortly after they bloom—ideally when the petals are fully open but before they begin to fade or curl. Dry them in a single layer away from direct sunlight to preserve their vivid color and potency. These blossoms can be enjoyed as a tea on their own, blended with other summer herbs, or frozen into colorful ice cubes for an eye-catching way to cool down on warm days.

Calendula (Calendula officinalis), also known as pot marigold, is a cheerful, sunny flower traditionally used to support skin, lymphatic, and digestive wellness. With its vulnerary, anti-inflammatory, and antimicrobial actions, calendula is especially valued for supporting the skin’s natural recovery process during the summer months when sunburn, scrapes, and insect bites are more common. The flower is a go-to for balms, salves, and infusions applied to dry, irritated, or damaged skin. Internally, calendula has also been used to ease digestive discomfort and support lymphatic movement and immune health (Popham, n.d.).

Calendula blooms from late spring through fall, making it a steady summer ally. Harvest flower heads when they are fully open and dry, ideally in the afternoon once the dew has evaporated. Picking every 2–3 days encourages continuous blooming throughout the season. Dry flower heads in a warm, well-ventilated space. For the strongest herbal preparations, use the whole flower head rather than just the petals (Barker, 2001). Calendula can be infused into oils, brewed as a wash or tea, or added to summer recipes as a bright, edible garnish. Its vibrant orange and yellow blossoms offer both visual appeal and versatile wellness support all season long.

Safety note: Calendula may cause hypersensitivity in those with an allergy to the Asteraceae (daisy) plant family.

Hibiscus (Hibiscus sabdariffa), known for its tart, ruby-red calyces, is a cooling herb beloved for its refreshing flavor and vibrant color. With a naturally sour and slightly astringent taste, hibiscus is often used in teas, syrups, infused honeys, and beverages. Its antioxidant-rich calyces contain vitamin C, anthocyanins, and polyphenols that help nourish the cardiovascular system and support immune function (Mahadevan et al., 2009). As a “refrigerant” herb, hibiscus is especially helpful during the summer months for easing internal heat, supporting fluid balance, and soothing overheated or irritated tissues in the digestive and urinary tracts (American Botanical Council, 2007).

Hibiscus calyces are harvested after the flowers bloom and the petals fall away, when the outer sepals become thick, fleshy, and richly colored. Harvest on a dry day and use fresh or dry them for long-term storage. Deeper red varieties tend to have higher antioxidant content (Hopkins et al., 2013). In warm weather, hibiscus shines in sun teas, herbal lemonades, and cooling blends with mint (Mentha spp.), lemon balm (Melissa officinalis), or rose (Rosa spp.). Whether steeped into a crimson tea or blended into a tangy syrup, hibiscus offers beauty and refreshment throughout the summer.

Jewelweed (Impatiens capensis, I. pallida) is a juicy, succulent plant commonly found along streams and in damp, shady areas. Known for its ability to soothe the skin, jewelweed has a long tradition of topical use after exposure to poison ivy, stinging nettle, insect bites, and other summer skin irritations. Offering moistening, cooling properties, jewelweed is used externally rather than internally. Jewelweed is often applied as a fresh plant poultice, infused into oil or vinegar for topical application, or even frozen into ice cubes for quick cooling support. These summer preparations can be especially useful when the skin feels hot, itchy, or inflamed.

Jewelweed can be harvested at any time throughout the growing season. Because it wilts quickly after picking, jewelweed should be used fresh or preserved soon after harvest. Clip stems gently near the base, and always leave enough of the patch intact to allow for reseeding and continued growth. Summer uses include poultices, infusions, salves, soaps, and “itch cubes” made from frozen jewelweed juice, to be applied topically.

Lemon balm (Melissa officinalis) is a gentle, lemon-scented herb celebrated for its calming, mood-brightening, and digestive-supportive properties. With cooling and slightly drying energetics, it’s a nice counterpoint to the heat of summer and is often used to ease tension, promote sleep, and soothe an overactive mind or unsettled stomach, especially when heat or stress is a factor. 

In summer, lemon balm flourishes in gardens, producing thick beds of fragrant leaves that release a bright, citrusy aroma when brushed. Its vibrant, bee-attracting presence adds beauty and pollinator support to summer landscapes. It’s lemony, sweet flavor makes it a favorite for children or those with sensitive palates, and its cooling nature is especially well-suited to warm-weather preparations. Lemon balm can be enjoyed as a chilled infusion to help ease overheated bodies and frazzled nerves, or steeped into calming popsicles and herbal lemonades for a gentle, mood-lifting refreshment. Refreshing on its own, lemon balm also pairs well with other cooling herbs, like spearmint (Mentha spicata) or chamomile (Matricaria chamomilla). For best results, harvest the aerial parts just before flowers open when the concentration of volatile oils is at its peak (Chevallier, 2000). Gently dry aerial parts to preserve their lemony aroma and potency.

Oat (Avena sativa) is a nourishing herb known for its deeply restorative effects on the nervous system. While oatstraw and milky oat tops are both valued, the fresh milky tops—harvested when the grain exudes a white sap when squeezed—are particularly noted for their ability to soothe frazzled nerves and ease emotional tension. During the height of summer, when heat, stress, or activity levels can feel depleting, oat infusions or tinctures offer gentle support. Oatstraw is rich in minerals like calcium and magnesium, making it ideal for long infusions that nourish the body and support resilience (Hoffmann, 2003). Milky oats, meanwhile, are often tinctured fresh and used when someone feels burned out, overstimulated, or emotionally drained (Groves, 2016).

Milky oat tops are best harvested during their brief “milky” window, which lasts about a week after flowering and before the grain fully matures. Squeeze the seeds gently—if a milky white sap appears, they’re ready. Oatstraw is typically harvested at the same stage, when the stems are still green and vibrant. Once gathered, dry the straw for nourishing infusions, or tincture fresh milky oats right away to preserve their calming properties. In summer, a cool oatstraw infusion can be enjoyed as a mineral-rich drink, or milky oat tincture can be added to a daily routine for grounding during intense or stressful times.

Peppermint (Mentha x piperita) is a pungent and invigorating herb known for its high menthol content and pronounced cooling sensation. It’s commonly used to support digestion, ease tension headaches, and encourage circulation. Peppermint has a fascinating dual nature: when sipped as a hot tea, it brings warmth and stimulates circulatory movement; when used externally or chilled, it offers strong cooling effects. In summer, it’s especially welcome in iced teas, foot soaks, body sprays, and infused vinegars. 

Peppermint’s bold flavor makes it a standout in blends and formulas that call for a stimulating herbal presence, while its uplifting aroma helps clear the mind and refresh the senses on hot, sluggish days. It pairs especially well with herbs like lemon balm (Melissa officinalis), chamomile (Matricaria chamomilla), or hibiscus (Hibiscus sabdariffa) in summer teas and infusions, bringing both balance and brightness to the blend. In the garden, peppermint thrives in moist, partly shaded areas and spreads readily by runners, making it both abundant and easy to harvest throughout the season. Its strong scent helps deter some pests, while its blossoms attract pollinators like bees and butterflies. Harvest the leaves just before flowering for peak flavor and potency, taking care to handle them gently to avoid bruising.

Spearmint (Mentha spicata) is a cooling and gently uplifting herb often used to soothe digestive upset, ease bloating, and calm the nerves. Milder than peppermint, it’s a favorite for children, pregnancy blends, and those who prefer a less intense mint flavor. Its antispasmodic and mild diaphoretic actions make it useful for supporting fevers, colds, and belly tension.

In warm weather, spearmint’s cooling energy and bright, sweet minty flavor are a welcome respite. Its refreshing taste blends beautifully in teas, infused waters, herbal baths, and culinary recipes. In summer, it’s especially popular for cooling drinks and gentle herbal soaks. A spearmint bath or compress can ease skin irritation, bug bites, or tired joints after long days outdoors. Its gentle nature makes it a wonderful option for children’s summer fevers, colicky tummies, or emotional overwhelm, while its crisp aroma enlivens both mind and body. Whether steeped fresh or dried, spearmint invites calm clarity and refreshment.

Spearmint thrives in the heat, spreading easily through the garden via underground runners and offering a generous harvest. Harvest the aerial parts when the plant is vibrant and fresh, ideally just before or just after flowering, and dry carefully to preserve color and aroma.

Plantain (Plantago spp.) is a familiar plant in lawns, trails, and backyards. Often dismissed as a weed, plantain is widely treasured in herbal traditions for its soothing, cooling properties. Both P. major and P. lanceolata are commonly used and valued for their ability to support skin, respiratory, digestive, and urinary health. Often considered “nature’s bandage,” plantain is known for easing irritation from bites and stings, calming minor wounds, and even helping to draw out splinters (Bruton-Seal, 2009). Its moistening qualities and gentle astringency make it a favorite for soothing everyday skin discomforts. The fresh leaves can be quickly turned into a simple spit poultice—perfect for bites, stings, or scrapes when you’re out and about—or mashed in a mortar and pestle with a bit of water. Because it’s so easy to find and work with, plantain is also a great herb to introduce to kids as they begin exploring herbalism.

To harvest, gather the young, vibrant leaves throughout the growing season, taking care to avoid areas that may have been treated with herbicides. For drying, lay the leaves in a single layer in a warm, shaded area, turning them occasionally, until they are completely dry. Beyond its herbal uses, plantain can be incorporated into summer meals. The young leaves can be added to salads or lightly cooked as a nutritious green. The seeds, commonly sold as psyllium husk, are a valued source of dietary fiber,can be ground and added to baked goods or sprinkled in beverages. You’ll also find it in cooling herbal syrups or as an infusion to sip when heat and irritation rise. 

Rose (Rosa spp.) has long symbolized love, but beyond its romantic reputation, the petals and hips of this well known plant have much to offer. Cooling, mildly astringent, and rich in aromatic compounds, rose petals are often used to for nervous system and emotional support—helping to soften the heart, ease feelings of grief or heartbreak, and gently encourage self-love and openness (Hardin, n.d.; Holmes, 2007). 

Rose has a long history of use as a vulnerary, promoting tissue repair and offering gentle, astringent support for skin that has been sun-kissed, scraped, or irritated. During summer, rose petals can be blended into refreshing teas, hydrosols, or infused oils for skin care and mood support. Rose hips, which develop later in the season, offer antioxidant and nutritive value, especially as a source of vitamin C and flavonoids that help strengthen the immune system and maintain skin resilience. Their bright, tart flavor makes them especially refreshing in cooling summer teas, and they’re also well-suited for making jellies, jams, and syrups.

Harvest rose petals on dry mornings shortly after they bloom, when their fragrance is most potent. Avoid petals that are fully opened or starting to wilt. For drying, spread petals in a single layer in a shaded, well-ventilated area, and store in an airtight container once fully dry. If you’re hoping to harvest rose hips in the fall, be sure to leave some flowers unpicked. The hips should be gathered when they’ve turned red or orange but are still slightly firm. Summer is a beautiful time to begin working with rose, not only for its seasonal abundance but also for the grounding and tender support it brings to herbal preparations and emotional wellness alike.

St. John’s wort (Hypericum perforatum) is a sunny, yellow-flowered perennial that blooms right around the summer solstice and has been used for centuries to support mood, nerve health, and skin integrity. Its bright blossoms are often gathered in midsummer to prepare infused oils, tinctures, and teas. In summer, it’s especially appreciated for its topical applications: the infused oil, deep red in color from the compound hypericin, is commonly applied to sunburns, bumps, and bruises, and may help ease sore muscles or support the body after long, active days in the sun. The plant is well known for its nervine properties and is often employed by herbalists to support emotional balance, ease mild to moderate depression, and calm frazzled nerves. While St. John’s wort’s effects are complex and multifaceted, some researchers believe its benefits for emotional health are due in part to its content of hyperforin and other mood-modulating compounds (Laakmann et al., 1998).

To harvest, gather the top few inches of the plant, including flowers, buds, leaves, and soft stems, when it’s in full bloom. Gently bruise the flowers between your fingers—if they stain your skin red, they are at peak readiness. For topical preparations, fresh flowering tops are typically infused into oil using warmth or sun exposure to help evaporate off the moisture, while tinctures for internal use are often made with fresh plant in alcohol to preserve key constituents. Whether used to support nerves or soothe summer skin, St. John’s wort is a beloved herb of the season, bringing brightness and balance where it’s most needed.

Some common contraindications to note—St. John’s wort isn’t recommended during pregnancy, nursing, or before surgery. It can increase photosensitivity and may raise the risk of cataracts with long-term use. It also interacts with many medications, including blood thinners and antidepressants, and is generally best avoided when taking pharmaceuticals.

Yarrow (Achillea millefolium) is a resilient and widely loved herb recognized for its support in first aid, digestive wellness, and circulatory balance. A longtime favorite in traditional herbalism, yarrow is both cooling and warming—qualities that reflect its ability to influence blood flow, helping the body release excess heat or retain warmth as needed (Wood, 2009). It is also astringent and antimicrobial, making it especially useful during summer for soothing bites, stings, scrapes, sunburns, and other minor skin discomforts. As a styptic, yarrow is known for its ability to slow or stop minor bleeding, making it a trusted ally in topical preparations. Internally, it is often included in teas for colds and fevers, thanks to its diaphoretic action, which encourages gentle sweating (Wood, 2007). Its bitter and aromatic nature supports digestion, particularly when sluggish or bloated. 

For summer harvesting, gather the upper third of the plant—including flowers, stems, and leaves—when the blooms are fully open. For best results, harvest in the morning after the dew has dried but before the sun’s heat diminishes its volatile oils (Moore, 2003). While the flowers are most commonly used, the leaves and stems also carry herbal value. Dry the plant in small bundles or on screens in a shaded, well-ventilated area. Whether infused into oil for bumps and bruises, made into a salve, or brewed into a cooling tea, yarrow is a versatile and abundant summer staple.

Safety note: Yarrow is contraindicated during pregnancy due to its high volatile oil content. Yarrow may cause hypersensitivity in those with an allergy to the Asteraceae (daisy) plant family.

Conclusion

Summer offers an abundance of herbs that support us in practical, beautiful, and deeply nourishing ways. From skin-soothing allies like plantain and aloe to mood-balancing blooms like butterfly pea and St. John’s wort, each plant carries its own gifts for the season. Whether used to cool, comfort, uplift, or protect, these herbs invite us to work with the rhythms of nature. In the next lessons, we will focus on practical projects and preparations, giving you the opportunity to work with these herbs directly through wellness preparations, creative crafts, and seasonal recipes.
