Botanical Nootropics: Session 1

Session 1: What are Nootropics?

Traditional medicine systems have assuredly been using herbs to support the health of the mind for many centuries, but the term nootropic is a relatively recent addition to herbal (and medical!) lexicon. It was invented in 1972 by researcher Corneliu Giurgea to describe a novel drug that he’d created, piracetam, which is considered the first synthetic nootropic. Giurgea had a fairly strict definition of nootropics, which had to meet all of these criteria (Brand, 2016; Giurgea & Salama, 1977):

  1. Enhance learning and memory
  2. Enhance cognition under stress
  3. Neuroprotective properties
  4. Nontoxic, few side effects
  5. Increase interhemispheric activity in the brain

Among herbalists, the term nootropic still incorporates most of these salient points: herbs that enhance memory, learning, or mental alertness, and—as with most of our common herbs—generally have few problematic side effects.

However, in the wider world, the general category of nootropics does also include “smart drugs,” both pharmaceuticals and drug-like dietary supplements, and you’ll find plenty of information about these on the Internet! There’s now a booming interest in nootropics, and a corresponding increase in the number of “brain-boosting” drugs, herbs, and supplements available. Some of these are entirely synthetic, while others were originally derived from plants, but have been highly concentrated to achieve potent effects. As always, simply because a substance is derived from a plant, that doesn’t mean it’s benign! 

Fortunately, there are a number of safe and useful herbal nootropics with a long history of use in Western herbalism, Ayurveda, Chinese medicine, and other herbal traditions, and we’ll focus our attention on these.

Nootropics, Nervines, and Friends

Of course, herbalists have been using herbs to support cognition long before we called them nootropics! (If you’re familiar with the term “adaptogen,” this story might ring a bell: we’ve been using herbs as restorative tonics and cognitive tonics forever, we just didn’t give them these highfalutin, marketable names!)

We use herbs in a variety of ways to affect the brain and nervous system. Some of these overlap with nootropics, while others provide complementary actions. Understanding some related categories of action can help you choose the best nootropic or other herb for any given situation, and assist in pairing herbal actions to create useful formulas.

Adaptogen: Adaptogenic herbs increase the body’s ability to adapt or respond to stressors. Because stress can impede learning and memory in different ways, some adaptogens can help improve cognitive function and mental alertness. Many herbs have both nootropic and adaptogenic properties—but not all adaptogens are nootropic, and vice versa!

Nervine: Broadly speaking, any herb that has a notable effect on the nervous system can be considered a nervine. That includes a lot of herbs, so in order to differentiate between them, we often categorize these as stimulating, relaxing, and tonic nervines. However, some herbalists limit the use of the term nervine to include just those herbs that have a relaxing effect on the nervous system, so in some cases you may find that “nervine” and “nervine relaxant” are used interchangeably.

Neurotrophorestorative: This is a more modern take on “nervine tonic.” It indicates a structural or functional restoration of the nervous system, and may also suggest neuroprotective activity.

Neuroprotective: As you’d expect, neuroprotective substances protect the cells and tissues of the nervous system from damage and degeneration. These herbs do not necessarily restore function, but rather are used to prevent loss of function. Neuroprotective herbs may not noticeably improve baseline brain or nervous system function, but may be able to slow down or halt a decline in function.Cerebral circulatory stimulant: These herbs increase blood flow to or within the brain, for example by stimulating dilation of the small vessels within the brain. In some cases, we’re quite confident that an herb enhances blood flow to the brain, specifically—usually because there’s been enough laboratory research to prove it! In other cases, we can clearly observe that an herb increases peripheral circulation, and we can extrapolate that it is likely increasing circulation to the brain as well.

Meet the Brain

In order to understand how nootropics work, it helps to have a basic understanding of some of the structures that underlie brain function.

Cellular Communication

The basic functional unit of the nervous system is the neuron, an individual cell that communicates using both electrical and chemical signals. Neurons are typically divided into three main parts: dendrites, which receive incoming signals from other cells and molecules; the cell body or soma, which contains the nucleus and directs cell function; and the axon, which carries outgoing information. The axons of individual neurons are bundled together to form nerves. The axons of some neurons are surrounded by the myelin sheath, a layer of lipids and proteins that helps insulate the axons and increases the speed at which impulses are conducted. Myelinated neurons are found in both the central nervous system (the brain and spinal cord) and the peripheral nervous system (the nerves outside the brain and spinal cord).

Neurons aren’t the only type of cell found in the central nervous system (CNS); in fact, they’re outnumbered by glial cells, or neuroglia, that assist and support neurons. But neurons are where the action happens: the primary function of the nervous system is transmitting information, and that’s what neurons do.

In the most common mode of cell-to-cell communication, electrical signals travel down the length of the neuron to the synapse, the terminus where the axon of the neuron meets its target cell. This stimulates the release of neurotransmitters or neurohormones, which travel across a gap called the synaptic cleft before they’re taken up by receptors in the membrane of the target cell. Neurotransmitters can be extremely fast acting or can take effect more slowly, depending on how they enter into and affect their target cells. In contrast, neurohormones are released into the bloodstream and travel throughout the body, so their effects are slower but more widespread.

There’s a vast array of neurotransmitters and neurohormones, and it’s not always easy to summarize how these chemicals influence the body and mind. An individual neurotransmitter can have very different effects on different tissues, depending in part on the type of receptor it binds to. For example, we frequently think of serotonin as a neurotransmitter that influences mood, and specifically plays a role in depression. However, serotonin isn’t just a happy hormone: it has dozens of other functions in the body that directly influence our stress response, perception of pain, sleep cycles, digestive processes, and cardiovascular function (Berger et al., 2009).

Brain Tissue Has Special Needs

Your brain is an energy hog! A fully developed brain weighs in at around 3 pounds, about 2% of our total body weight—but it accounts for as much as 25% of our total glucose consumption and 20% of our oxygen use (Kahmi, 2019). That’s because neurons have a very high demand for fuel in the form of oxygen and glucose. Some neurons start to die after just 5 minutes without oxygen; likewise, an insufficient supply of glucose to the brain can lead to neurological impairment.

These crucial nutrients are delivered via the blood, and the brain is highly vascularized in order to keep its tissues fully supplied. If you stretched out all the capillaries in the brain—that is, the tiniest blood vessels that directly supply cells with oxygen and other nutrients—they’d go on for 400 miles (Cipolla, 2009). The endothelium that lines the blood vessels in the brain has a very unique property that allows it to tightly regulate what substances pass into and out of brain tissue; many substances that are transported throughout the rest of the body cannot enter the brain. This special property of the cerebral vasculature is known as the blood-brain barrier.

The brain also has an unusually high concentration of lipids (fats), which are crucial to both the structure and function of the brain. In neurons, as in all human cells, cholesterol and other lipids form the plasma membrane that makes up the outermost layer of the cell. The ability of the cell membrane to perform key processes, such as transporting molecules into or out of the cell, depends in part on the lipids that are packaged into the membrane. Within the central nervous system, lipids—especially essential fatty acids—can also act as messengers, and are involved in the synthesis and transmission of neurotransmitters (Müller et al., 2015).

Barriers to Healthy Brain Function

Given the complexity of the brain, there’s no shortage of things that can go wrong! We’ll touch on a few of the problems that can disrupt brain function, which may give you some ideas about the range of concerns that might suggest the use of nootropics and other herbal support for the nervous system.

Neurotransmitter Dysfunction

Neurotransmitters are the drivers of communication between neurons, so problems with the production, release, or uptake of these compounds can lead to a range of health issues. We can have too much or too little of certain neurotransmitters, or they might be broken down or reabsorbed too quickly. Some of the most well-known neurotransmitter disorders are Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinson’s disease, and depression, but schizophrenia, epilepsy, and the autoimmune disorder myasthenia gravis are also associated with alterations in certain neurotransmitters. Neurotransmitter imbalance can also affect how we feel on a day to day basis, particularly with regard to mood, stress, and sleep.

Traumatic Brain Injury

Traumatic brain injury (TBI) is a broad category that includes everything from a mild concussion to severe damage. A fall, blow to the head, or other acute external injury can cause alterations in brain function that lead to classic symptoms such as loss of consciousness, partial amnesia, and changes in mood or memory; TBI can also affect sensory perception, physical mobility, speech, and other aspects of nervous system function. Among other things, TBI can affect neurotransmitter levels, which is one reason that cognitive and behavioral changes can last for weeks or months. In more serious injuries, there may be swelling or bruising within the brain and areas of ischemia (loss of circulation) that can lead to permanent functional changes. Severe TBI can also cause neuronal death and brain atrophy.

Cerebral Atrophy

Cerebral atrophy is a feature, rather than a cause, of many brain disorders. Loss of neurons or neuronal connections can affect the entire brain or only certain parts of it, and may be associated with TBI, stroke, dementia, or other brain disorders.

A certain degree of “brain shrink” is also a normal part of aging. Decreased brain volume, particularly in the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex, along with changes in synaptic activity, contribute to normal age-related changes in memory and processing speed (Harada et al., 2013). However, this mild form of cerebral atrophy is much less severe than the extreme atrophy associated with dementia and other pathologies. 

Those who’ve experienced “pregnancy brain”—that is, forgetfulness and decreased ability to focus during pregnancy—may not be surprised to hear that brain volume also appears to decrease during pregnancy! But on the bright side, brain shrink during pregnancy may actually help promote attachment and bonding, and it seems to be a transient phenomenon that resolves within a few years after giving birth (Hoekzema et al., 2016).

Protein Plaque Development

One of the primary hallmarks of Alzheimer’s disease is the development of clusters of protein fragments within and between neurons, known as plaques and neurofibrillary tangles. These plaques and tangles are believed to lead to disrupted cell function and death, which ultimately causes the progressive symptoms of Alzheimer’s dementia. It’s not fully understood what causes the development of these protein plaques in the first place; they also occur in the brains of those without dementia, but at a much lower rate than in those with Alzheimer’s disease. The production of these proteins is actually a normal part of brain function, but in the healthy brain they are cleared away rather than accumulating (National Institute on Aging, 2017). Factors that play into protein accumulation include genetics, chronic inflammation, and vascular problems.

Systemic and Neuroinflammation

Chronic inflammation is increasingly accepted as a critical factor in the development of neurodegenerative disorders such as Alzheimer’s, and a potential contributor to age-related cognitive decline. This pertains to both inflammation specifically within the brain (neuroinflammation) and chronic systemic inflammation, which can act as a driver to exacerbate neuroinflammation and neurodegeneration (McGrattan et al., 2019).

Blood Glucose Dysregulation and Brain Health

The brain is dependent on glucose for fuel, so fluctuations in blood glucose levels affect brain function very quickly. Even relatively mild hypoglycemia can cause the CNS to trigger a stress response that leads to symptoms like irritability, anxiety, sweating, and shaking.

But too much blood glucose is also a serious problem for the brain. Long-term dysregulation of blood glucose levels has an array of negative effects on the body, including the development of insulin resistance and type 2 diabetes. Chronically elevated blood glucose damages the capillaries and other small blood vessels. It’s especially damaging to organs that have an extensive capillary network, such as the eyes, kidneys … and the brain.

Hyperglycemia and diabetes can alter both the structure and the function of the brain; it accelerates brain aging, affects neuron activity, and can even trigger cerebral atrophy (Edwards, 2019). Cycling between excessively high and low blood sugar levels is a major complication for diabetics; episodes of severe hypoglycemia, in which the brain does not receive adequate glucose for essential function, may be linked to structural brain damage (Seaquist, 2015). Type 2 diabetes increases the risk of developing Alzheimer’s or vascular dementia by as much as 60% (Chatterjee et al., 2016). In fact, the connection between diabetes and Alzheimer’s disease is so strong that some researchers have suggested that Alzheimer’s is actually a consequence of insulin resistance within the brain, and should be described as type 3 diabetes (de la Monte & Wands, 2008).

How Do Nootropics Work?

Now that you have a basic understanding of the key pieces of the CNS, we can return to the question at hand: just how do nootropics work to affect brain function?

In some instances, there’s plenty of research that points to the “how” of nootropic herbs; for example, it’s well established that ginkgo (Ginkgo biloba) leaf extracts improve peripheral and cerebral circulation (McKenna et al., 2001). At other times we rely on information provided by traditional use of an herb to support memory, longevity, and vitality. One of the brilliant things about working with herbs is that they’re a combination of many different “active substances,” so most nootropic herbs can be expected to have multiple supportive properties.

Here are some of the primary ways nootropics are currently thought to support brain health.

Enhance Circulation and Vascular Function

Now that you know how much glucose and oxygen the brain needs, you can appreciate why increased cerebral circulation has the potential to improve brain function. Some herbs promote the delivery of blood to the brain—typically by promoting overall circulation—while others have more nuanced effects, such as increasing vasodilation, stabilizing capillaries, or promoting vascular elasticity within the brain (Bove, 2019). Enhanced circulation to brain tissue also promotes the removal of metabolic waste products, which is another benefit to overall function.

Increase Uptake of Glucose or Oxygen

Nootropic substances can also enhance the uptake or metabolism of glucose and/or oxygen within brain tissues (Suliman et al., 2016). This can occur along with or independent of increased delivery of blood to the brain. Ginkgo is an example of a plant that both increases cerebral circulation and also enhances the utilization of oxygen by brain cells (DeFeudis & Drieu, 2000).

Alter Neurotransmitter Production, Release, or Activity

By altering the amount or function of neurotransmitters, nootropics can have noticeable effects on both brain activity and on cognitive functions such as learning, memory, and mood. Rosemary (Rosmarinus officinalis or Salvia rosmarinus) is a classic “brain tonic,” long associated with memory. Scientific research has demonstrated that volatile oils in rosemary inhibit acetylcholinesterase, the enzyme that degrades acetylcholine (Bove, 2019)—in other words, rosemary’s constituents increase the activity of acetylcholine, a neurotransmitter that’s a primary driver of learning and memory.

Neuroprotective Properties

As mentioned, neuroprotective and nootropic are really two slightly different actions, but in practice there’s a great deal of overlap: many nootropic herbs are also neuroprotective.

The neuroprotective activity of herbs is often assumed to be related to their antioxidant capacity, and that is certainly one factor. The cell membrane of neurons is composed largely of fatty acids, which are particularly susceptible to oxidative damage. Consequently, brain tissue is at high risk of damage from oxidative stress, so antioxidant compounds are naturally desirable for promoting brain health. 

But phytochemicals such as phenols from fruits have shown a more pronounced neuroprotective effect than synthetic antioxidants, which suggests that there may be a more nuanced effect at work that goes far beyond their direct “oxidation-quenching” properties. For example, some dietary phenols are able to affect gene transcription factors that contribute to cell survival, and have other anti-inflammatory effects far beyond their simple antioxidant capacity (Kumar & Khanum, 2012). This brings us back to the point that herbs have complex, multifaceted effects, which are likely related to the diversity of compounds they contain, rather than one individual “active constituent.”

One specific way that some neuroprotective herbs may work is by modulating the production of neurotrophins. This group of cell-signaling proteins influences the growth, differentiation, and survival of neurons, and promotes neural plasticity and memory function. A decline in neurotrophins, such as brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) and nerve growth factor (NGF), is associated with neurodegenerative disorders such as Alzheimer’s disease as well as mood or psychiatric disorders including depression (Sangiovanni et al., 2017). There are quite a few herbs that promote BDNF and other neurotrophins; not surprisingly, many of these have a history of use in supporting mood, memory, and brain function. Notable examples include green tea (Camellia sinensis) leaf, coffee (Coffea arabica) seed, St. John’s wort (Hypericum perforatum) aerial parts, bacopa (Bacopa monnieri) whole plant, and saffron (Crocus sativus) stigma (Sangiovanni et al., 2017).

Neuroregeneration?

Until quite recently, it was believed that neurons could not regenerate, but a growing amount of research suggests that humans may well be able to regrow neurons, including in the parts of the brain that are primarily involved in learning and memory. Although it’s still up for debate among researchers, the loss of neuroregenerative capacity may be one of the hallmarks of Alzheimer’s disease, rather than business as usual for the healthy human brain (Steiner et al., 2019). 

We certainly don’t have concrete information suggesting that herbal nootropics work by stimulating central neuron regeneration, but it’s an interesting possibility that may come to light in the future. And in the meantime, even if we don’t always know exactly how herbal nootropics work, we can certainly see their effects!

Effects and Applications of Nootropics

And just what effects might we expect to see from regular use of nootropic herbs? As mentioned, nootropics generally support learning, memory, and overall cognitive function. That’s probably enough to interest most of us! But according to herbalist and naturopathic physician Dr. Mary Bove (2019), there are some particular benefits to herbal nootropics: 

  • Improve memory and learning including speed of learning, working memory, and long-term memory
  • Stimulate concentration and focus in sports-related performance as well as learning
  • Delay onset of age-related cognitive decline
  • Enhance dreams and promote the body’s ability to recuperate through sleep
  • Support high mood and improved social skills
  • Reduce feelings of depression and/or anxiety

Consequently, herbal nootropics can be useful to optimize healthy brain function, and may also be appropriate to support individuals addressing some of the following concerns:

  • Mood imbalance
  • Learning disorders
  • Attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD)
  • Mild cognitive impairment (MCI) related to aging, peripheral vascular disease, or traumatic brain injury

Dietary and Lifestyle Support for Brain Health

In addition to the use of botanical nootropics, a holistic approach should also incorporate nutritional support and a brain-healthy lifestyle. Establishing these pillars of support will go a long way in promoting overall wellness and provides a foundation for health that can ultimately make your work with herbs much more successful.

As we age, our brains naturally shrink, and it’s believed that this is at least one factor in age-related cognitive decline. Healthy dietary patterns have been linked to high brain volume later in life, meaning what we eat could limit “brain shrink” and help preserve healthy cognitive function (Croll et al., 2018).

There’s a good deal of controversy these days about what constitutes a healthy diet, with advocates for everything from paleo to vegan claiming they’ve got the golden ticket. In the study mentioned above, a high-quality diet was interpreted to mean “high intake of vegetables, fruit, whole grains, nuts, dairy, and fish and low intake of sugar-containing beverages” (Croll et al., 2018, para. 3). In general, the dietary guidelines that have seen the most research substantiation in promoting overall health emphasize the inclusion of lots of fruits and vegetables along with healthy fats such as olive oil and fish, consumption of nuts and seeds, and very limited intake of added sugars and highly processed foods (Ranade, 2018).

The MIND diet, which incorporates aspects of the Mediterranean diet, has been shown in clinical trials to slow age-related cognitive decline (Morris et al., 2015a) and decrease incidence of Alzheimer’s disease (Morris et al., 2015b). The MIND diet limits the intake of pastries and sweets, fried foods, red meat, and butter, margarine, and cheese, while emphasizing intake of vegetables (particularly leafy green vegetables), berries, nuts, olive oil, whole grains, fish, poultry, and beans.

There’s a lot we still don’t know about nutrition, but we can say that there is no single, unified diet that works for everyone! For now, we’ll focus on a few key steps you can take to keep your brain healthy at every stage of life. One thing these approaches all have in common is that they all help in moderating chronic inflammation, which, as mentioned, is one of the major drivers in not just neurodegenerative disease but many other chronic illnesses as well. 

Eat Vegetables: Colorful, Diverse, and Lots of Them!

There’s really no end to the health benefits of a diet that’s high in vegetables and fruits, and brain health is just one more on the list! Vegetables and fruits contain an array of phytonutrients that have neuroprotective, anti-inflammatory, and even nootropic effects (Vauzour, 2012). 

Polyphenols are a huge range of compounds present in all colorful fruits and vegetables, as well as other plant-derived foods like beans, tea, and coffee. Lots of research has looked at the effects of specific types of dietary polyphenols on memory and prevention of cognitive decline, ranging from cocoa flavonols to green tea flavonoids to blueberry anthocyanins. All of these foods have been found to support brain health, with effects that include increased cerebral blood flow, decreased expression of inflammatory cytokines, and other neuroprotective benefits (Vauzour, 2012).

But we can also zoom our view out even further and focus on the general consumption of fruits and veggies, rather than individual compounds. A meta-review of nine different studies found that the consumption of a diet high in either vegetables or vegetables and fruits is linked to slower rates of cognitive decline and decreased risk for Alzheimer’s disease (Loef & Walach, 2012). Notably, this doesn’t appear to hold true for a diet that includes high intake of fruits alone, which could have to do with the relatively high sugar content of most fruits or the absence of certain key nutrients not found in fruits.

The fact that veggies and fruits in general are linked to better brain health points to an important principle. Yes, individual phytonutrients like flavonoids and other polyphenols are great for our brains, as well as our vascular and metabolic health. But when we consume these in whole foods, they come packaged along with fiber, vitamins, and minerals—not to mention flavor!—which all add to the total benefit, not just for the brain, but for the body as a whole.

Certain types of vegetables seem to have a particularly strong link to brain health, notably the dark leafy greens and cruciferous (brassica family) vegetables (Loef & Walach, 2012). But it also pays to eat across the spectrum of colored vegetables; these different pigments indicate the presence of a variety of different compounds, including different types of polyphenols, so eating a rainbow of colored fruits and veggies means we’re getting a wide diversity of phytonutrients that can support brain health through an array of actions.

While just three servings of vegetables daily has a positive effect for brain health (Loef & Walach, 2012), in this case more really is better: higher intakes of fruit and vegetables are linked with progressively better health outcomes such as reduction in risk of stroke, heart disease, cancer, and all-cause mortality (Aune et al., 2017).

Maintain Balanced Blood Sugar

We’ve covered the importance of stable blood glucose levels for brain health, and what we eat is a major consideration for keeping blood sugar levels within a healthy range.

Limiting consumption of added sugars is a crucial first step here, since elevated blood glucose can lead to rebound hypoglycemia and the development of insulin resistance. Likewise, avoid foods that are high in simple carbohydrates and low in fiber and other nutrients. But balanced blood sugar is as much about what you do eat as what you avoid: prioritizing whole grains, non-starchy vegetables, and a moderate amount of whole fruits will help build a diet that’s high in fiber and nutrients while minimizing blood sugar peaks and crashes. Finally, incorporating sources of healthy fats and protein, particularly from plant sources, into meals or snacks throughout the day can keep you feeling satiated and contribute to stable blood sugar levels.

Those who already have diabetes or insulin resistance need to be extra attentive to their diet, and working with a nutritionist is highly recommended. For the rest of us, the general guidelines covered here are a great foundation.

Healthy Fats

We mentioned earlier that fats play an important role in the brain, which is actually composed of about 60% lipids. The dietary fats that we consume directly determine which lipids end up being packed into the phospholipids that make up cell membranes, which in turn affects how those cells function.

When cell membranes incorporate an excessive amount of saturated fatty acids, they behave differently than those that incorporate polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs); cell membranes that contain PUFAs have greater membrane fluidity, which makes them less susceptible to damage and cell death (Chang et al., 2009). But there’s even more to it: the type of PUFA found in the cell membrane also influences cellular function, and helps to determine which signaling molecules are produced within the cell membrane. Those signaling molecules go on to have a strong role in either promoting or calming inflammation. This is one of the reasons that omega-3 essential fatty acids (EFAs) are so important: these essential PUFAs drive production of inflammation-quenching signaling molecules, and they’re crucial for balancing out the pro-inflammatory omega-6 EFAs that are ubiquitous in the standard Western diet.

Diets low in omega-3 EFAs have been linked to accelerated age-related cognitive decline, decreased overall cognitive function and to neurodegenerative disease, as well as depression, anxiety, and other mood disorders, and some trials have shown supplementation with omega-3 EFAs can improve cognitive function as well as enhancing and stabilizing mood (Chang et al., 2009; Kumar & Khanum, 2012).

Some of the best dietary sources of omega-3 EFAs are oily fish such as sardines, mackerel, anchovies, wild salmon, and herring. (Some other fish also contain omega-3 EFAs but have a high environmental impact or may contain high levels of mercury.) Many nuts and seeds such as chia, hemp, flax, and walnut can also provide omega-3 EFAs. Keep in mind that while fish contains eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) and docosahexaenoic acid (DHA), the omega-3 EFAs we can use in cell membranes, plant-based sources actually provide a precursor molecule that has to be converted by the body into EPA and DHA. That conversion rate is very low, so it requires a much higher intake of these foods to obtain adequate amounts of the omega-3 EFAs.

These aren’t the only healthy fats around, though. For example, olive oil is a monounsaturated, rather than polyunsaturated, fat, but overall seems to have an anti-inflammatory effect on the body. Olive oil is a key component of the Mediterranean diet, which seems to promote cognitive health. Some research has also found that olive oil may have some specific cognitive benefits independent of other dietary factors (Berr et al., 2009).

Two comparative studies examined the differences between a Mediterranean diet supplemented with nuts, the same diet supplemented with extra-virgin olive oil, or advice to follow a low-fat diet. Interestingly, one study found that both the olive oil and nut groups tested higher on cognitive function than counterparts who ate a low-fat diet (Valls-Pedret et al., 2015), while the other study found that those who consumed olive oil outperformed both other groups (Martinez-Lapiscina et al., 2013). Here’s one thing that was consistent in both studies: those on a low-fat diet fared worse with regard to cognitive aging and development of mild cognitive impairment.

Once considered the bane of a healthy diet, saturated fats have gotten some positive attention in recent years. However, they still present problems when consumed in very high amounts or in the absence of unsaturated fats. Saturated fats vary in their makeup, and the type and source makes a big difference in how they affect the body.

One saturated fat that’s often touted as being particularly good for the brain is coconut oil, and more specifically the medium-chain triglycerides (MCTs) that it contains. MCTs and coconut oil have been proposed as possibly helping to prevent or even to improve existing Alzheimer’s dementia. This is based on the fact that cerebral glucose metabolism declines during mild cognitive impairment and dementia, and consumption of MCTs can increase the production of ketones, an alternative energy source for the brain (Chatterjee et al., 2020). Virgin coconut oil is very high in MCTs, and also contains antioxidant polyphenols. 

Some clinical trials have shown that adding either coconut oil or MCT oil to the diet of those with mild to moderate Alzheimer’s disease can improve memory and cognitive performance (Chatterjee et al., 2020). However, there are some important differences between refined coconut oil, virgin coconut oil, and MCT oil, and skeptics caution that because all coconut oil contains a high amount of saturated fats, it should be avoided. While it certainly hasn’t been shown to be a cure-all for cognitive health, there does seem to be benefit in using virgin coconut oil as part of dietary fat intake, particularly if it replaces less-healthy fats.

Keep in mind that all fats are not inherently brain superfoods! Certain fats and oils are highly prone to oxidation or can promote inflammation in other ways; corn, soy, canola, and other vegetable oils are best avoided, and hydrogenated or trans-fats should ideally be completely eliminated from the diet.

Move Your Body, Exercise Your Brain

This one probably doesn’t come as much of a surprise, given the abundance of evidence that shows how important movement is for overall health. Physical exercise changes both the structure and the function of the brain; it promotes neural plasticity, increases brain volume, improves cerebral circulation, improves metabolism of glucose and oxygen, and alters neurotransmitter levels (Mandolesi et al., 2018). If you think back to the start of this session, you’ll notice that this sounds an awful lot like our list of ways that nootropics benefit the brain!

Indeed, physical exercise does seem to directly improve cognitive function. Numerous studies have found that exercise enhances memory, attention, and executive function, slows cognitive decline with aging, and decreases risk of dementia. This isn’t only related to aging, since exercise improves brain function throughout life; as just one example, physically active children have been found to outperform their sedentary counterparts on cognitive skills tests (reviewed in Mandolesi et al., 2018).

The recommendation to achieve 150 minutes per week of moderate exercise also applies to brain health—but for those who don’t or can’t meet that target, even small amounts of light exercise can still have major impacts on brain health. The authors of one study concluded that each hour spent performing light physical exercise, such as light housework or walking at a typical pace, is associated with increased brain volume (Spartano et al., 2019). And it truly is never too late to start, since physical exercise can even help slow the progression of mild cognitive impairment and existing Alzheimer’s disease (Jia et al., 2019).
