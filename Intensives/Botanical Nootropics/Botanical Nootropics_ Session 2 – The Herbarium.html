Botanical Nootropics: Session 2

Session 2: Using Botanical Nootropics

For better or for worse, botanical nootropics aren’t simply a magic wand (or frond!) that can instantly rewire your brain. To be sure, many people find that herbal nootropics have real benefits, and by using them judiciously we may be able to focus our attention, work more effectively, enjoy mindful presence, or simply have a little more success in remembering what we meant to get at the grocery store! 

The goal of using herbs, nootropic or otherwise, is not to make you a superhuman, or even a different human—it’s simply to support your unique body (along with mind and spirit) to find its own balance. This might be a good time to point out that while we live in a world that often asks us to do more, and do it even faster, sometimes the best response is not to ask our brains and bodies to push through and deliver even more, but rather to take a step back and inquire about what we’re really asking of ourselves (or what’s being asked of us!) and how else we might respond. Do we need an herb to help us work at the computer for another 2 hours, or do we actually need to take a 15 minute break and go for a walk instead?

Of course, there are plenty of times and reasons that we really do need a little extra support to get through our to-do list, or just to get through our days. Botanical nootropics and other herbal allies can be incredibly helpful in supporting our day to day brain function and overall cognitive health; they can also be a wonderful aid for those managing cognitive issues like attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), traumatic brain injury, cognitive impairment, or changes in brain function related to stress, chemotherapy, menopause, or simply aging. 

However, most herbs are fairly gentle in their actions, and they won’t necessarily counteract a life that’s out of balance. If you’re operating under a chronically high stress load, working far too many hours, not moving your body, and eating in a way that doesn’t nourish you—don’t worry, herbs can absolutely still help you through! But in the long run, we can’t rely on herbs to make up for nutrient deficiencies or lifestyle imbalance, or use them as a band-aid to mask deeper health concerns; not only is that unfair to our bodies (and the plants, too!), it simply isn’t very effective. We don’t all have the ability to enjoy a perfectly balanced, organic diet, the time or capacity to exercise every day, access to clean air and water, or a blissfully stress-free life, but we can each make daily choices that move us back in the direction of balance, even if that’s just a 10 minute walk, a single change in our eating habits, or a few deep breaths to calm the nervous system and nourish the brain. 

We’ll kick off this lesson with a video from herbalist and chiropractor Dr. Charis Lindrooth, who explores the ways we can use herbs, nutrition, and other practices to help create that balance, with an eye to boosting brain health. Then we’ll move on to a dozen monographs to help you get started using botanical nootropics, including an overview of the nootropic use of each herb as well its other properties and overall effect on the body. Finally, we’ll take a brief look at some common bigger-picture health concerns that can have a major impact on cognitive function.

Plant Monographs

As herbalists, we try to keep in mind that there’s no one herb for any given complaint. As you’ll see in the following monographs, there are a wide variety of botanical nootropics; they all support healthy brain function, but they each have a number of other effects and a particular way of interacting with the body as a whole. It’s best to choose herbs that match an individual’s overall patterns, as this approach can address multiple related concerns; it’s also a good idea to work with herbs that can be combined in a balanced formula to support multiple body systems. So rather than simply taking a single nootropic herb—say, bacopa (Bacopa monnieri)—you might combine bacopa with a more warming, stimulating herb that also benefits the nervous system, like St. John’s wort (Hypericum perforatum), or with a calming, uplifting herb like lemon balm (Melissa officinalis).

For new herbalists, it can be challenging to grow our understanding of herbs into a more pattern-based approach, rather than trying to match herbs to conditions. If this way of thinking about herbs holistically is new and exciting, you might also want to consider some of our other courses for beginning herbalists!

Bacopa – Bacopa monnieri (Plantaginaceae) – Aboveground parts

Will the real bacopa please stand up? Also called brahmi or water hyssop, the herbalist’s bacopa is sometimes confused with gotu kola (Centella asiatica, another plant that’s also called brahmi) or with the common garden ground cover that’s also known as bacopa (Chaenostoma cordatum)! Our bacopa is a succulent, water-loving creeper that’s widely distributed throughout tropical and subtropical regions. It has a wildly bitter taste, and while it’s occasionally used as a food, it’s generally reserved for herbal applications. One taste and you’ll know why! Traditionally the whole plant, including the root, is used in herbalism, but you’re more likely to find just the aerial parts in commerce.

Actions: Antispasmodic, laxative, neuroprotective, nervine, nootropic

Energetics: Cooling

Use: The use of bacopa in Western herbalism is largely derived from Ayurveda, where it’s considered medhya rasayana, an herb that nourishes the mind or intellect. Bacopa is both restorative and strengthening to the nervous system; it can be useful in cases of chronic stress with deficiency and fatigue, as well as general support for memory, learning, and concentration (Pole, 2012). In the ayurvedic tradition, bacopa is commonly prepared as an herbal ghee or milk decoction.

While it is invigorating to a depleted nervous system, bacopa is not an acutely stimulating herb—in fact it can be very calming, and can even help restore disrupted sleep patterns. This is an excellent example of the amphoteric or balancing capacity of many herbs: to the depleted or deficient individual, bacopa can be rejuvenating, while someone who’s more agitated, distracted, or “wired” may find bacopa calming. In both cases, bacopa can help improve focus and mental acuity.

Short-term use of bacopa can help give a boost to focus and working memory, and because of its calming effects, it’s particularly helpful in improving focus for those with anxiety and acute or chronic stress (Bove, 2019). Bacopa and its saponin constituents have neuroprotective effects that may help mitigate damage from exposure to environmental stressors such as cigarette smoke or heavy metals, and it may help to repair damaged connections between neurons (Calabrese, 2017). With longer-term use, bacopa can improve overall memory and cognitive function, and some clinical trials have demonstrated that extended use of bacopa improves memory acquisition and retention in elderly participants (Bove, 2019; Calabrese, 2017). As both a calming and nourishing nervine, bacopa may be useful in supporting those with substance addiction, depression, attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), Parkinson’s disease, and Alzheimer’s disease (Pole, 2012).

Bacopa is a fairly cooling herb that can both suppress the appetite and act as a laxative, and it contains saponins that may be irritating to a sensitive gastrointestinal tract (Bove, 2019). This is of particular concern when being used by elders, who may be more likely to have decreased appetite or irregular bowel transit. To offset this effect, it can be combined with warming carminative herbs that help to stimulate the appetite and facilitate digestion, such as cardamom (Elettaria cardamomum ) seed, fennel (Foeniculum vulgare) seed, or ginger (Zingiber officinale) rhizome. Conversely, bacopa might be an excellent choice for someone with stress-related constipation, especially if they also have agitation or difficulty focusing due to stress.

 Some laboratory data has suggested that bacopa may help stimulate thyroid function (Bove, 2019). This is an interesting potential connection with bacopa’s effect on the mind, since low thyroid function can cause fatigue, difficulty concentrating, and mental sluggishness, but so far there’s no clinical evidence to support or refute this theory.

 Safety: Use caution in those with gastrointestinal irritation or sensitivity; start with smaller doses and increase as needed. Theoretically, bacopa could have an additive interaction with thyroid medications, although there are no reports of herb-drug interactions.

 Dose: Infusion or powder: 2-6 g dried herb/day; Tincture: 5-30 mL (1:5; 25%) in divided doses; Fresh juice: 3 tsp/day (Pole, 2012).

Bhringaraj – Eclipta prostrata (Asteraceae) – Leaf

Also known as false daisy, Eclipta prostrata, formerly known as E. alba, is a member of the Asteraceae (aster or daisy) family that grows worldwide in warm temperate to tropical climates. Thanks to its adaptability, bhringaraj can (and does!) grow almost anywhere, provided there’s a long warm summer, some moist soil, and a bit of shade. Bhringaraj is most commonly known in Western herbalism as a tonic to restore thinning or greying hair, but according to ayurvedic and Chinese traditions that’s just the beginning of what this plant can offer!

Actions: Alterative, choleretic, febrifuge, hepatoprotective, laxative, nervine, nootropic, styptic, tonic, vulnerary

Energetics: Cooling

Use: Bhringaraj is another medhya rasayana, an herb that promotes the health of the mind and acts as a restorative tonic. It’s specifically used to promote longevity and help slow mental and physical signs of aging (Pole, 2012); it is thought to help protect the senses—not just vision and sight, but also taste and smell—into old age (Winston & Maimes, 2007). Bhringaraj may also have neuroprotective effects, and preclinical studies have shown benefits in improving memory, learning, and general cognitive function (Mithun et al., 2011).

Regardless of age, bhringaraj is thought to help calm the mind, alleviate tension and agitation, and improve blood flow to the head, thus nourishing the brain. It has a cooling effect and helps to decrease excess vata and balance pitta. Excess vata in the head may be associated with dizziness, ringing in the ears, hearing problems, and insomnia (Pole, 2012), while excess heat from pitta can drive hair loss or greying (Mithun et al., 2011). Bhringaraj can also help restore balance and calm to the agitated mind and other emotional disturbances caused by excess pitta and vata (Pole, 2012).

The cooling nature of Eclipta is also useful for the liver, where it acts as a hepatoprotective and choleretic, stimulating the release of bile. In both Chinese medicine and Ayurveda, this activity on the liver is believed to help build and restore the blood; it has a strengthening, restorative effect, and may be particularly helpful where there is anemia (Pole, 2012) or other signs of deficiency. In Chinese medicine, bhringaraj (han lian cao) is described as a Liver and Kidney yin tonic, and among other things, acts to clear heat from the Blood (Tierra, 1998). It may also help to strengthen the vasculature and balance blood pressure.

However, bhringaraj can be too cooling, particularly in the digestive system. A bit like bacopa, mentioned above, bhringaraj can have a laxative effect that may be either helpful or problematic, depending on the individual! Bhringaraj has a bitter and choleretic effect that can improve appetite and increase secretion of bile and digestive enzymes (Pole, 2012), but it may need to be combined with warming herbs that support digestion, such as black pepper (Piper nigrum) fruit, ginger (Zingiber officinale) rhizome, or cardamom (Elettaria cardamomum) seed.

Safety: No concerns noted. Talk with a practitioner before using bhringaraj during pregnancy or lactation (Gardner & McGuffin, 2013).

Dose: Powder: 250 mg – 5 g daily; Tincture: 5-15 mL (1:5, 25%) daily (Pole, 2012). May be taken in divided doses.

Ginkgo – Ginkgo biloba (Ginkgoaceae) – Leaf

Ginkgo is the last remaining member of its eponymous plant family—in fact, it’s the only species in its genus, family, order, and subclass! These trees are sometimes described as living fossils, as they remain largely unchanged by evolution since the Cretaceous period, more than 100 million years ago (Crane, 2019). Individual trees are also exceptionally long-lived, and may survive for up to 3,500 years (POWO, 2020). So next time you see these golden-leaved beauties lighting up a nearby park or neighborhood, you might give a nod of appreciation for their endurance and longevity, as well as their loveliness!

Actions: Cerebrovascular tonic, circulatory stimulant, inflammation modulant, nootropic

Energetics: Neutral, slightly drying

Use: Unlike the vast majority of plants used in the practice of herbalism, ginkgo leaf isn’t known to have been widely used in any system of traditional herbal medicine. Ginkgo nut has been used in less formalized Chinese “folk” medicine traditions to help alleviate lung disorders including bronchitis, among other things, but the leaf was not part of the canon of Chinese herbal materia medica until quite recently (Dharmananda & Freuhauf, 1997). 

Much of what we know about the use of ginkgo leaf has been established within the last 50 years or so, and focuses on the use of a standardized extract rather than crude herbal preparations (that is, teas, powders, or simple tinctures). The standardized extract (EGb761) includes a regulated amount of flavones and lactones; it uses on average a 50:1 ratio, using 50 grams of raw herb to produce just 1 gram of finished extract (Braun & Cohen, 2015). This extract is widely used and has been extensively researched, and in clinical studies it has a good safety profile. Still, it’s important for herbalists to bear in mind that this kind of highly concentrated standardized extract may be more similar to a medication than a traditional herb preparation. Some herbalists recommend only the use of standardized extract, while others opt for tinctures or other traditional extracts of ginkgo leaf. David Winston finds that tea is of little to no value, as the key constituents are poorly water-soluble, but suggests the use of a basic tincture for mild circulatory issues and the standardized extract for more significant vascular problems or for cognitive support (Winston & Maimes, 2007).

The most prominent use of ginkgo is as a cerebrovascular tonic, in which it’s used to help promote memory and general cognitive function by boosting blood flow to the brain, protecting against oxidative damage and excessive inflammation, and protecting the vascular lining. Many clinical trials have been conducted to assess whether ginkgo is truly helpful as a nootropic, and the results are sometimes contradictory. However, an overall review of the evidence “suggests that oral ginkgo extract improves cognitive function in people with mild to moderate cognitive impairment when used long-term and it also provides benefits for people with dementia and neuropsychiatric symptoms when used long-term (approximately 6 months)” (Braun & Cohen, 2015, p. 420). 

As a nootropic, ginkgo is most appropriate for slowing mental decline, mild cognitive impairment, and early stage dementia; it has not necessarily been shown to be helpful in preventing dementia when used long-term by healthy adults. Several studies have found that for both vascular cognitive impairment and Alzheimer’s dementia, the combination of standard medical treatment with ginkgo is superior to either ginkgo or medication alone (Yancheva et al., 2009; Zhang & Hue, 2012). 

Another benefit of ginkgo is that it is mildly anxiolytic, and specifically has been found to be helpful for those with anxiety related to cognitive issues, for example after a concussion or other traumatic brain injury, or in ADHD or dementia. As a nootropic, ginkgo is not a quick fix; the general consensus seems to be that ginkgo should be used for a minimum of 6-12 weeks, and possibly as long as 3 months, before assessing for efficacy (Bove, 2019).

In addition to improving cerebral blood flow and oxygenation, ginkgo has inflammation-modulating properties, including reduction of inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNoS), which, as Charis Lindrooth discussed earlier in the lesson, is a key factor in neuroinflammation. It has been shown to increase acetylcholine concentration, and some limited clinical evidence suggests that, in some circumstances, Ginkgo biloba extract may be as effective as cholinergic drugs used to slow cognitive decline (Braun & Cohen, 2015; Yancheva et al., 2009).

Because it promotes cerebral blood flow, ginkgo may also be helpful in addressing some instances of tinnitus and vertigo. Studies have shown mixed results, which could be due to the fact that it is only useful for tinnitus caused by insufficient blood flow (Braun & Cohen, 2015), and is not likely to resolve tinnitus related to physical or neurological damage.

The key actions relevant to ginkgo’s use in the brain are also useful in other parts of the body, though this is often overlooked. By improving peripheral circulation and promoting healthy function of the capillaries and vascular endothelium, it may be useful for those with Reynaud’s syndrome or vascular occlusive disorders, as well as protecting against atherosclerosis. Ginkgo is sometimes suggested as an “aphrodisiac,” in the sense that it promotes peripheral circulation and thus may enhance sexual response; some evidence shows that it could be helpful for those with sexual dysfunction that’s related to the use of selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor (SSRI) medication (Cohen & Bartlik, 2008; Mischoulon, 2018). And as an inflammation modulant with a history of traditional use in respiratory disease, it may be appropriate to help manage asthma, chronic bronchitis, or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), particularly for those with other relevant complaints such as impaired circulation. 

Safety: Ginkgo is often listed as contraindicated with blood thinning medications. Most clinical trials have found that ginkgo does not alter blood clotting time or increase bleeding, but there have been some case reports of increased bleeding associated with ginkgo (Gardner & McGuffin, 2013). This is of particular concern when using ginkgo standardized extracts. Those with a blood clotting disorder or using blood thinning medications should talk with an experienced practitioner before taking ginkgo.

Because it increases blood flow to the head, ginkgo can sometimes cause headaches in those who are already prone to vasodilative headaches (Winston & Maimes, 2007), such as some types of migraines and other headaches with a diffuse, throbbing quality.

Dose: Tincture: 2-3 mL (1:4, 60%) 3x/day (Winston & Maimes, 2007); Standardized extract: 120 mg, 2x/day (Braun & Cohen, 2015).

Gotu kola – Centella asiatica (Apiaceae) – Leaf

This creeping perennial is a common culinary herb in many Asian cultures, and can be juiced for a drink, added to soup, congee, or curry, or even used as salad greens. Gotu kola and other related species, collectively known as pennyworts, grow all over the world, mostly in tropical and subtropical regions, and in some locations are so common they’re considered invasive weeds. If you’d like to cultivate these weedy wonders in a cooler climate, gotu kola can easily be grown in a pot and brought indoors for the winter.

Actions: Antispasmodic, anxiolytic, circulatory stimulant, nervine, nootropic, tonic, vulnerary

Energetics: Neutral to cooling

Use: Among Western herbalists, gotu kola may not be one of the first herbs that spring to mind when we’re searching for a relaxing nervine. But in fact it’s a wonderful and gentle anxiolytic, as well as a nootropic that assists in focus, memory, and learning. It may even have some mild adaptogenic properties and help to maintain healthy cortisol and catecholamine levels in response to stress (Winston & Maimes, 2007). Both chronic and acute stress can impact short-term memory and affect our ability to think clearly and maintain focus; gotu kola can be a great support for those dealing with anxiety or stress-related changes to cognitive function. 

In Ayurveda, gotu kola is understood to help alleviate excess pitta and vata affecting the mind, and thus is helpful in addressing stress, insomnia, and difficulty concentrating, for example in ADHD; it also protects the brain and is useful in age-related cognitive decline (Pole, 2012).

 Compounds in gotu kola seem to interact with the brain’s gamma aminobutyric acid (GABA) system; inhibiting the breakdown of this calming neurotransmitter may be one way that gotu kola contributes to feelings of calm and relaxation and can help to decrease anxiety and depression (Winston & Maimes, 2007). It also seems to help inhibit acetylcholinesterase, the enzyme that breaks down acetylcholine, and may increase neurite growth, both of which contribute to improved memory and learning (Sabaragamuwa et al., 2018).

Gotu kola is also used as a venous tonic to help improve peripheral circulation; it’s useful when there is poor circulation with impaired epithelial function, poor venous return, and in microangiopathy, in which damage to capillary walls results in edema, neuropathy, and poor wound healing (Chong & Aziz, 2013). Microangiopathy commonly results from diabetes, but can also manifest in non-diabetic chronic venous insufficiency.

Gotu kola is also an excellent vulnerary and restorative for connective tissue outside the vasculature. It promotes growth of connective tissue and repair of microcirculation, speeds up the healing process by increasing wound granulation, and can help reduce scar formation (Bone, 2008). Clinical trials have found that gotu kola can help reduce the formation of keloids and excessive scar tissue; it may also help reduce existing fibrotic tissue, and slow or prevent fibrosis in the internal organs, as in chronic kidney or liver disease (Stansbury, 2017). To promote the health of the skin and wound healing, gotu kola can be used internally and applied topically as an infused oil, cream, or compress.

Safety: No concerns noted, other than occasional reports of contact dermatitis resulting from topical application of gotu kola extract (Gardner & McGuffin, 2013). Long-standing use of gotu kola as a vegetable suggests it is generally well-tolerated. 

Dose: Infusion: 6-15 g dried leaf/day divided into 1-3 doses (Tierra, 1988); Tincture: 3-15 mL (1:3, 25%) daily (Pole, 2012) in divided doses.

Green tea – Camellia sinensis (Theaceae) – Bud, leaf

As a central nervous system stimulant, caffeine is one of the most accessible nootropics and one that many of us rely on daily. Green tea does have a modest amount of caffeine, but this familiar kitchen herb has much more to offer than just an afternoon pick-me-up! Many of the properties of green tea are also shared by white and black teas, which come from the same species but require different processing methods. Green and white teas are usually highest in beneficial flavonoids and lower in caffeine than black and oolong teas. For those who just can’t tango with caffeine, even in small amounts, green tea that’s decaffeinated using water or carbon dioxide still has many of the same benefits, even though it has a total lower polyphenol content (Chow & Hakim, 2011).

Actions: Antioxidant, astringent, hypolipidemic, inflammation modulant, nootropic 

Energetics: Cooling and drying

Use: Interest and health research surrounding green tea has focused on its antioxidant capacity, and particularly on polyphenols including epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG). These anti-inflammatory flavonoids and other polyphenols found in tea have been found to be useful in mitigating the development of disorders caused by excessive or chronic inflammatory response, including atherosclerosis and cardiovascular disease, diabetes, cancer, and neurodegenerative diseases (Pastoriza et al., 2017). 

Green tea consumption appears to improve endothelial function and to provide a protective effect against heart attack, stroke, hypertension, and atherosclerosis (Pastoriza et al., 2017). Remember that healthy circulatory function is crucial for brain function, so this is a long-term strategy for protecting the brain! Likewise, green tea may also help to improve insulin sensitivity, regulate blood glucose levels, and prevent postprandial hyperglycemia (Pastoriza et al., 2017)—and consequently may help protect the brain from damage related to excessively high or low blood sugar.

The majority of observational studies support an association between green tea consumption and decreased risk of Alzheimer’s disease, mild cognitive impairment, and other forms of dementia (Kakutani et al., 2019). EGCG, the catechin flavonoid that’s thought to underlie many of the benefits of green tea, does seem able to cross the blood-brain barrier and exert protective effects by decreasing neuroinflammation and oxidative damage (Cascella et al., 2017). It may also increase acetylcholine levels, reduce damage to neurons caused by amyloid beta aggregation, and suppress iNoS production (Cascella et al., 2017). 

But keep in mind that EGCG isn’t the only game in tea town. Tea also contains lots of other interesting compounds, such as the amino acid L-theanine, which has the nifty effect of causing relaxation without drowsiness by increasing alpha wave activity in the brain. It decreases excitatory glutamate activity in the brain and decreases the stress response, while also providing some neuroprotective effects (Adhikary & Mandal, 2017). L-theanine can help offset the highly stimulating effects of caffeine, which could be one reason some people don’t feel as “wired” from drinking tea, even though some black teas may have as much caffeine as a cup of coffee. 

So what’s the best way to get these health benefits from the tea you drink? Older research suggested that theanine content was significantly higher in green tea, but more recent studies have found that while theanine content varies among different tea samples, there’s minimal difference between white, green, and black tea when it comes to theanine (Boros et al., 2016). The longer steeping time of black tea may actually yield a brew with higher L-theanine content—though any calming effect may be counteracted by more caffeine, as well (Keenan et al., 2011).

EGCG and other polyphenols are increased with longer steeping time, but as any tea-loving purist will attest, this can significantly impact taste, making for a bitter and astringent brew. Perhaps more importantly, long steeping can result in a concentration of tannins that can be irritating to sensitive gastric mucosa. Consequently, tea infusions may be best used as a functional food; if you’re looking for more concentrated doses, consider the use of encapsulated powder, or simply adding green tea powder to smoothies or other foods.

Safety: Generally regarded as safe when used in dietary amounts. Use caution with large amounts of caffeinated tea during pregnancy or for those with a sensitivity to caffeine. It’s prudent to wait a bit after drinking tea before taking supplements or medications, especially iron and other minerals, because the tannins in tea can prevent other substances from being properly absorbed.

Dose: Infusion: 1-6 cups daily as a dietary beverage (Pastoriza et al., 2017). A standard beverage tea uses 1 tsp dried leaf (1-2 g) per 8 fl oz water; green tea is traditionally prepared using water that has been boiled and allowed to cool slightly, to around 185 degrees F, and steeped for 2-3 minutes. Powder or encapsulation: 300 mg of tea extract or 3 g dry leaf daily (Babu & Liu, 2008).

Lion’s mane – Hericium erinaceus (Hericiaceae) – Fruiting body, mycelium

This adorably funny-looking mushroom has some of the cutest common names around, including satyr’s beard and pom-pom mushroom. Lion’s mane is a forest mushroom that grows on decaying or damaged wood; it forms a round globe with tufts of spines that hang down toward the ground. This fungus is not extremely common in the wild, so if you’re lucky enough to spot one, you might consider simply leaving it to mature rather than harvesting it. Fortunately, lion’s mane is widely cultivated, and you can easily purchase prepared mycelium or fruiting body extracts, or, if you’re ambitious, even grow your own!

Actions: Anti-inflammatory, immunomodulant, nervine, nootropic

Energetics: Neutral

Use: Lion’s mane is widely known (at least in herbal circles!) as a nervous system trophorestorative or tonic. It has a reputation for helping to improve memory and focus, with antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and immunomodulant properties that are useful in neurodegenerative disorders. Some key terpenoid constituents of lion’s mane are small enough to cross the blood-brain barrier, so it’s possible that it may be able to exert some direct neuroprotective effects in the brain, as well as working through the immune system to modulate inflammation. 

Among other things, lion’s mane and its terpenoid fraction stimulates the production of nerve growth factor (NGF); as you might guess from the name, NGF stimulates neuron growth and survival, and deficiency of NGF is linked with dementia (Powell, 2014). Lion’s mane may be used as a key support strategy following traumatic brain injury, in part due to this ability to promote the restoration of neuronal function and connection.

Controlled clinical trials have found lion’s mane to be helpful in improving communication, memory, and comprehension in older adults with mild cognitive impairment when consumed as food or as an extract (Kawagishi & Zhuang, 2008). However, one study found that cognitive improvements declined after the extract was discontinued, so it may need to be used on an ongoing basis, or for an extended period of time (Mori et al., 2009).

Lion’s mane is also reported to have a calming, mood-enhancing quality; in Chinese medicine it is used to calm the shen, a property Western herbalists might equate with relaxing nervines or anxiolytics. One clinical trial found that 2 g daily of lion’s mane promoted increased feelings of wellbeing and a reduction in anxiety and depression (Nagano et al., 2010). Terpenoids in lion’s mane act as opioid receptor agonists, which could certainly explain the feel-good effect! 

In Chinese medicine, lion’s mane is also considered to strengthen the Stomach and Spleen, and is used for digestive support (Powell, 2014). Laboratory studies have found that polysaccharide-rich extracts promote the health of the gastrointestinal mucosa, protect against gastric ulcers, and may help eradicate the Helicobacter pylori bacterium (He et al., 2017). And, as with other mushrooms used in herbalism, lion’s mane has immunomodulant properties that encourage the innate immune response; it may be useful for adjuvant support during cancer, as well as for general immune health. 

Taken together, these qualities illustrate why lion’s mane is sometimes considered a general longevity tonic: not only can it support cognitive function and healthy mood, it may help alleviate the decline in digestive and immune function that are frequently seen among elders.

Some care is required when choosing preparations of lion’s mane and other mushrooms used in herbalism. Raw mushroom powder is difficult to digest and not likely to be beneficial; instead, lion’s mane and other mushrooms are best used as decoction, powdered extract, or cooked and consumed as food. Tinctures are also a good option, but should be prepared using a two-phase extraction method that combines a water-based decoction with an alcohol-based tincture.

Safety: Lion’s mane is used as a food and is generally regarded as safe. No concerns are noted for mushroom or mycelial extracts, though isolated constituents (such as erinacines) may have side effects or contraindications.

Dose: Decoction: 2.5-5 g dried or 25-50 g fresh fruiting body (Chinese Pharmacopeia as cited in Powell, 2014), or equivalent in tincture or powdered extract. Recommended dosage for powdered extract will be much lower, and depends on concentration of individual products. 

Nigella – Nigella sativa (Ranunculaceae) – Seed

Often known as black seed, nigella is also called black cumin or black caraway, and as those names suggest, it’s widely used in cooking. Like cardamom, garlic, ginger, or thyme, this peppery spice is a 

a kitchen-apothecary crossover, and is used extensively in both folk medicine and everyday cooking throughout India and the Middle East. You might also know the lovely garden flower Nigella damascena, or “love-in-a-mist.” Although love-in-a-mist (N. damascena) and black seed are in the same genus, and both can be used in cooking, they are different species and may not have the same herbal properties.

Actions: Analgesic, anti-inflammatory, aromatic, carminative, hepatic, hypoglycemic, hypolipidemic, nootropic, spasmolytic, stomachic

Energetics: Warming and drying

Use: Nigella is widely used in traditional Arabic and Islamic medicine systems such as Unani, and it’s said that the prophet Muhammed extolled the virtues of nigella to heal every disease except death (Ali et al., 2018). It is used for respiratory, digestive, and immune support, both as a crude herb and an expressed oil, and is often combined with honey. Like other aromatic carminatives, common applications include indigestion, gas and bloating, and smooth muscle spasm; it’s also used to ease headache, lower back pain, and general arthralgia (Engels & Brinckmann, 2017).

Nigella is also part of the ayurvedic materia medica. Here it’s also used as a warming digestive herb that can strengthen erratic or incomplete digestion and help resolve bloating, gas, and loose stool, and to improve liver function and digestion of fats (Pole, 2012). 

Nigella is also considered a medhya rasayana, and is used to remove stagnation affecting the mind. In addition to promoting clear thinking and concentration, nigella is calming to the mind and has a direct effect on the nervous system and mood (Pole, 2012). 

As with many of the other nootropics you’ll meet in this lesson, nigella and its constituent thymoquinone have neuroprotective and choline-modulating properties; numerous preclinical trials show improvement in parameters of learning and memory as well as possible antidepressant and anxiolytic properties. A small clinical trial found that consumption of nigella seed significantly improved working memory, attention, and other cognitive function in healthy elderly subjects (Sayeed et al., 2013). Another study of 48 adolescents found that 500 mg of crude herb powder daily for 4 weeks improved mood, decreased anxiety, and improved short-term and long-term recall (Sayeed et al., 2014).

Nigella is also used to remove stagnation in the lungs (Pole, 2012) and may be helpful in easing cough, labored breathing, and asthma. Thymoquinone has received a good deal of research attention for its ability to improve symptoms of asthma, but nigella seed also contains high amounts of omega-6 and omega-9 essential fatty acids, which may help with respiratory disorders through their inflammation-modulating properties (Yarnell & Abascal, 2011). Several clinical trials have found that cold-pressed nigella seed oil, crude seed powder, or water extract are effective in helping to control asthmatic symptoms and improve pulmonary function (Koshak et al., 2017). It’s of note here that nigella is sometimes used as a fixed oil—a complex lipid that contains high amounts of essential fatty acids and many other compounds—and occasionally as an essential oil, which may present safety issues due to toxicity of concentrated thymoquinone and other volatiles (Yarnell & Abascal, 2011).

Finally, nigella also shows promise in helping to maintain balanced blood glucose and blood lipids. Numerous studies have found nigella beneficial in cardiovascular and metabolic disorders including hypertension, diabetes, and hyperlipidemia (Hamdan et al., 2019). As discussed elsewhere, cardiovascular health and blood sugar control are both crucial for the long-term health of the brain, so nigella might be useful as both a nootropic and a general tonic for those who could benefit from support for cardiovascular and metabolic health.

Safety: Although nigella is generally regarded as safe for use in foods, traditional usage indicates that black seed and black seed oil should not be used in high doses during pregnancy (Pole, 2012). 

Dose: Whole seed or powder: 1-10 g/day (Pole, 2012); Tincture: 3-5 mL (1:3, 60%) 3x/day (Yarnell & Abascal, 2011). 

Rosemary – Salvia rosmarinus, formerly Rosmarinus officinalis (Lamiaceae) – Aerial parts

Ah, rosemary, the classic memory herb! Native to the Mediterranean region, rosemary has a history of ancient use, as described on cuneiform tablets dating back to 5000 BCE. Rosemary was later mentioned in early Greek medical texts, and it may have spread to and naturalized throughout other parts of the world in the hands of Roman soldiers and travelers (Embuscado, 2008). By Shakespeare’s time, rosemary was a common garden herb, even in chilly England, and considered a virtual panacea for all sorts of physical and spiritual woes (Keiser, 2008). Ophelia’s famous soliloquy in William Shakespeare’s Hamlet helped cement the famous association of rosemary with memory: “There’s rosemary, that’s for remembrance” (Shakespeare, 1992, 4.5.199-200). As we’ll see, she was right!

Actions: Antimicrobial, aromatic, bitter, carminative, cholagogue, circulatory stimulant, diaphoretic, emmenagogue, nervine, nootropic

Energetics: Warming and drying

Use: Rosemary is frequently used in herbalism as a warming, stimulating nervine and circulatory enhancer. You may be familiar with the use of rosemary as a hair rinse or wash—that’s also due to its ability to promote circulation to the scalp, stimulating and nourishing the roots of the hair. 

Much of rosemary’s activity is related to its volatile oil content. As with many other aromatic herbs, rosemary acts as a carminative, helping to soothe indigestion, colic, gas, and bloating. When we first taste rosemary, we’re more likely to notice its pungent and aromatic qualities, but it’s also quite bitter, and like other digestive bitters can be used to stimulate secretion of bile and digestive enzymes, help normalize bowel transit, and generally improve sluggish or erratic digestive function.

Rosemary is also used to help soothe headache, and may be used both internally or topically as part of a formula to help ease joint and muscle pain. Again, this is largely due to its ability to enhance circulation, and it may be most appropriate where warming herbs are called for—for example, where pain or tension is soothed by heat, rather than in conditions that already show signs of heat, such as redness, swelling, or irritation. The volatile oils are also highly antimicrobial, so rosemary may be useful in wound healing, helping to stave off infection as well as increase circulation to the site of injury.

But what about the business of rosemary and memory? Some historians cite the use of rosemary garlands by ancient Greek scholars and students to help with memory and learning (Embuscado, 2008). Centuries later, Gerard’s famous Herball of 1597 (a translation of an earlier Dutch herbal) notes that “Rosemarie comforteth the braine, the memorie, the inward senses, and restoreth speech” to those “with the dumb palsie” (Woodward, 1931/1969). (That last phrase refers to those with tremors or paralysis and loss of speech, which we might associate with Parkinson’s disease or stroke; note that in this time period, the word “dumb” simply meant the inability to speak.)

As we know, increased circulation to the brain is a key function of herbal nootropics, so it makes sense that this excellent circulatory stimulant would help perk up mental function. Laboratory data has also shown that rosemary’s volatile oils inhibit acetylcholinesterase production and may have neuroprotective effects including reduced neuroinflammation and amyloid beta accumulation (Habtemariam, 2016).

Multiple controlled clinical trials have demonstrated that inhalation of rosemary essential oil increases cognitive performance, recall, and working memory in both adults and children (Filiptsova et al., 2017; Moss & Oliver, 2012; Moss et al., 2017). Internal consumption of rosemary extract, diluted in water, was found to have similar beneficial effects on cognitive performance (Moss et al., 2018). Interestingly, one study found that consumption of small amounts of crude rosemary, similar to culinary consumption, were actually more effective in increasing cognitive performance than very large doses (Pengelly et al., 2012).

Safety: Rosemary is a mild emmenagogue. While small, culinary amounts are generally considered safe, larger doses should be avoided during pregnancy.

Dose: Infusion: 6-12 g dried aerial parts/day divided into 1-3 doses (Mills & Bone, 2005); Tincture: 1-2 mL (1:5, 40%) 3x/day (Hoffmann, 2003). 

Sage – Salvia officinalis (Lamiaceae) – Leaf

There are hundreds of varieties of sage, many of which make an appearance in herbalism. But amidst many more rare or exotic Salvia species, common garden sage remains among the most widely used.

This familiar kitchen and garden herb has a surprising number of uses in herbalism, and it’s easy to grow in almost any climate, as long as it gets plenty of sun. Although it originated in the warm climates of the Mediterranean region, common sage has spread throughout Europe and North America, and will overwinter even in colder climates. And in addition to stocking your pantry and apothecary, this useful herb will also draw bees and other beneficial pollinators to your garden!

Actions: Antimicrobial, antispasmodic, antisudorific, aromatic, astringent, bitter, carminative, choleretic, emmenagogue, nervine, nootropic

Energetics: Warming and drying

Use: Like rosemary and many other Lamiaceae (mint) family plants, sage acts as a carminative that can help ease indigestion, gas, and bloating. It also acts as a digestive bitter and choleretic, increasing bile production to help with fat digestion. (This is one reason that sage makes a great seasoning for meat dishes and other rich foods, along with its wonderful flavor!)

One property that sets sage apart is its use as an astringent and antisudorific (an herb that acts to stop sweating). It has traditionally been used to astringe secretions including sweat, saliva, and breast milk. Several open trials have found sage effective in alleviating episodic excessive sweating, including in menopausal hot flashes (Bommer et al., 2011), when taken daily for several weeks. As you might surmise, sage can be a very drying herb—but, conversely, it can also be used to help counteract excessive dryness or atrophy of the tissues. According to herbalist Matthew Wood (2008), the effect of sage’s bitter and oily properties on nutrient assimilation and metabolism ultimately increases lipid and water content in the body, “hence sage combats drying and aging [and] the skin and tendons are strengthened” (p. 449). Herbalist Michael Moore advises that “the properties of sage do change with the method of preparation” (as cited in Wood, 2008, p. 449); hot tea can be used to increase internal secretions and as a diaphoretic, while cold tea acts as a strong astringent for sweat, saliva, breast milk, and mucus.

Sage is also a potent antimicrobial and antiseptic. Combined with its astringent properties, this makes it a classic aid in a tea or gargle for sore throat, laryngitis, or a rinse for mouth sores or inflamed gums.

The use of sage as a nootropic reportedly dates back to 77 CE when Roman naturalist Pliny the Elder listed sage as a memory booster (Blumenthal et al., 2000). Historically, sage was also viewed as a restorative or stimulant tonic, as it helps to increase appetite and assimilation, lift the mood and increase mental alertness, and restore vitality, while modern pharmacology suggests that sage has GABAergic and cholinergic activity (Braun & Cohen, 2015).

Clinical trials have been conducted to assess the effects of inhaled sage essential oil and ingested sage tincture on memory and attention. Although most trials have been quite small and in some cases poorly controlled, all the studies point to improvement in performance of cognitive tasks, such as word recall, delayed recall, and attention, among both young and elderly participants (Lopresti, 2017). Trials have also found inhalation of sage essential oil to be beneficial for mood, helping to promote feelings of calm and focus (Lopresti, 2017).

Two specific uses for sage as a nootropic emerge here. For those with mild cognitive impairment (MCI) or dementia, sage may help to slow cognitive decline as well as alleviate anxiety that can accompany it. Sage may also be particularly helpful for menopausal transition with hot flashes, irritability, and “brain fog” or mild decline in focus, memory, or attention. Wood cites menopause or andropause with dryness of tendons and skin as a specific indication for sage and explains that, according to herbalist Phyllis Light, it may help with transition of sex hormone production from the gonads to the adrenal cortex (Wood, 2008). 

Safety: Sage contains varying amounts of thujone, which is toxic in very high doses; while adverse reactions are likely only if consuming more than 15 g sage leaf or equivalent extract in a single dose (Hoffmann, 2003), use of sage in more than culinary amounts is contraindicated during pregnancy and lactation.

Dose: Infusion: 3-9 g dried aerial parts/day divided into 3 doses (Blumenthal et al., 2000); Tincture: 2-4 mL (1:5, 40%) up to 3x/day (Hoffmann, 2003).

Saffron – Crocus sativus (Iridaceae) – Stigma

Saffron tops the list of the world’s most costly spices. That’s largely due to the incredible amount of work that goes into its harvest; crocus corms (bulbs) are planted by hand each summer, then in autumn each flower must be harvested by hand on the day it blooms, before its precious aromatics are lost to heat and sunlight. Only three delicate, bright red stigmas (essentially, a flower’s pollen landing pad) are harvested from each blossom to produce the strands of saffron—and it may take more than 150 blooms to produce a single gram of saffron (English, 2020)! 

Actions: Anodyne, antidepressant, aphrodisiac, bronchodilator, carminative, emmenagogue, hepatoprotective, inflammation modulator, nervine, nootropic

Energetics: Warming and drying

Use: Most saffron in commerce comes from Iran, India, Spain, Italy, and Morocco, but you might say there’s a “growing” interest in saffron cultivation in the United States, as well. You certainly can grow saffron in your home garden, but you’ll want to make sure you’re growing the right crocus. Crocus sativus is sometimes called autumn crocus—but so is Colchicum autumnale, a similar-looking but unrelated—and highly toxic!—plant. Luckily, you’re unlikely to make a dangerous mistake, since Colchicum lacks the distinctive red stigmas of Crocus sativus … but you might be disappointed when your autumn crocus bulbs don’t yield saffron!

Saffron is well-known as a culinary herb, famous for its use in paella, bouillabaisse, and other seafood dishes, as well as rice dishes, breads, and pastries. It’s a key flavor in Persian, Indian, and Moroccan cuisine, and it’s long been used in Ayurveda and traditional Islamic and Arabic herbalism.

Saffron was widely used in classical medicine, and its wide range of uses was described by Ibn-Sina, a Persian philosopher and physician considered one of the key figures in the development of modern medicine. According to Ibn-Sina, the many virtues of saffron include its use to improve the complexion, dissolve hot swellings of the ear and skin, strengthen the eyesight, strengthen the respiratory organs, stimulate sexual desire, exhilarate the heart, reduce blockage and adhesion of the uterus, reduce the appetite, tonify the liver and stomach, and act as a sedative to weaken the senses (Hosseinzadeh & Assiri-Hasi, 2012). That’s quite a list! Translated into the modern language of herbal actions, we might understand some of the key actions of saffron as a euphoriant or antidepressant, bronchodilator or anti-asthmatic, digestive tonic or carminative, and hepatoprotective, among others.

In the ayurvedic tradition, saffron is often taken in warm milk and used as a restorative herb that benefits all doshas. It is used to invigorate the blood, and thus can bring on menstruation and promote fertility, as well as increase hemoglobin, making it useful for problems related to anemia. It is used as a physiologic tonic for the heart and nervous system to stimulate blood flow, regulate vata disturbance, and ease nervous debility; it also promotes the related emotional health of heart and mind, encouraging compassion and love and helping to reduce depression (Pole, 2012).

A review of clinical trials found that saffron is consistently as effective as antidepressant therapy in addressing mild to moderate depression, and is also effective in improving symptoms of major depression (Lopresti & Drummond, 2014). There has also been interest in using the saffron petal as well as stigma; one double-blind controlled trial found that both stigma and petal were equally effective in alleviating depression (Akhoundzadeh et al., 2008). 

The nervine and nootropic effects of saffron may be related to neuroprotective, serotonergic, and cholinergic effects, as well its ability to modulate inflammation and protect against oxidative damage. Saffron may also have some adaptogenic properties, as it has been shown to modulate the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal stress response and decrease elevated cortisol levels (Lopresti & Drummond, 2014). Numerous preclinical studies have shown that use of saffron elicits improvement in attention and memory. In one placebo-controlled clinical trial, saffron improved cognitive function in mild to moderate MCI, and three separate controlled trials found that it increased cognitive function even in moderate to severe Alzheimer’s disease (Cicero et al., 2018). According to naturopathic doctor Mary Bove (2019), saffron may need to be used consistently for at least 3 months in order to really see its benefits in neurodegenerative disorders.

With such a costly herb, dose is a particularly important consideration; you’ll want to make sure you’re using the smallest amount possible to really see a benefit. The majority of clinical trials on saffron use a dose of 15 mg twice daily; however, many studies lack clarity about the exact preparation used. Although described as “saffron extract,” this may actually refer to either an actual standardized extract or to crude saffron powder that has been tested to confirm minimum levels of constituents such as crocin or safranal—and at least in some cases it refers to petals as well as stigma. 

How does this translate for the home herbalist using saffron threads? Each saffron “thread” (stigma) weighs 2-3 mg on average (Deo, 2003; Duke et al., 2008), so you’d need to take up to 15 threads daily to get that 30 mg dose (15 mg twice daily). However, traditional preparations may call for a lower dose of crude herb: ayurvedic herbalist Sebastian Pole (2012) recommends just 1-5 threads daily; Mary Bove (2019) suggests a tasty preparation of saffron honey using ¼ tsp saffron in ½ cup honey. By taking 1-3 tsp of infused honey daily, you’d be getting somewhere between 5 and 15 mg of saffron daily. That may not sound like a lot, but as traditional herbalists know, sometimes the synergy of compounds in whole herbs can be much greater than the sum of their parts, and smaller doses can go a long way!

Safety: Saffron may induce toxicity in daily doses of 5 grams or more, which far exceeds the amount generally used in herbalism. Saffron is generally regarded as safe in culinary amounts, but larger amounts are not recommended during pregnancy (Gardner & McGuffin, 2013).

Dose: Dried herb: 1-5 strands daily, raw or soaked in warm water or milk (do not boil) (Pole, 2012). This same dose may also be prepared as an infusion; allow water to boil then cool slightly before adding saffron. Standardized extract: 15 mg 2x/day. See text above for further discussion.

Schisandra – Schisandra chinensis (Schisandraceae) – Fruit with seed

The native range of the schisandra vine is far-eastern Asia, including parts of Russia, China, Mongolia, and Japan. You’re not likely to stumble across this climber on a forest ramble in most parts of the world, but a related rare species, Schisandra glabra, is native to the American southeast (Foster & Chongxi, 1992). Fortunately, schisandra is easily cultivated, and can thrive in a wide variety of climates, although it does need a cold winter and limited direct sunlight. Both the leaves and the flowers are pleasantly fragrant, which may help tide you over for the first 2-3 years as you wait for the vine to set fruit.

Actions: Adaptogen, astringent, expectorant, hepatoprotective, immunomodulant, inflammation modulant, nephroprotective, neuroprotective, nootropic

Energetics: Warming and drying

Use: Schisandra is known in Chinese medicine as five flavor berry or herb (wu wei zi) because it contains all five flavors: sweet, salty, bitter, pungent, and sour. This gives it a restorative effect on all the major yin organ systems, and an overall tonifying quality. However, many people find that schisandra can have a net warming and drying quality.

Schisandra fruit has long been used by indigenous peoples of Eastern Russia, reportedly to improve night vision and stamina while hunting, reduce hunger and thirst, and as a general tonic (Panossian & Wikman, 2008). This indigenous knowledge helped trigger the wave of research that led to the classification of herbal adaptogens by Russian scientists during and after WWII (Panossian & Wikman, 2008).

Today, schisandra is well known as an adaptogen and “mild CNS stimulant that enhances reflexes, work performance, and mental activity” (Winston & Maimes, 2007, p. 196). At the same time it has an overall calming effect, and thus can be helpful for both fatigue and deficiency as well as high stress, anxiety, and deficiency-type insomnia with early morning awakening (as long as it’s not taken too close to bedtime!). Schisandra has been extensively researched, with multiple clinical trials showing benefit in what was previously called asthenia—which we might call burnout in current parlance—with marked fatigue, depression, and general malaise (Panossian & Wikman, 2008). Schisandra is also an immunomodulant, and helps improve innate immune function, which can be disrupted by the immunosuppressive effects of chronic stress (Winston & Maimes, 2007). 

Many other clinical trials have shown increased physical performance and stamina in activities such as swimming, running, and other athletic events. In a similar manner, schisandra also benefits mental stamina, focus, and alertness; one clinical trial found it helped increase focus in tasks requiring sustained attention, such as sailors standing night watch and workers performing extended shifts—though, notably, it was found to have some negative side effects of sleeplessness and increased excitability after 2-3 weeks of continuous use. In other trials, schisandra helped reduce sensory fatigue and eyestrain during computer use and decreased fatigue-related errors when performing tasks requiring sustained mental activity, such as editing (reviewed in Panossian & Wikman, 2008).

Schisandra may also help mitigate what’s often called “chemo brain.” This common side-effect of chemotherapy drugs, many of which are neurotoxic, results in impaired memory, concentration, learning, attention, and executive function, which may extend even beyond the duration of chemotherapy. Schisandra and other adaptogens may be able to help reduce the neurotoxicity and negative cognitive impact of certain chemo medications (Seo et al., 2018). However, this promising possibility hasn’t been widely tested in clinical trials, and it isn’t an approach to try on your own. Many chemotherapeutic drugs have a very narrow dose range that might be altered by the use of schisandra, so schisandra shouldn’t be used during chemotherapy without the guidance of a doctor or specialized practitioner.

In the language of Chinese medicine, schisandra is said to astringe the jing and can help to retain fluids; it can be useful in curbing frequent urination, diarrhea, premature ejaculation, and excessive sweating, including night sweats (Winston & Maimes, 2007). An often overlooked benefit of schisandra is its effect on the respiratory system; it can help ease wheezing, coughing, and chronic respiratory impairment. According to David Winston, schisandra is particularly indicated when it’s difficult to draw a full, deep breath (Winston & Maimes, 2007).

Schisandra also acts as both a nephroprotective and hepatoprotective. It may be a useful adjunct to substances that can damage the kidneys or liver, such as certain pharmaceuticals, but it can also impact the metabolism of these drugs. It’s best to talk with an experienced practitioner before using schisandra alongside pharmaceuticals.

Safety: Schisandra may increase the bioavailability of some pharmaceuticals by slowing down their rate of clearance from the body (Gardner & McGuffin, 2013). It can cause heartburn or gastrointestinal irritation in sensitive individuals.

Dose: Dried fruit: 1.5-6 g daily; Decoction: soak 1 tsp (2.5 g) dried fruit in 8 fl oz hot water, then decoct for 10 minutes; drink 4 fl oz 3x/day; Tincture: 2-4 mL (1:5, 35%) 3x/day (Kuhn & Winston, 2008).

Tulsi – Ocimum tenuiflorum (Lamiaceae) – Aerial parts

Tulsi has a unique place in the materia medica of Ayurveda. It’s been called “The Incomparable One” and “The Queen of Herbs” (Singh et al., 2010), thanks to both its bounty of uses in herbalism and its role as a sacred plant that’s part of Hindu spiritual practices. Tulsi is a wonderful plant to cultivate—and to cultivate a relationship with—it’s generally very easy to grow and lends its quiet strength and calming presence to both garden and gardener.

Actions: Adaptogen, aromatic, antifungal, antimicrobial, antiseptic, carminative, diaphoretic, expectorant, hepatoprotective, immunomodulant, nervine, nootropic, radioprotective

Energetics: Warming and drying

Use: In many ways, tulsi serves as a buffer for the woes of modern life. It’s a wonderfully restorative adaptogen that helps to uplift the spirit as well as modulate the physiological response to stress. It also assists the body in detoxification in multiple ways, including protection from radiation, heavy metal

toxicants, and persistent environmental pollutants (Cohen, 2014). Tulsi also helps to protect against oxidative cellular damage and limit the chronic inflammation that is connected with a host of modern health concerns (Jamshidi & Cohen, 2017), from heart disease and diabetes to cancer and immune disorders.

Key properties of tulsi in Ayurveda include its use as a rasayana, or general restorative tonic, with a special ability to increase vitality, maintain balance in the body and mind, and provide spiritual and mental purity or clarity (Pole, 2012; Winston & Maimes, 2007). Tulsi has an ability to clear and disperse sluggishness and stagnation from many systems of the body, including the mind, the respiratory system, and the digestive tract. It is used as an expectorant to help clear mucus from the lungs and improve labored breathing, and as a carminative and digestive aid. Tulsi is said to have a prabhava or special action in reducing fevers, and thus can be used as a febrifuge in virtually any situation, regardless of the underlying cause or pathology (Pole, 2012). However, it is a heating herb, so in cases of excess pitta it should be combined with cooling herbs.

Tulsi has been shown to directly upregulate immune response and can be helpful in warding off or limiting viral infection (Jamshidi & Cohen, 2017). It also has direct inhibitory activity against a variety of organisms, including bacteria and fungi; while this doesn’t necessarily confer systemic protection, it can be useful in topical applications such as an oral rinse, wound wash, poultice, or compress.

As a nootropic, tulsi helps to increase cerebral circulation and memory; David Winston suggests it for foggy thinking, attention deficit disorders, recovery from head trauma, and depression (Winston & Maimes, 2007). Multiple studies have found that tulsi helps to improve mood, decrease anxiety, and boost attention and cognitive function (Jamshidi & Cohen, 2017). The best results may come from using tulsi for several weeks or longer, though some people may feel the uplifting effects of a single cup of tulsi tea!

Safety: Tulsi can have a blood glucose lowering effect; those using medication to lower blood glucose should work with an experienced practitioner and monitor their blood sugar regularly (Gardner & McGuffin 2013). Although generally regarded as safe in culinary amounts, tulsi may not be appropriate for regular use or in high amounts during pregnancy or while trying to conceive.

Dose: Infusion: 1-9 g dried aerial parts/day; Tincture: 5-15 mL (1:5, 25%) daily in divided doses (Pole, 2012).

Addressing Related Concerns

In the next lesson, we’ll dive into preparing and using botanical nootropics, but before we get there, it’s time to take a step back and look at the big picture. You know by now that there are plenty of good reasons to use herbal nootropics, and lots of situations in which they can be incredibly useful. When we feel like our brains are dragging, our eyes are sagging, and we can’t even remember what else we were supposed to get done today—well, no wonder we’d like a little herbal boost to get us back on track! 

While nootropics can certainly be useful, they aren’t the only type of herb that can help improve cognitive function. In the practice of traditional herbalism, we generally blend nootropics with other types of supportive herbs. In particular, adaptogenic herbs and nervines, which help modulate our response to various kinds of stressors, are often very helpful; as you’ve seen in the preceding monographs, many nootropics are also adaptogens, and these multifaceted herbs can be ideal for supporting mental and physical stamina.

And perhaps even more importantly, we have to remember that short-term fixes often won’t save the day in the long run. Nootropics can be helpful as a short-term aid, but we also need to make sure that we’re addressing other underlying factors that might be present, whether that’s related to a particular health issue or to a more general imbalance.

In addition to health conditions that directly impact cognitive function, such as Alzheimer’s disease, ADHD, or brain trauma, there are some fairly common imbalances that can also contribute to brain fog, mental fatigue, and forgetfulness. By addressing these underlying factors in addition to using appropriate botanical nootropics, you’re likely to have much more success in getting to the root of your concerns.

It’s outside the scope of this short intensive to look at every single health imbalance that might affect cognitive function, but we will cover a few common issues here. (Of course, we hope it goes without saying that you should also discuss any of your health concerns with your health care practitioner!) Detailed discussion of herbal support for each of these imbalances is a much bigger conversation, but you can find more information in the resources at the end of this section.

Anemia

The term “anemia” indicates a deficiency of red blood cells or hemoglobin, the oxygen-carrying protein in the blood. Anemia can be caused by a variety of conditions, but the most common type is iron-deficiency anemia. Iron is needed to produce hemoglobin, so deficiency results in fatigue, muscle pain, and other nebulous symptoms that can include feelings of mental fatigue and difficulty concentrating. An important consideration is that someone may have low iron and can feel very tired and run down long before they’re technically diagnosed with iron-deficiency anemia. Iron deficiency can be addressed by supplementation and/or dietary modification, but in some cases it can take time to improve and may require extra support for digestive function and gut health.

Blood Sugar Imbalance

We’ve already said a mouthful about the importance of blood sugar balance for brain health throughout this course, but it’s so important that it deserves a mention here, too. Plenty of people who don’t have diabetes or metabolic syndrome are prone to blood sugar swings, and mild hypoglycemia can certainly lead to feeling tired, spacey, and unable to concentrate! Those who experience a mid-afternoon slump may actually be struggling with low blood sugar following their mid-day meal. (This isn’t true in every single case, of course; it may just be that their mid-morning coffee has worn off, or they’ve been sitting for too long and need a walk!) Nutritional and lifestyle support can go a long way in helping to improve mild blood sugar imbalance, with regular movement, adequate protein consumption, and a careful look at intake of simple sugars topping the list.

Chronic Stress and Cortisol Dysregulation

Chronic stress can sometimes feel like the merry-go-round that rules our modern lives. The impacts aren’t limited to the brain and nervous system, but many of us will certainly notice that high stress can change our cognitive function. Not only does stress directly impact our thinking, the dysregulated cortisol response that often accompanies chronic stress can also contribute to blood sugar and thyroid imbalances. If that terrible trifecta isn’t enough, we often have insomnia and poor sleep piled on top! With so many interlocking concerns, it can be hard to know where to begin; herbal nervines and adaptogens are a great starting point, although you may also need to address other acute concerns.

Gut Health

Did you know that the gut is home to our second brain? The enteric nervous system, located within the gastrointestinal (GI) tract, sends information about the goings-on in our digestive tract to the central nervous system, and vice versa. It’s increasingly recognized that GI function affects our overall health in part through this gut-brain axis. The gut microbiome—the mixture of flora that densely populates the GI tract—also influences the function of the nervous system, and evidence is mounting to suggest that this microbiome-gut-brain triangle can affect cognitive function as well as our emotional and mental state (Gareau, 2014; Noble et al., 2017).

This is no surprise to herbalists and other practitioners of traditional medicine systems like Ayurveda, Chinese medicine, and Unani-Tibb. We’ve long recognized that digestive function is fundamental to health and wellbeing…as we like to say, you’re not just what you eat, you’re what you digest and assimilate! 

The integrity of the GI system influences our mind and spirit in part because it determines our nutritional status; if you can’t take in nourishment due to compromised gut integrity, you’re likely to develop nutrient deficiencies, including those that contribute to poor mental function. What’s more, some specific gut health issues such as increased intestinal permeability (leaky gut) can have downstream neurological and psychological effects including decreased focus, memory impairment, and neuroinflammation (Noble et al., 2017).

Thyroid Imbalance

The thyroid gland is part of the master regulatory system of the body, and imbalances in thyroid hormone production or use can have some widespread effects. Some of the most notable effects of low thyroid function include fatigue, lethargy, and difficulties with concentration and memory, along with weight gain, feeling unusually cold, constipation, dry or thinning hair, and decreased libido. Low thyroid function is a fairly common occurrence, especially among adult women. Herbs and nutrition can be used to help support healthy thyroid function, although it’s important to work with a healthcare practitioner for initial testing and ongoing guidance and support.
