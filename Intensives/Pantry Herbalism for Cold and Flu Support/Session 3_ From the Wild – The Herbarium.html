Session 3: From the Wild

Introduction

Like kitchen herbs and spices as well as some common pantry foods, many wild plants walk the line between edible and herbal, providing the necessary nutrients we need for proper functioning of our bodies and thus helping them to find balance, while also providing various supportive herbal actions. There are wild herbs used for easing colds and flu that are widespread throughout multiple regions of the world (including many backyards!), which means we often don’t have to go far to find herbal support when it’s needed. As an added benefit, wildcrafting (harvesting plants from the wild) is also a way to ensure you’re using fresh, high-quality herbs in your preparations. Wild plants are also an economical option since they don’t require a purchase, just some time and labor in picking and processing them. All of this adds up to accessible, affordable, and potent botanicals you can add to your home preparations for respiratory support. 

Wildcrafting Tips and Ethics

Before we hone in on a few wild plant allies for cold and flu support, let’s step back for a moment and talk about two key aspects of wildcrafting—namely, safety and ethical considerations. Harvesting wild plants requires proper identification of species so that you know beyond the shadow of a doubt that you’re avoiding toxic or harmful plants that may look something like the safe and beneficial herbs you’re seeking. 

Investing in a few good plant identification books is a great first step; check out the Recommended Resources section at the end of this session for some suggestions. While plant identification apps can be a helpful tool in your wildcrafting toolkit, they can also be inaccurate and are not meant to be relied on for identifying plants you intend to consume, so it’s important to confirm the information with other reputable sources. If you’re new to wildcrafting, it’s best to stick with plants that are relatively easy to identify and to research any poisonous look-alikes before venturing out with your herb basket. Even better, find an in-person teacher or mentor who is familiar with the plants of your bioregion and explore the local flora with a guide. 

While harvesting, choose plants that look healthy and vibrant; avoid harvesting browned leaves, foliage that appears unhealthy or diseased, and areas infested with bugs. It’s also important to avoid wildcrafting in polluted areas, since plants absorb what’s in the soil around them. Roadsides are notorious for collecting herbicides, pollutants from passing vehicles, and other waste, so it’s best to stay well away from roads. Other places to avoid include areas near power lines, railroad tracks, conventional farmlands, and industrial zones. Some lawns and city parks might be maintained with chemical fertilizers, herbicides, and pesticides, and even soils around homesteads and waterways can contain various contaminants. The bottom line is that before you harvest, be sure you know the previous and current uses of the land. Keeping a distance of at least 50 feet from roadsides and other polluted areas is a good general rule, but the farther away, the better.

In addition to safety measures, ethical considerations are another important part of responsible wildcrafting. Any time you leave your own land on a foraging excursion, make sure you have permission to harvest from either public or private land. Research federal, state, and local regulations in your area, and obtain harvesting permits if needed. Bear in mind that certain endangered and vulnerable plants are protected by law; however, there are also plenty of plants that remain legally unprotected, yet are nevertheless becoming rare as wild populations are depleted. This is true for many plants used in herbalism, and especially those that have commercial demand. 

A wonderful resource for educating yourself about herbs that have been overharvested and/or are ecologically sensitive to overharvesting is United Plant Savers (UpS), a United States-based organization that is devoted to the conservation of North American plants. UpS (2022) maintains a Species At-Risk List, which now categorizes herbs into three lists:

  • “Critical” plants are at a very high risk of extinction, and therefore are not recommended for wild harvest (except for sustainable use for ecological research and traditional use by Indigenous peoples).
  • “At-Risk” plants are those whose wild populations are threatened from overharvest, habitat destruction, and other ecological concerns. UpS recommends only using cultivated sources of these plants or seeking herbal alternatives when possible. 
  • “In Review” plants are suspected by those in the herbal community to be subject to overharvesting and are currently being reviewed to determine their status. In the meantime, caution is advised for both personal and commercial use.

Familiarizing yourself with the plants on the Species At-Risk List and following the suggested guidelines regarding their harvest and use is a great way to educate yourself about the status of North American plants; this resource is linked up in the Recommended Resources section. Of course, this list only covers North American species, so research is required for plants that grow on other continents. Even for those living in North America, there may also be other plants local to your area that are vulnerable or threatened, so it’s always a good idea to research the ecological status of any species you plan to harvest from the wild. The IUCN Red List of Threatened Species is another help tool and is also included in the Recommended Resources section.

However, there are many plants that have abundant wild populations, and some are even considered “weeds” or invasive plants in some regions. These wild and weedy allies are great candidates for wildcrafting! Even so, it’s still important to be mindful of your harvesting practices, so we’ve put together a few tips for sustainable wildcrafting:

  • Only harvest a small percentage of any given patch of plants. While 10% is often given as a general harvesting guideline, assess each situation on a case-by-case basis using your best judgment and ecological knowledge. 
  • Harvest with a spirit of generosity for animals that may rely on the plant for survival, and be sure to leave behind plenty of robust, healthy plants to reproduce.
  • Take only what you need and remember that dried plants have a shelf life, so only harvest what you will actually use or process within a year or so.
  • Become a steward of the plants you love and the land that supports them. Harvest in a way that impacts the area minimally, and observe the effects of your harvest so that you can adjust accordingly in the future.

Here, we’ve covered some basic guidelines for safe and ethical wildcrafting, but this is only the beginning of what there is to know! If you’re interested in learning more about identifying and harvesting wild plants, The Foraging Course and the Botany and Wildcrafting Course are great ways to develop your wildcrafting skills. To dive deeper into sustainability and discover how you can become a steward for the plants, we highly recommend the Sustainable Herbalism Intensive, which is available right here in The Herbarium!

For this session, we have intentionally chosen to feature three widespread plants with abundant populations in many parts of the world. Not only does this make for a more sustainable harvest, but it also means that you’re more likely to be able to find these common plants in your own bioregion. Let’s explore how these wild plant allies can be used for respiratory support!

Pine (Pinus spp.) needle and resin

Abundant in many areas of the globe, relatively easy to identify, and available year round, pine (Pinus spp.) is definitely a wild plant to become familiar with! White pine (Pinus strobus) may be the most commonly discussed pine in Western herbalist circles, but get to know your local pine and you can likely use it in the same way! 

Pine needles are rich in immune-supporting vitamin C, and can even be harvested throughout the winter (look for the most vibrant, green needles), although the new spring growth may be highest quality. A cup of pine needle tea can feel wonderfully refreshing in the dead of winter and may help to keep colds and other viral infections at bay. You can brew a restorative and nourishing cup of pine needle tea by adding a tablespoon of chopped needles steeped and a cup of hot water just off the boil to a heatproof mug and steeping for 10-15 minutes, covered. You might be surprised to find out that it has an earthy, grounding flavor, quite distinct from the scent of a pine! 

Both the needles and the resin of pine trees are antimicrobial and offer additional support for respiratory conditions. Pine can help to thin or break up congested mucus and move it out of the body, and is considered a stimulating expectorant ideal for a stuck, wet cough. Pine has a warming, drying energy, and can be irritating to an overly dry cough, and may need to be combined with more relaxing or moistening respiratory herbs to avoid aggravating dryness. Both needles and resin can be incorporated into respiratory steams and congestion-easing rubs.  

You can prepare a respiratory steam as you would a tea of the needles, but feel free to use more chopped needles and a larger bowl. Once the steam bath cools down to a comfortable temperature, you can place your face over the bowl at a comfortable distance, drape a towel over your head to make a tent, and then breathe in the familiar scent of pine in the moist air. Pine needles make an excellent infused vinegar, honey, or syrup, as many of the needles’ constituents are extracted in water; the resin, however, is not soluble in water and is better prepared as a high-alcohol tincture or oil. 

Pine resin can be harvested any time of year as well, but it’s important to only harvest resin from where it is dripping below a wound—the resin is there to act as a protective layer on the wound of the tree! Dripping resin can be scraped off the tree with a butter knife or similar tool (you might want to have a tool that is dedicated to resin harvest, as it will be difficult to clean all of the resin off!). 

See The Herbarium’s white pine monograph for a deeper look at this beloved tree and its herbal uses and safety considerations!


Pine Resin Chest Rub

Resin from various coniferous trees can be used in this salve preparation. If you don’t have access to pine trees, spruce (Picea spp.) or fir (Abies spp.) resin can be substituted. These resins contain volatile terpenes that can help ease respiratory congestion when used as a chest rub. The warming and stimulating nature of tree resin can also be soothing to aches and pains that often accompany the flu. It’s important to only harvest resin from where it is dripping below a tree’s wound—the resin is there to act as a protective layer on the wound of the tree! Dripping resin can be scraped off the tree with a butter knife or similar tool (you might want to have a tool that is dedicated to resin harvest, as it will be difficult to clean all of the resin off!). Note that pine resin is easier to work with if frozen and then broken down into smaller pieces with a hammer, so after collecting you can store resin in the freezer prior to use. 

Ingredients

¼ cup pine (Pinus spp.) resin
½ cup (4 fl oz) oil (e.g., olive, almond, or herb-infused oil)
1-2 tbsp (¼-½ oz) beeswax, grated
Vitamin E (optional) 

Directions

  1. Gently heat pine resin and oil in a double boiler over low heat (or a heat-safe glass or ceramic bowl or canning jar over a small saucepan of simmering water) until resin melts. The melted resin mixture is hard to clean, so you may want to use a dedicated container for this step!
  2. Strain mixture through a coffee filter.
  3. Return to double boiler and add grated beeswax. Gently heat until mixture is melted. 
  4. To determine if the salve is the consistency you desire, dip a spoon into the mixture and place it into a refrigerator so the salve will cool quickly. If the sample is too soft, add a bit more grated wax; if the sample is too hard, add a bit more oil. 
  5. If desired, add vitamin E oil (approximately ½ teaspoon) and stir to mix into the salve.
  6. While warm, pour the salve into clean, dry glass or metal containers and allow to cool. 
  7. Once cool, place the lids on securely and label.
  8. Store in a cool, dark place for up to 1 year.

Violet (Viola spp.) leaf and flower 

Like pine needles, violet leaves and flowers are also rich in vitamin C. Violets are one of the earliest wild plants to appear in the spring, and have a long history of culinary as well as herbal use. 

There are many species of violet that can be eaten or incorporated into herbal preparations, including the common blue violet (V. sororia) and sweet violet (V. odorata). Some herbalists use johnny jump-up (V. tricolor) in similar ways, and it does contain vitamin C, but it is not as demulcent as other Viola species. 

Always be sure you are 100% certain of your identification before consuming wild violet plants! Violets have been confused for poisonous plants, such as larkspur (Delphinium tricorne) or monkshood (Aconitum uncinatum). Look for the heart-shaped leaves of violets—both larkspur and monkshood have more than two lobes per leaf. Also, do not consume African violets (Streptocarpus spp.), which are unrelated, inedible plants! 

For an immune-supportive boost of vitamin C, harvest fresh leaves and/or flowers and incorporate them into salads, juices, or smoothies. Violet also makes a lovely tea, vinegar, or syrup, not only for the vitamin C content, but also for soothing respiratory symptoms. Violet has a cooling and moistening energy, and its demulcent and expectorant properties are soothing to a sore throat, dry cough, and other respiratory irritation. 

Fresh violet leaf and/or flower can also be made into a low-alcohol (approximately 25-40%) tincture or a glycerite for these purposes—you might be surprised at how goopy these become! That “goop” is evidence of violet’s soothing mucilage, and if you don’t see it in your tincture, you may have used too high a percentage of alcohol or a species that is not as rich in mucilage as V. sororia and V. odorata. 

If you are looking for a demulcent herb for a dry cough or respiratory irritation, but don’t have access to wild violets, there are also some kitchen pantry items that are quite demulcent! Flax seed, chia seed, and even oatmeal can be soaked and made into a porridge or beverage to soothe respiratory dryness. 

Violet is an herb with so many benefits for the entire body! Check out the violet monograph for a comprehensive overview of its many uses, supporting research, and safety considerations.

Throat-Soothing Violet Tea

Violet is a moistening demulcent that coats and soothes irritated respiratory mucosa. A simple infusion of this diminutive plant packs a powerful punch when it comes to offsetting the pain and irritation of a dry, scratchy throat and accompanying cough. 

Ingredients 

2 tbsp dried violet (Viola spp.) leaf and/or flower (or 4 tbsp fresh)
1 cup (8 fl oz) water
Honey to taste (optional) 

Directions 

  1. Add violet to a mug or heatproof jar either directly or in a tea infuser. 
  2. Heat water to boiling, then pour into mug or jar.
  3. Cover and let steep for 5-10 minutes.
  4. Remove tea infuser or strain infusion, composting the herb.
  5. Add honey if desired.

Plantain (Plantago spp.) leaf 

If you can’t find any violet on your foraging adventures, chances are you will find some plantain (Plantago spp.), another wild respiratory demulcent! Various species of plantain can be found in most regions of the world, and it is often considered a “weed,” due to its amazing ability to colonize even the most compacted or disturbed habitats (including your garden!). The two most common species of plantain are broadleaf plantain (Plantago major) and narrowleaf plantain (P. lanceolata)—perhaps you can guess the differences between the two! Indeed, the leaves of these two closely related species have two very different shapes; broadleaf plantain has egg-shaped leaves, while narrowleaf plantain has long, thin (lance-shaped) leaves. The leaves of both species form a basal rosette and have prominent veins that become stringy when the leaf is pulled apart. 

Plantain is a powerful ally for lung health, helping to soothe inflamed and irritated airways. It has demulcent, expectorant, and inflammation-modulating properties, and is commonly used to soothe asthma, respiratory allergies, and other sources of respiratory tract irritation. Plantain is ideal for soothing a dry cough, but it can also be used as a respiratory tonic to keep the respiratory tract mucosa happy. 

For dry respiratory conditions, fresh plantain works best, as dried leaf will be less moistening. Fresh leaves can be prepared as a tea, tincture, juice, or syrup. 

The seed husks of plantain have an even higher content of mucilage than the leaves, and are sold in commerce as psyllium (these commercial products typically use the seed husks of P. ovata). While psyllium is most commonly promoted as a source of soluble fiber to promote bowel regularity, the mucilaginous quality of psyllium can also soothe respiratory mucosa. Simply take about a tablespoon of psyllium and let it soak in 1 cup of room-temperature water for an hour or so, stir it well and drink up.

Learn more about this versatile and widespread herb in the plantain monograph! 

Forager’s Syrup 

This wild herb syrup draws upon the benefits of pine, violet, and plantain to produce a tasty syrup that is demulcent, expectorant, and contains vitamin C. Use a small amount daily for a boost to the immune system or a larger amount during respiratory infection to ease coughs, congestion, and irritated respiratory mucosa. The syrup combines the fresh juice of plantain and violet with a pine needle infusion, all teamed up with antimicrobial, expectorant, and soothing raw honey. You will need a blender to make this recipe. Note: this syrup should not be given to children under 1 year of age. 

Ingredients 

½ cup fresh plantain (Plantago spp.) leaf, chopped
½ cup fresh violet (Viola spp.) leaf and/or flower, chopped
⅛ cup fresh pine (Pinus spp.) needle, chopped
½-2 cups (6-25 oz) raw honey 

Directions 

  1. First, make the plantain and violet juice: Combine fresh plant material with 2 tablespoons of water in a blender and blend on high for 1-2 minutes. 
  2. Strain plantain and violet juice through a coffee filter or fine strainer. Measure the amount of liquid you have squeezed out of the filter or strainer, and set aside. 
  3. Heat ½ cup water to boiling. 
  4. Place pine needles in a heat-safe glass jar or mug and pour ½ cup of just-off-the boil water over them. Let steep, covered, for 20-30 minutes. 
  5. Strain pine needle infusion through a coffee filter or fine strainer. Measure the amount of liquid you have squeezed out of the filter or strainer, and pour it into a small saucepan. 
  6. Add plantain and violet juice to the saucepan. 
  7. Add honey in a 1:2 liquid:honey ratio (e.g., for 1 cup of liquid, use 2 cups of honey) for a shelf life of 1 year (refrigerated), or in a ratio of 2:1 liquid:honey for a shelf life of 3 weeks (refrigerated) (Cech, 2000; Gladstar, 2012). 
  8. Warm mixture just slightly to enable the liquid and sweetener to mix. Avoid heating above 110 degrees F (Cech, 2000). 
  9. Transfer syrup to a sanitized bottle or jar (ideally, a dark-colored jar to protect from light exposure) using a sanitized funnel and cap tightly. 
  10. Label and store in the refrigerator. 
  11. Take 1 teaspoon to 1 tablespoon as needed.

Conclusion

Respiratory infections can come on quickly and we might not always be prepared with a fully stocked herbal toolkit when the scratchy throat, cough, or congestion hits. And in times of acute respiratory illness, it’s best to stay home and rest up (unless immediate medical treatment and a call or trip to the doctor is required) in lieu of venturing out to our local herb shop or natural foods store. Luckily, most of us have at least one of the kitchen herbs, pantry items, or wild herbal allies mentioned in this intensive close at hand, and this is the perfect time to make do with what we have! We hope this intensive has inspired you to become even more familiar with the spices and herbs you see and use every day and empowers you to manage from home respiratory symptoms that do not require medical attention.
