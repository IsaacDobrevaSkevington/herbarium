Session 2: Herbal Materia Medica for ADHD & Cognitive Wellbeing

Now that we have a nice background on ADHD from Session 1, it’s time to delve into some of our most useful herbal supports for someone with ADHD, focus, memory, and cognitive issues. The goal is not to use every single herb for every person but to find those that match the patterns in the individual. Herbs can be useful on their own, but they work best in a combination formula alongside diet, lifestyle, behavioral therapy, and nutritional supports specifically geared towards the individual.

Core Herbs for ADHD Support

Consider one or more of these herbs as the backbone for your herbal protocol. They’re safe, gentle, and often quite effective. All are safe for children. Note that all suggested doses are for adults; see above directions to calculate suggested dose for a child.

With its brightly aromatic leaves, lemon balm calms, uplifts, and increases focus, and is especially useful when anxiety, overstimulation, frustration, anger, or irritation are present (Groves, 2016; Romm & Romm, 2000; Winston & Maimes, 2007). It makes a nice supportive herb in blends. This nervine-nootropic has performed well in a variety of human cognition studies, including with ADHD children, adults, and college students, and seniors with varying degrees of dementia. In a double-blind study, just one 1,600 mg dose of encapsulated leaves – not even a very potent form – improved mental performance and calmness in just one hour (Kennedy et al., 2003)! More concentrated extracts made people too sleepy at the highest doses, hindering cognition, but since it works so quickly, you can easily fiddle around to find your lemon balm sweet spot (Kennedy et al., 2002). Lemon balm tincture taken daily and lemon balm cream applied topically long term improved quality of life and decreased dementia ratings in seniors in two separate studies (Akhondzadeh et al., 2003; Ballard et al., 2002). In ADHD in children, researchers paired lemon balm with valerian root. The combo reduced school children’s poor ability to focus from 75 to 14%, hyperactivity from 61 to 13%, and impulsiveness from 59 to 22% (Gromball et al., 2014) Another study found that the combination reduced hyperactivity, attention deficits, and impulsivity in children (Ross, 2015).

Part used: Aerial parts

Adult Dose: 1-5 ml fresh aerial/leaf tincture (95% alcohol), 2-3 times per day. Glycerite at double the dose. Tea, 1 heaping teaspoon steeped for 5 to 15 minutes – this isn’t as strong as a fresh plant tincture, but it’s quite nice in combination with holy basil (Easley & Horne, 2016; Groves, 2016; Winston & Maimes, 2007).

Safety: Generally very safe and well tolerated. The tannins might upset sensitive stomachs, but taking it with food helps. Reduce dose or blend it with a mild uplifting herb if it’s too sedating.

Bacopa also supports focus and calms anxiety without over-sedating; however, it’s slower acting. In Ayurveda, it’s called brahmi, a name sometimes shared with gotu kola. This nootropic nervine supports and protects brain function via various mechanisms for all ages and has a long history of use for cognition in India (Groves, 2016; Romm & Romm, 2000; Stough et al., 2013; Winston & Maimes, 2007). In adults and elders, those taking 300 to 450 mg of bacopa extract daily for 12 weeks saw the greatest improvements in memory free recall versus other cognitive tests (Pase et al., 2012). A review of nine studies noted better cognition, speed of attention, and decreased reaction time (Kongkeaw et al., 2014). ). A handful of studies found bacopa beneficial for school-aged children, particularly those with ADHD, with significant improvements in immediate memory, perception, reaction/performance times, learning, and memory tasks (Shinomol et al., 2011), and more bacopa research on children is underway (Kean et al., 2015).

Part Used: Aerial parts/whole herb

Adult Dose: Often utilized as a capsule or standardized extracts (follow label directions), you can also tincture if fresh or dried. Consider adding 10% glycerin to stabilize the tannins and formulate it with faster-acting, better-tasting herbs (Groves, 2016; Winston & Maimes, 2007). Combining it with rosemary may synergize its effect (Ramachandran et al., 2014).

Safety: Generally very safe and well tolerated, though it is quite bitter and astringent and might upset sensitive stomachs. Market quality is iffy, but it’s not hard to grow in a well-watered pot (Groves, 2019).

Gotu kola may be one of our most broad-spectrum, useful, safe herbs for cognitive wellbeing. Long-revered in Ayurveda as a brain tonic and fed to children when they go back to school, modern preliminary research supports the claims for young and old alike. A gentle and calming adaptogen-nervine-nootropic, gotu kola quells anxiety while also easing the effects of stress (Groves, 2016; Romm & Romm, 2000; Winston & Maimes, 2007). It also improves circulation, blood vessel integrity, and the quality of connective tissue throughout the body, and is healing to the brain, nerves, gut, skin, etc. Lab studies suggest that it improves mitochondrial function, protects the nerves from damage, and reduces neural inflammation (Gray et al., 2015; Shinomol et al., 2011). Adults taking 750 mg of gotu kola extract daily for two months demonstrated better working memory and mood (Wattanathorn et al., 2008). Intellectually disabled children showed better memory and overall mental ability, and healthy adults had a reduced startle response to loud noises (Bradwejn et al., 2000; Shinomol et al., 2011). Children with ADHD demonstrated better attention, cognition, and impulse control over the course of four months taking a combination formula that included gotu kola, bacopa, lemon balm, ashwagandha, spirulina, and white peony (Katz et al., 2010). Consider it not only in ADHD but also general cognitive and neurologic conditions including dementia support, head trauma or concussion, and older adults returning to school (Groves, 2016; Khalsa, 2018; Winston & Maimes, 2007). A recent review of 11 clinical studies on gotu kola for cognition confirmed that gotu kola appears to improve alertness and cognitive function while reducing anger and appears to have no adverse effects (Puttarak et al., 2017).

Part Used: Leaf

Adult Dose: This vine-like leafy green, a creeping groundcover from India, is somewhat feeble – K.P. Khalsa likens it to spinach or lettuce – and works best when taken in large doses long-term (Khalsa, 2013). It’s wonderful in blends alongside more fast-acting, overt herbs (Groves, 2016; Winston & Maimes, 2007). Use the tincture in formula (1-5 ml 2 to 3 times/day), fresh 1:2 in 95% or dry 1:5 in 50-60% alcohol (Groves, 2016; Winston & Maimes, 2007). For dry herb as tea or cooked into food like saag, Khalsa (2013) recommends one to two ounces per day.

Safety: Gotu kola has proven itself safe for all ages in several human studies and thousands of years of use in Ayurveda. Quality control is a concern, especially since gotu kola loves to grow in sewage sludge in polluted places – grow your own or buy it certified organic from a reputable company (Groves, 2016). According to David Winston (n.d.), the best quality comes from Hawaii. Three cases of women developing hepatoxicity on gotu kola all stem from one weight loss product in Argentina (Jorge & Jorge, 2005) and appear to be related to adulteration or quality, not gotu kola itself (Gardner & McGuffin, 2013).

Additional Herbal Support

One or more of the previous three herbs tend to appear in almost any formula I create for cognitive support and ADHD; however, many other herbs may be of use in formulas and as simples (single-ingredient remedies).

Herbs that Stimulate and Increase Focus

Turn to these herbs for people who need to be perked up and brain sharpened. Of the two, rhodiola tends to be more overtly stimulating while schizandra provides a calm, clear, focused energy. Both may be beneficial for adults transitioning off stimulant ADHD medications. I’m less likely to use them for children; however, they should be safe for teenagers and young adults. It especially important to start with low doses and work up to find the dose that works without being overly stimulating.

Rhodiola (Rhodiola rosea)

Rhodiola grows in tough, rocky alpine and coastal cliff regions throughout the world, particularly in Siberia where it’s popular as a longevity tonic. Of all the herbs discussed in detail in this lesson, rhodiola has the strongest stimulating adaptogenic effect. It is not quite as strong as caffeine, though combining it with caffeine will synergize the stimulation, including any manic aspects of the herb. I use rhodiola in low to modest doses as a simple for clients who are weaning off of stimulant medications like Adderall (with their doctor’s guidance) and want a boost of energy and clarity, or as 10 to 25% of a formula for clients looking for more pep. Rhodiola’s effects kick in within one day, and according to both the research and traditional use, it can be taken in larger doses for an acute need (such as exams) or in smaller doses on a daily basis (Groves, 2016; Winston & Maimes, 2007). Studies testing the herb’s ability to alleviate mental and stress-induced fatigue have generally been positive (Ishague et al., 2012). In one study, students performed better during stressful exams while taking rhodiola (Spasov et al., 2000). In another, hospital night shift workers were less fatigued and had better mental function (Darbinyan et al., 2000).

Part Used: Root, rhizome

Adult Dose: Small doses are preferred long term while moderate/large doses can be used in acute situations, such as exams or after an all-nighter. Take 0.5-2 ml tincture (dry root 1:5, 60% alcohol) one to three times daily solo or in formula (Groves, 2016; Winston & Maimes, 2007). Try 100 to 200 mg capsules for daily use (Groves, 2016) or up to 1,000 to 2,000 mg (Easley & Horne, 2016). Avoid near bedtime.

Safety: Rhodiola may stimulate mania, anxiety, and insomnia in sensitive individuals (Groves, 2016; Winston & Maimes, 2007). Avoid in bipolar disorder. Combining it with caffeine may potentiate these effects (Groves, 2016). Sustainability and adulteration are major concerns for this slow-growing, small, boreal root crop of alpine and coastal cliff areas (Bejar et al., 2017). Seek organically or naturally cultivated rhodiola, ideally from North America, sold by reputable suppliers, or use something else.

Schizandra (Schisandra chinensis)

Herbalists debate whether schizandra is energizing or calming. It’s best known for promoting a calm, clear, alert, and focused state (Winston, n.d.; Winston & Maimes, 2007). According to David Winston, monks consumed the herb for better meditation, and Thomas Easley has clients eat several whole berries to help wean off ADHD medications – the herb likely provides both liver/detox/withdrawal and direct cognitive support here (Easley, 2017). Schizandra has many historical uses in traditional Chinese and Russian medicine and as adopted into Western herbalism: not only for focus and mental clarity but also as a liver protector, liver detoxifier, digestive bitter/sour, adaptogen, nervine, mood support, respiratory and immune tonic, and so on, yet human research on schizandra, particularly its cognitive benefits, is limited (Groves 2016; Panossian & Wikman, 2008; Winston & Maimes, 2007). A handful of studies have explored the benefits of a rhodiola/schizandra/eleuthero combination product called ADAPT-232. In one double-blind study, the formula was given to healthy, tired adult female nurses, teachers, and doctors experiencing psychological stress. Within two hours, researchers noted improvements in attention, speed, and accuracy in stressful cognitive tasks versus the placebo group (Aslanyan, 2010). David Winston finds it useful for ADHD in teenagers and adults in combination with milky oat, bacopa, and rhodiola (Winston & Maimes, 2007).

Part Used: Berry

Adult Dose: Tincture 0.5-3 ml 1-3 times per day (dry berries 1:5, 60% alcohol) (Groves, 2016). Up to 10 whole dry berries eaten per day (Easley & Horne, 2016).

Safety: Schizandra is usually well tolerated. Use caution combining schizandra with medications; it may affect CYPP450 pathways including inhibiting CYP3A4 (Natural Medicines Comprehensive Database, 2017). As an herb that’s quite bitter, sour, astringent, and pungent, it may aggravate sensitive stomachs, hyperacidity, and ulcers. Whether or not schizandra overstimulates (increasing insomnia and anxiety) may depend on the dose and form. I don’t see overstimulation in modest doses as a tincture, often in formula, and it is often used in traditional Chinese medicine for mood and sleep, while Thomas Easley, who considers it quite stimulating, has clients eat several whole berries per day (T. Easley, personal communication, 2017).

Herbal Synergists

Consider adding these herbs to your blends to give them some oomph and flavor. Herbal stimulants (above) and other aromatics such as holy basil also work well as synergists.

With its fragrant, evergreen-like needles, rosemary has been used to perk up the brain and hold memories since antiquity. Ancient Greek and Roman students braided sprigs into their hair to help them remember their studies. Dioscorides, author of the oldest recorded Western herbal, wrote that “eating of its flower in a preserve comforts the brain, the heart and the stomach; sharpens understanding, restores lost memory, awakens the mind, and in sum is a healthy remedy for various cold ailments of the head and stomach” (Pengelly et al., 2012). Several preliminary and human adult studies have explored rosemary’s value in cognition – whether inhaled and consumed – in a variety of conditions. Inhaling rosemary essential oil increased cognition in cubicle workers and improved performance in overall quality of memory and alertness (Moss et al., 2003). It may or may not work better for elders than younger clients. Researchers who gave adults an alcohol extract blend of sage, lemon balm, and rosemary or a placebo for two weeks noted improvements in elder subjects but not younger adults. Yet, in another study, university students received 500 mg of rosemary or placebo twice daily for a month. The rosemary group showed better prospective and retrospective memory and sleep quality, and less anxiety and depression (Nematolahi et al., 2018).

Part Used: Aerial parts

Adult Dose: With rosemary, less is more. In a study of elders, the lowest dose of 750 mg crude rosemary powder in tomato juice had the greatest improvements in memory while the highest dose of 6,000 mg worsened it (Pengelly et al., 2012). Try 0.5-1 ml tincture (fresh 1:2 in 95%) 1-3 times daily. 500 to 1,000 mg in capsules per day. Liberally in food, including fresh sprigs in tea (lovely with a fresh lemon wedge). Rosemary makes a fantastic synergist. In one study, combining rosemary and bacopa increased the activity of either alone. In the lab, 50 micrograms per milliliter of only bacopa or rosemary increased antioxidant activity by 100.3% or 113.4%, respectively. Combined (25 mcg/ml each), they increased antioxidant activity 155.9% (Ramachandran et al., 2014). I add about 5 to 15% to tea formulas and 1 to 5% to tincture formulas (Groves, 2019).

Safety: Quite safe. Some people may experience a headache from the increase in circulation and vasodilation. Avoid large doses in pregnancy due to a potential emmenagogue effect (Winston & Maimes, 2007). Low doses should be fine for children (Romm & Romm, 2000).

Mints offer more than a pleasant flavor. As with many mint family herbs – lemon balm, rosemary, sage, holy basil – the mint genus shows promise for cognitive acumen (Groves, 2016; Romm & Romm, 2000). Several studies have shown that inhaling peppy peppermint essential oil increases alertness, memory, and cognitive performance (Moss et al., 2008; Ho & Spence , 2005). People performed better on clerical tasks like typing, memorization, and alphabetization with improved accuracy and speed when the aroma of peppermint was present versus no odor (Barker et al., 2003). Meanwhile, a handful of studies have been conducted on healthy adults and elders with cognitive impairment taking a proprietary spearmint extract. Subjects showed improved cognition within one day with greater improvements over several weeks to months including memory, spatial working memory accuracy, attention, concentration, the ability to fall asleep, vigor, working memory, and alertness (Cook et al., 2015; Herrlinger et al., 2018; Nieman et al., 2014). Even though none of these studies were conducted on ADHD specifically, nor on children, both are quite kid-friendly.

Part Used: Aerial parts

Adult Dose: Mints are particularly nice in tea, glycerite, or syrup form (Easley & Horne, 2016; Groves, 2016; Groves, 2019). Consider adding them to bland herbs such as lemon balm and gotu kola to improve their flavor while adding additional benefit. Try 1 teaspoon of dry herb per cup of tea, 1-3 times daily. Cold water infusions of fresh herb sprigs make a nice summer sipper with light aromatics. And, of course, you can dilute the essential oil in a diffuser or with water in a spray bottle to inhale when a pick-me-up is needed (Groves, 2016). I make a fresh 1:2 (95% alcohol) tincture of peppermint to make up 1 to 5% of a formula – it’s strong!

Safety: Limit peppermint essential oil exposure with children – not for children under 3; children ages 3-6: diffusion or topical use up to 0.5% dilution (Tisserand Institute, n.d.). Do not use undiluted directly on the skin (Gardner & McGuffin, 2013). The tea is generally well tolerated. Peppermint tea and tincture (and, to a lesser extent, spearmint products) may aggravate acid reflux in sensitive individuals. Also avoid large doses in pregnancy or while breastfeeding (Easley & Horne, 2016; Groves, 2016).

Additional Herbal Tonics

These herbs play a wonderful supportive role in your ADHD protocol or formula. They support the nervous-adrenal system and improve the overall activity of your plan. For the most part, little research has been conducted on them.

Milky oat seed acts as a supreme nervine, nourishing and soothing the nervous system. Herbalists often turn to it for burnout and feeling “wired and tired.” Best as a fresh extract, it makes a fabulous backbone for a blend, especially where nervousness or excitation are present. For adults, try 1-5 ml of the fresh tincture (1:2, 95%), or corresponding vinegar or glycerin extract, up to three times daily. Very safe, but avoid it in oat allergy. It doesn’t contain gluten but contains avenin, which may cross react with gluten for some people (Gardner & McGuffin, 2013; Groves, 2016; Winston, n.d.; Winston & Maimes, 2007).

Hawthorn berries – and possibly also the leaf and flower – offer nervine, anti-inflammatory, and cerebral circulation support that is particularly useful for children with ADHD as a supportive part of a protocol. David Winston states that if he could only give one remedy for ADHD in children, it would be hawthorn berry solid extract. Hawthorn traditionally calms “disturbed shen” in traditional Chinese medicine, which may be characterized by insomnia, agitation, anxiety, and bad dreams. For adults, try 1/8 to 1/4 teaspoon of solid extract (preferred form), though larger doses of the tincture or powder mixed into recipes may be of benefit as well. Very safe and food-like with some theoretical cardiac herb-drug reactions (Gardner & McGuffin, 2013; Groves, 2016; Winston, n.d.; Winston & Maimes, 2007).

Holy basil, also called tulsi and sacred basil, offers many the mint family herbs’ cognitive pick-me-up alongside calm energy nervine-adaptogen-nootropic properties. Human studies have confirmed traditional uses including for cognition, memory, anxiety, sleep, fatigue, stress, and depression (Jamshidi & Cohen, 2017; Sampath et al., 2015). It blends well with lemon balm, rosemary, mint, bacopa, and milky oat (Groves, 2016; Winston & Maimes, 2007). Enjoy the aerial parts in tea, fresh plant tincture, capsule, glycerite, or fresh sprigs infused in cold water. Generally safe, though I have seen a few people get paradoxically anxious when they take it. It has hypoglycemic effects (Jamshidi & Cohen, 2017), so take it with food or sweetened with honey.

Ashwagandha roots bring deep, calm, restorative energy to the whole body, particularly the nervous system and the brain (Groves, 2016; Winston & Maimes, 2007). Researchers combined it with several herbs discussed here (gotu kola, lemon balm, and lemon balm, as well as white peony and spirulina) and found it effective for ADHD in children (Katz et al., 2010). A recent study examined the mental benefits for memory and cognitive decline of a 300 mg root extract capsule twice daily in adults for eight weeks. Those taking ashwagandha had better immediate and general memory as well as executive function, attention, and information processing speed (Choudhary et al., 2017). It’s usually well tolerated, but be careful in hyperthyroid disease or alongside thyroid medications – it may aggravate thyroid storm – as well as in nightshade sensitivity. It may interact with other medications as well. Do not use in pregnancy without professional supervision (Gardner & McGuffin, 2013; Groves, 2016; Winston & Maimes, 2007).
