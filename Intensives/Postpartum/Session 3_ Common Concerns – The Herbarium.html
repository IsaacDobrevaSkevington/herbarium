Introduction

In this video, Shona R. MacDougall, RH(AHG), introduces a number of the most common scenarios of the postpartum period involving the birthing parent, and how to support them holistically through mind and body.

Postpartum Depression and Anxiety

The postpartum period brings about significant physical and psychological changes. Feeling the “postpartum blues” as well as new anxieties arising with the challenges throughout this transition are common. Up to 50% of women report a transitory experience of low mood and mild irritability in the early weeks of the postpartum period; in some cases, this may persist and develop into postpartum depression, a serious condition that can affect more than 20% of mothers in the postpartum period (Stewart & Vigod, 2019). Symptoms can include persistent sadness, lack of interest in the baby, and difficulty functioning in daily life. Postpartum depression is also termed perinatal depression, which includes the occurrence of symptoms arising throughout the pregnancy period.

Postpartum depression (PPD) is characterized by a persistent low mood and a loss of interest in activities that were once pleasurable. Symptoms typically appear within the first few weeks after childbirth but can develop up to a year postpartum. The most telling symptom is a persistent feeling of sadness that does not abate. This can be accompanied by an overwhelming sense of despair and hopelessness. Many with PPD report a significant loss of interest in activities they once found enjoyable, which can include not only hobbies and social activities but also caring for their newborn. This disinterest can be deeply troubling for parents who expect to feel a strong bond with their baby.

Fatigue and low energy levels are common in PPD, often making it hard to cope with daily tasks. This is frequently compounded by sleep disturbances, such as difficulty falling or staying asleep, or conversely, sleeping excessively but still feeling exhausted. Changes in appetite, whether a loss of appetite leading to weight loss or overeating resulting in weight gain, are also common. Additionally, feelings of worthlessness or excessive guilt are typical, with parents feeling that they are failing in their role. In severe cases, these negative feelings can lead to thoughts of self-harm or even harming the baby (Stewart & Vigod, 2019), which require immediate medical intervention.

Postpartum anxiety (PPA), while less discussed than PPD, is equally important. Postpartum anxiety affects a considerable number of those in the postpartum period as well, up to 17% of women in one study (Paul et al., 2013). It often involves excessive worry and fear that is disproportionate to the reality of the situation. Postpartum individuals with PPA may experience relentless, overwhelming worry about their baby’s health and safety, often envisioning worst-case scenarios and experiencing circular catastrophic thinking. This is a heightened state of fear can manifest in physical symptoms such as heart palpitations, shortness of breath, dizziness, or nausea, which can further exacerbate the feeling of being unwell.

Sleep disturbances are prevalent in PPA, with many finding it difficult to fall asleep due to their anxiety or waking frequently to check on their baby. This can lead to chronic sleep deprivation, which in turn can worsen anxiety. Increased irritability and frustration are common, making it challenging to manage emotions effectively. Hypervigilance, or the over-monitoring of the baby or environment for potential dangers, is another hallmark of PPA, leading to constant and often irrational fear and worry.

Postpartum psychosis (PPP) is the most severe form of mental health condition in the postpartum period, characterized by extreme confusion, a loss of touch with reality, paranoia, delusions, disorganized thought processes, and hallucinations. In consideration for the health of the mother or birthing parent and baby, these conditions need to be addressed urgently by a primary healthcare provider.

Despite their prevalence, the above conditions are frequently underdiagnosed and undertreated. When left unaddressed, PPD, PPA, and PPP have the potential to impact the wellbeing of both the mother or birthing parent and the infant, leading to prolonged psychological suffering and adverse outcomes. It is important for new parents, caregivers, and healthcare providers to have awareness surrounding these conditions and the challenges associated with postpartum mental health disorders.

Hormonal Changes in Postpartum Depression and Anxiety

Postpartum depression is closely linked to the dramatic hormonal changes that occur after childbirth. During pregnancy, levels of hormones such as estrogen and progesterone are significantly elevated, supporting the development of the fetus and preparing the body for childbirth. However, immediately after delivery, there is a rapid and substantial drop in these hormones, which can profoundly impact mood regulation. Estrogen plays a crucial role in modulating neurotransmitters like serotonin, which are vital for maintaining mood stability. The sudden decline in estrogen levels can lead to decreased serotonin activity, contributing to feelings of sadness, irritability, and depression.

 The stress hormone cortisol, which is typically elevated during pregnancy, plays a role as well, and may remain high or fluctuate erratically postpartum, further exacerbating mood disturbances. These hormonal upheavals experienced after childbirth are a significant factor in the development of PPD which highlights the need for careful monitoring and support during this vulnerable period.

 Postpartum anxiety is also influenced by the hormonal shifts that occur after childbirth. In addition to the drop in estrogen and progesterone, the postpartum period involves significant changes in other hormones, such as oxytocin and prolactin, which are essential for lactation and bonding with the newborn. While oxytocin is often associated with positive emotions and stress reduction, its levels can fluctuate postpartum, potentially contributing to anxiety. Prolactin, which stimulates milk production, is also linked to mood regulation and imbalances in prolactin levels can affect emotional stability.  

The thyroid gland can also experience dysfunction postpartum, leading to symptoms of anxiety and nervousness if thyroid hormone levels become imbalanced. The interplay of these hormonal changes creates a complex physiological landscape that can predispose new mothers or birthing parents to anxiety and underscore the importance of addressing hormonal health as part of a comprehensive approach to postpartum mental wellbeing. 

Support Strategies for Postpartum Depression and Anxiety

The importance of social support and interpersonal relationships cannot be underplayed in helping new parents navigate the various mental health issues that can arise postpartum. Ensuring that there is adequate support within the social sphere can potentially mitigate some of the early stressors of the postpartum period.

Support groups, parents can share their experiences and receive emotional camaraderie from peers, have been shown to be highly beneficial (Strange et al., 2014). These groups provide a sense of community and understanding, reducing feelings of isolation and loneliness. Family support is equally as important. Involvement and support from family members can alleviate the burden on new mothers, providing them with the necessary emotional and practical help to navigate the postpartum period.

Professional support from healthcare providers, including midwives, obstetricians, and mental health providers, to monitor and address postpartum mental health issues is important. Regular check-ins with these experts may help identify symptoms early and provide timely interventions. Additionally, providing education to family members about PPD and PPA can help them better understand the challenges the individual is facing and possibly offer more effective support.

Therapies such as cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT), focusing on identifying and challenging negative thought patterns and behaviors that contribute to depression and anxiety, can be effective in managing PPD and PPA. CBT offers tools for replacing these harmful thoughts with more positive and supportive ones, helping to reduce symptoms and improve overall mood. This therapy format also offers practical coping strategies to manage daily stressors and challenges (Milgrom et al., 2015).

A healthy, whole foods diet plays a crucial role in managing PPD and PPA, providing the essential nutrients needed for optimal mental and physical health during this period. Nutrient-rich foods, such as fruits, vegetables, whole grains, proteins, and healthy fats, support physiological balance and are vital for mood regulation. Consuming a balanced diet also helps in replenishing the body’s stores of vitamins and minerals depleted during pregnancy and childbirth, fostering overall wellbeing and resilience against stressors. Omega-3 fatty acids, found in fatty fish and flaxseeds, among other foods, have been shown to reduce symptoms of depression (Liao et al., 2019). A whole foods diet, rich in fats, proteins, and fiber, with reduced intake of sugary foods, can stabilize blood sugar levels, preventing mood swings and energy crashes. Prioritizing a whole foods diet can enhance mental health, energy levels, and ability to cope with the demands of the postpartum period.

Herbal Support for Postpartum Depression and Anxiety

There are a number of herbs that can be used to support PPD and PPA. Nervines and nerve tonics help with regulation of the nervous system, and adaptogens help the body cope with the stressors of new parenthood. The safety profiles of individual herbs must be considered with extra caution during lactation, due to transference of phytochemicals through milk to baby.

St. John’s Wort

St. John’s wort (Hypericum perforatum) aerial parts  are one of the most well-known herbs for supporting depression. It has been found to be effective in mild to moderate depression due to its serotonergic activity (Seifritz et al., 2016). The herb works by increasing the levels of serotonin, dopamine, and norepinephrine in the brain, which helps improve mood (Seifritz et al., 2016). However, its safety during lactation is not well established, and it can interact with several medications, including birth control pills and antidepressants. Therefore, it should be used under the guidance of a clinical herbalist, with oversight from applicable healthcare providers.

Lemon Balm

Lemon balm (Melissa officinalis) aerial parts are an aromatic herb from the mint family known to brighten mood and commonly used to reduce symptoms of PPD and PPA. Lemon balm has calming and mood-enhancing properties, and is used to reduce stress and anxiety and promote relaxation. It works by modulating the activity of gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA) receptors in the brain, which play a key role in regulating mood and anxiety levels (Scholey et al., 2014). New parents can benefit from lemon balm and regular use may help reduce mild to moderate depressive symptoms and reduce anxiety, contributing to improved sleep and overall wellbeing during the postpartum period.

Motherwort

Motherwort (Leonurus cardiaca) aerial parts have anxiolytic effects on the mind and body, reducing both catastrophic thinking and a rapid heart rate, making it particularly useful in managing PPD and PPA. Its name reflects its long-standing association with childbirth and postpartum recovery. Motherwort is a mild sedative, helping to calm nervous tension and anxiety. It also has cardiotonic properties, which can help regulate heart palpitations often associated with anxiety. Through its effects on the nervous system, motherwort can provide emotional support and promote a sense of calm and stability for new mothers navigating the challenges of postpartum life.

Passionflower

Passionflower (Passiflora incarnata) aerial parts are well known for their ability to reduce anxiety and circular thinking and promote restful sleep, making this herb valuable for PPD and PPA. The herb contains compounds that are believed to modulate the levels of GABA in the brain (Appell et al., 2011), leading to a calming effect on the nervous system, reducing symptoms of anxiety, and improving sleep quality, which is often disrupted during the postpartum period.

Lavender

Lavender (Lavandula spp.) flower bud has a reputation for its calming aroma and anxiety-reducing actions. Lavender’s soothing properties can help reduce feelings of anxiety and promote relaxation, making it a valuable plant for PPA. It can be used in aromatherapy, and is known to reduce anxiety and improve sleep quality (Karadag et al., 2017, Lillehei et al., 2015). 

Ashwagandha

Ashwagandha (Withania somnifera) root is an adaptogen widely used to combat stress, enhance mood, and improve mental wellbeing, making it particularly beneficial for PPD and PPA. This herb helps modulate the body’s stress response by balancing levels of cortisol (Lopresti et al., 2019), the hormone released during stress. By reducing cortisol, ashwagandha can alleviate symptoms of anxiety and depression, promoting a sense of calm and emotional stability.

Tulsi

Tulsi (Ocimum sanctum) aerial parts, another adaptogenic herb celebrated for its ability to reduce anxiety and improve mood, is an ally for managing PPD and PPA. Tulsi also works by regulating cortisol levels (Sampath et al., 2015). Its calming effects help soothe the nervous system, increase mental clarity, and promote a sense of tranquility and emotional balance.

Chamomile

Chamomile (Matricaria chamomilla) flower is a gentle yet effective herb with calming, anxiolytic actions. It works by promoting relaxation and easing the mind, which can help reduce anxiety symptoms and improve sleep quality. It is safe for babies and during lactation.

Safety of Herbs in Postpartum Depression and Anxiety

The interplay of physical, mental, and hormonal changes creates a complex physiological landscape that can lead to PPD and PPA. Herbs can play an important part of a comprehensive approach to postpartum mental wellbeing, and seeking out the advice of an experienced clinical herbalist, along with consulting healthcare providers throughout this period, is vitally important. Pharmacological treatment, in severe cases of PPD and PPA, and certainly PPP, may be determined necessary by a physician, in which case it is important to assess any herbal protocols with both an experienced clinical herbalist and your healthcare provider for potential herb-drug interactions.

Potential Red Flags in the Postpartum Period

While childbirth and the postpartum period is generally safe, complications can arise that may need immediate attention. Understanding the potential red flags and warning signs, including excessive bleeding, infections, deep vein thrombosis, and postpartum eclampsia, is essential for ensuring the health and wellbeing of both the mother and baby.

Infections

Infections are another significant concern in the postpartum period. They can occur in various forms. Wounds at the site of a tear, episiotomy, or a cesarean section incision can potentially become infected. Warning signs include redness, swelling, warmth, pain, and discharge from the wound site. Fever may also accompany these symptoms. Mastitis is an infection that can occur when bacteria enters the breast tissue, causing painful, swollen, red areas on the breast, fever, and flu-like symptoms. If there is a concern of infection, seek medical support.

Deep Vein Thrombosis

Deep vein thrombosis (DVT) is a condition where blood clots form in the veins, usually in the legs. Though rare, pregnancy and the postpartum period increase the risk of DVT due to changes in blood flow and clotting factors. Warning signs of DVT include: Swelling in one leg, pain or tenderness in the leg, often starting in the calf, red or discolored skin on the leg, or a feeling of warmth in the affected leg.

DVT can lead to pulmonary embolism if a blood clot breaks free and travels to the lungs. Symptoms of a pulmonary embolism include sudden shortness of breath, chest pain, and a rapid heart rate. Immediate medical attention is required if these symptoms occur. Preventative measures for DVT include gentle movement after delivery, compression stockings, and in high-risk cases, anticoagulant medications prescribed by a physician.

Postpartum Eclampsia

Postpartum eclampsia is a rare but severe condition that can develop after childbirth, even when preeclampsia does not occur during pregnancy. It typically occurs within 48 hours after delivery but can develop up to several weeks postpartum. The exact cause of postpartum eclampsia is unknown, but it involves high blood pressure and excess protein in the urine. Warning signs include:

  •     Severe headaches
  •     Vision changes, such as blurriness or sensitivity to light
  •     Upper abdominal pain, often under the ribs on the right side
  •     Nausea or vomiting
  •     Sudden weight gain and swelling, particularly in the face and hands
  •     High blood pressure

In severe cases, postpartum eclampsia can lead to seizures, stroke, and organ damage. Immediate medical treatment is crucial to manage this condition.

Postpartum Hemorrhage

Postpartum hemorrhage is a serious condition characterized by excessive bleeding following childbirth. It is one of the leading causes of maternal morbidity and mortality worldwide and is typically defined as losing more than 500 mL (approximately 16 fl oz) of blood after a vaginal delivery or more than 1000 mL (approximately 32 fl oz) after a cesarean section. Early detection is important, and warning signs include soaking through more than one menstrual pad per hour, passing large blood clots, and continuous bright red bleeding. Additional symptoms might include a rapid heart rate, dizziness, blurry vision, weakness, a drop in blood pressure, or a loss of consciousness.

Acute postpartum hemorrhage occurs when a birthing parent experiences severe vaginal bleeding that occurs typically within 24 hours after birth, regardless of delivery method. This is referred to as primary postpartum hemorrhage, whereas secondary postpartum hemorrhage may occur anywhere from 24 hours to up to 12 weeks after delivery (Wormer et al., 2023). 

Prompt medical intervention is required. Since postpartum hemorrhage can be a serious condition that can lead to major complications including death, only advanced practitioners should employ herbal preparations while ensuring that they are not contraindicated with any medications and simultaneously seeking appropriate medical attention. Herbal supports in this context should be considered complementary to medical interventions such as administration of medication and uterine compression.

Herbs to Support Postpartum Hemorrhage

 

There are herbs with a long history of traditional use in supporting the body during a postpartum hemorrhage. Many of these herbs are considered styptics and astringents, useful for their ability to contract and pull tissues together and help slow, staunch, and resolve bleeding. 

Advanced preparation of postpartum hemorrhage supportive herbs is extremely important. It is critical for these herbs to be in preparations that are on hand and ready to use. A tea, for example, should be made up in advance and cooled for as-needed dosing. Tincture formulas may be given as often as once per minute (Clark, 2004) if needed so they should be available in the appropriate quantity.

Shepherd’s Purse

Shepherd’s purse (Capsella bursa-pastoris) aerial parts administered immediately after birth may help to reduce the severity of bleeding. One study of 100 women who experienced postpartum hemorrhage found that those who were given both oxytocin and 10 drops of shepherd’s purse extract had a statistically significant improvement in bleeding, as compared to those who were only given oxytocin (Ghalandari et al., 2017).

Yarrow

Yarrow (Achillea millefolium) aerial parts are a classic styptic and used to help support excessive bleeding in a variety of situations. Herbalist, midwife’s assistant, and doula Demetria Clark (2004) suggests taking a blend of half shepherd’s purse extract and half yarrow extract to combat postpartum hemorrhage, equaling ½ teaspoon in warm water every 30 minutes. 

Blue Cohosh

Blue cohosh (Caulophyllum thalictroides) root has a specific affinity for helping the body to adjust hormonally after birth, when used in small doses of 10 drops or less under guidance of an experienced herbalist. It is often used in Chinese medicine to combat heavy bleeding (Qin et al., 2018), may be used as a uterine and ovarian tonic (Romm, 2018), and is contained in many popular anti-hemorrhagic herbal formulas for the postpartum period. 

Motherwort

Motherwort (Leonurus cardiaca) aerial parts support the uterus in contracting and regaining normalcy after the major changes of labor and delivery, and makes an excellent addition to a postpartum hemorrhage formula. It may also be given after birth proactively before any excess postpartum bleeding is noted to help stave off excess blood loss. 

Injectable intramuscular doses of motherwort are used in Chinese medicine to staunch bleeding. One study of a large number of those who had cesarean sections demonstrated that use of intramuscular motherwort in 586 women in addition to intravenous use of oxytocin produced significantly lower rates of postpartum hemorrhage as compared to the 21,933 women who used intramuscular oxytocin alone (Li et al., 2022). Similarly, one meta-analysis suggested that those who had a vaginal delivery were at lower risk for postpartum blood loss if they were given additional injected motherwort rather than only oxytocin (Yu et al., 2019).

Lady’s Mantle

Lady’s mantle (Alchemilla vulgaris) aerial parts are a blood coagulant which have a specific indication for excessive bleeding, especially that which comes from the uterus but is not menstrual blood (Whelan, n.d.). The flower essence or water drops collected from the tops of the leaves in the early morning (which are actually excreted by the leaves in a process called guttation) is also specific for aiding in the body’s energetic transition into the postpartum period. 

Witch Hazel

Witch hazel (Hamamelis virginiana) leaf or bark is among the most astringent, tissue-tightening herbs. It is almost always used topically, but can be used in tincture form during a postpartum hemorrhage for its powerful tissue-toning abilities and usefulness in staunching bleeding (Clark, 2004).

Supporting Recovery After Postpartum Hemorrhage

Supporting the body after blood loss can be done with both herbs and foods, which can be complementary to standard medical interventions such as a blood transfusion. Herbal support during recovery from a postpartum hemorrhage should be done alongside lifestyle shifts including gentle exercise, rest, and a diet including plenty of iron-rich foods such as dark leafy greens, lentils and beans, shellfish, and lean and red meats.

Nutritive Herbs

Blood building, tonifying, and nutritive herbs are the most useful for restoring and rebuilding the blood and improving hemoglobin after a birth with significant blood loss. Overnight infusions of nettle (Urtica dioica) leaf help to replenish the blood and build back stores of nutrition. Raspberry (Rubus idaeus) leaf infusion helps to restore tone to the uterus after birth. Dried fruits like apricots and goji (Lycium barbarum) berries can also be incorporated into the diet to improve iron and other essential mineral levels. 

Yellow dock (Rumex crispus) root is a good source of iron (Idris et al., 2019) and can be taken on its own as a tonic or blended into a nutrient-rich molasses-based syrup to help improve the body’s absorption of iron and regulate digestion (Angier, 2008). It may be combined with other iron-rich foods and herbs, such as seaweed or nettle, for optimal absorption. 

Recognizing and Responding to Red Flags

Recognizing and responding to these red flags requires awareness from both healthcare providers and new mothers and parents; family and friends, alongside midwives and herbalists who are also in close contact can also be aware of what to look for. Education about potential warning signs and when to seek medical help is crucial. Routine postpartum checkups are essential for monitoring postpartum recovery and identifying any early signs of complications.

Conclusion

This session explored how to best holistically support many of the most common concerns of the postpartum period for the birthing mother, including dealing with acute physiological changes, postpartum anxiety and depression, and how to respond to possible warning signs and seek further care.
