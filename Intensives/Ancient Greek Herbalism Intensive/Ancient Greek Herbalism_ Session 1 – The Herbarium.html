Ancient Greek Herbalism: Session 1

OVERVIEW OF HERBAL MEDICINE IN ANCIENT GREECE

“A healer is a man worth many others for his skill to cut out arrows and spread soothing medicines on wounds.” – Homer, The Iliad (11.514–515)

The ancient Greeks believed that the gods were the first herbalists and that knowledge of medicinal plants were from divine insight. It is these very herbs – written about by ancient healers, woven into fantastical myths, and depicted in artistic imagery – that we will explore in this course. Many of these herbs remain familiar to us in our kitchens and apothecaries, including sage, oregano, and thyme, while others have fallen out of popular use for a variety of reasons, including becoming endangered, prohibitively expensive, or simply outdated in terms of their use. In this Session, we will explore: material evidence of herbal medicine in ancient Greece, including the influences from Egypt and India; the key historical figures who wrote about plants with medicinal applications; and the four humours of ancient Greek medicine that left an indelible mark on our perceptions towards health. These concepts will provide a foundation on herbal medicine in the ancient Greek world and unveil the timeless magic of these plants.

Key Periods of Ancient Greece

  • 30th – 11th centuries BCE: Minoan civilization on Crete
  • 16th – 12th centuries BCE: Mycenaean civilization on mainland Greece
  • 8th – 6th centuries BCE: Hesiod writes mythological stories and Homer writes The Iliad and The Odyssey. Greek settlements rise across the Mediterranean region, including western Italy, Asia Minor, and north Africa.
  • 5th – 3rd centuries BCE: Hippocrates and scholarship in philosophy, medicine, and the natural sciences is on the rise. Theophrastus writes Enquiry into Plants. Alexander the Great conquers a vast empire. Agnodice presumably first female to study and practice medicine.
  • 1st – 2nd centuries CE: Dioscorides writes De Materia Medica. Galen resurrects theory of four humours. 

Our journey begins thousands of years ago on a rugged and picturesque land nestled between mainland Europe and the vast lands of Asia, with the African continent to its south. The earliest ancient Greek civilizations were the Minoans on the island of Crete and the Mycenaeans on mainland Greece. The earliest evidence of their use of plants is found on Bronze Age tablets dating from the 14th and 13th centuries BCE, which list coriander, safflower, and saffron (Arnott, 1996). Herbs from Egypt and Asia were also identified in early use, including opium from the poppy plant (Papaver somniferum), which was considered one of the most important drugs of the region for its analgesic and sedative properties (Arnott, 1996).

In addition to ancient tablets and in much greater quantity, surviving pottery, statues, and frescoes often depicted plants important to  ancient Greek cultural practices. Images of Olympic winners and revered heroes on clay pottery were depicted receiving or wearing a crown of bay laurel or olive leaves, symbolizing victory, power, and glory. Life-size marble ancient gods and goddesses, which can be visited in museums around the world, can also be found wearing, holding, or standing beside a sacred plant ally. An ancient fresco (buried intact after a volcanic eruption in ca. 1,600 BCE) on the island of Santorini illustrates women picking the bright threads from wild crocuses. Saffron (Crocus sativus), plucked by hands across millennia for use in foods, medicines, and dyes, is believed to have been associated with a Minoan goddess of medicine, presumably pictured in the fresco. High in analgesic crocin and immune-boosting antioxidants, saffron was associated with fertility, marriage, and initiation rites, and had stimulant and aphrodisiac properties (Ferrence, 2004). The herb was later included in an ancient Roman recipe with pepper, ginger, and hyssop to ward off the plague (Heilmeyer, 2007). 

Due to Greece’s location on the Aegean, Ionian, and Mediterranean seas, the Greeks characteristically took to the “wine-dark sea” for trade and new locations for settlements over the centuries (Homer, ca. 750 BCE/1995). Ancient amphora, large jugs used for transport and storage, have been found to have remnants of “Lamiaceae herbs (mint, rosemary, thyme, oregano, sage), juniper, and terebinth/mastic (genus Pistacia), [as well as] the presence of pine (Pinus), [legumes] (Fabaceae), [ginger] (Zingiberaceae), and [walnuts] (Juglandaceae),” indicating that trade was quite extensive, as some of these herbs are not native to Greece (Foley et al., 2012, p. 389).

Greece has a variety of herb species, including:

  • 26 species of St. John’s wort
  • 14 species of thyme
  • 11 species of sage
  • 11 species of tulip, with 3 endemic to Crete
  • 10 species of the genus Oreganum, including dittany, endemic to Crete
  • 5 different mountain teas, one endemic to Evia and one to Crete
  • 4 species of mint
  • 4 species of peony

For centuries, the early Greeks believed in the power of the gods and goddesses and built grand marble temples, held sacred ceremonies, sang powerful incantations, and made regular sacrificial offerings to beseech their goodwill. These divinities were “an explanation of something in nature; how, for instance, any and everything in the universe came into existence: men, animals, this or that tree or flower, the sun, the moon, the stars, storms, eruptions, earthquakes, all that is and all that happens. Thunder and lightning are caused when Zeus hurls his thunderbolt. A volcano erupts because a terrible creature is imprisoned in the mountain and every now and then struggles to get free. Myths are early science, the result of [human’s] first trying to explain what they saw around them” (Hamilton, 1942, p. 19). Each divinity was also associated with a sacred herb; the genus Artemisia was associated with Artemis, the strong goddess of the hunt, while the legendary musician and poet Orpheus was associated with fragrant hellebore (Helleborus cyclophyllus). Even mortals were associated with herbs; elecampane (Inula helenium) was named in honor of Helen of Troy. In Session 2, we will further explore the mythological stories of the herbs associated with divinities. 

During the Classical period of Greece (5th – 4th centuries BCE), investigations into the natural sciences and philosophical thought began to shift the healing mindset from the mystical to the scientific. Instead of seeking cures from the gods, who were often blamed for illness and to whom the sick begged for forgiveness, physicians began focusing on science to understand how the body worked. Sickness was no longer thought to be the result of a mortal transgression, but the result of imbalances in the body from a variety of factors, including nutrition, social class, environmental cleanliness, and the state of one’s mental health. This began the emergence of the practice of the four humours, for which Hippocrates (459-370 BCE) is credited with developing. 

INFLUENCES FROM EGYPT

Egypt, the land of grand pyramids, fascinating hieroglyphics, and magical divinities, is situated at the northern rim of the African continent, directly south of Greece, with its northern coast along the Mediterranean Sea. Surviving written evidence of the practice of herbal medicine there dates to as early as the 31st century BCE (McGovern et al., 2009), although the practice likely dates to a much earlier period when knowledge throughout Africa was only passed down orally. “Therefore… we have only a partial grasp of the true scope of Egyptian medical knowledge… [and] like their counterparts in the rest of Africa, the Egyptian priest-physicians often kept their best knowledge secret” (Finch, 2001, para. 5). While a tremendous amount of African traditional medicine has not been recorded, communities throughout the continent have practiced impressive medicine, treating issues ranging from traumatic wounds, smallpox, intestinal parasites, broken bones, and pregnancy complications. Many African cultures developed their own extensive materia medica, with the most well-known being the Egyptian.

The practice of medicine in Egypt is best known through Imhotep (ca. 27th century BCE), an Egyptian pharaoh counselor and pyramid architect who was revered as a god of healing and who would later influence the Greek god of healing Ascelpius. Imhotep is considered the author of the Edwin Smith Papyrus, one of the earliest known medical documents in the world, which dates to 1,600 BCE but is considered a copy of Imhotep’s original text from centuries earlier (van Middendorp et al., 2010). (The scroll was named after the 1800s antique collector Edwin Smith, considered “part scholar and part huckster” (Mukherjee, 2010, p. 39), who later also found the Ebers Papyrus, named after Georg Ebers, to whom he sold it). A second discovered papyrus (although not credited to Imhotep), The Ebers Papyrus, dating to circa 1,500 BCE, includes more than 700 remedies for a variety of common ailments, from animal bites to diabetes, using herbs and magical incantations, a signature Egyptian ritual. The existence of both scrolls has been undoubtedly influential throughout history, although it is not known what knowledge was shared with the Greeks at that time.

In ancient times, it was not uncommon for men to travel by sea and land for trade, war, or scholarly pursuits. Most notably, in 331 BCE, Alexander the Great (336-323 BCE), the king of Macedonia who would conquer a vast empire stretching to India, founded the city of Alexandria in Egypt. Previously a small fishing port, Alexandria would become a major metropolis, where for centuries, “western spice traders met with African and Asian spice merchants. Exotic spices such as cinnamon, cassia, black pepper, ginger, and many others traded hands from Arabs to Greeks… [with] spices coming by caravan from India, China and Southeast Asia, [as well as] via overland caravans coming through modern-day Jordan, Israel, Syria and Turkey into Europe” (Mundigler, n.d., para. 2). The city would also become the capital city of knowledge with the Library of Alexandria, considered the largest of its time, claiming a vast collection of scrolls from across the ancient world and visited by countless scholars from Greece, Egypt, Assyria, and Persia (Haughton, 2011).

For hundreds, if not thousands, of years the Greeks revered Egyptian customs, and in some sources, they considered Egyptian physicians to be descendants of the gods. The Greek historian Herodotus (484-420 BCE) in The Histories impressively notes that unlike Greek doctors who were generalists, in Egypt “the practice of medicine is so specialized among them that each physician is a healer of one disease and no more” (Herodotus, ca. 425 BCE/1998, 2.85.2). Egyptian medicine was complex and sophisticated, applying a variety of diagnostic methods, herbal medicine, physical manipulations, surgical techniques, and a magico-spiritual element that would be dismissed by later and current medical practices, but which “in the absence of anything better often the only support a physician could give the natural healing processes” (Abou El-soud, 2010, p. 85). The ancient Egyptians, however, did not completely separate medicine and religion; “for them, health and illness were manifestations of a person’s relationship with the universe around him, a universe that included not just people and animals but spirits and gods as well” (Zucconi, 2007, p. 27).

Due to the confluence of Egyptian and Greek thought over many centuries, the influence of Egyptian practices on Greek medicine has been inconclusive. Some scholars state an unequivocal connection: “Sumerian, Egyptian, Babylonian, and Assyrian Herbals formed the foundation of the Greek Herbals” (Budge, 1978, p. viii). And “the roots of Greek medicine were many and included ideas assimilated from Egypt and the Near East, particularly Babylonia” (Hemingway, 2004, para. 2). Other scholars have been more critical of these conclusions: “The question about the links between Egyptian and Greek medicine is far from being resolved. Some convergences can in fact be detected, but when you study them further, you will find that they reveal a loan, or a simple coincidence, or else a false interpretation of the sources. At the end of the 6th century BCE, Pharaonic medicine was outclassed and influenced little by little by Greek medicine” (Marganne, 1993, p. 35).

One specific and rare exception of exacting herbal uses identified by scholars is the use of garlic to foretell if a woman will give birth naturally or be able to conceive, a method described similarly in both ancient Egyptian and Greek texts: “Leave overnight a clove of garlic moistened … in the body (i.e., in the vagina). If you smell garlic on her breath, she will give birth (normally). If you cannot smell it, she will not give birth normally, and this will always be the case” (Jouanna, 2012, p. 5).

Despite this inconclusiveness, there is an abundance of evidence that people traded and learned from each other constantly. Dioscorides’ De Materia Medica and Theophrastus’ Enquiry Into Plants, both celebrated ancient Greek texts, frequently mention the Egyptian uses of herbs and other indicators that knowledge of these plants had no borders. One example includes the comparison of oil extraction from castor beans (Ricinus communis) between Greek and Egyptian customs. While the Greeks dried, pounded, and boiled the beans to extract the oil, the Egyptians grinded and pressed fresh beans (Dioscorides, ca. 60/2000). Much like today, the oil was used for skin diseases, uterine health, scars, earaches, and digestive worms. 

Egyptian names of plants were also included in Greek materia medica. Purple amaranth (Amaranthus blitum) is a vegetable which “Egyptians call it eclotoripam, others, riplam, the Latins, blitum, and the Dacians, bles” (Dioscorides, ca. 60/2000, 2.143). The reference to other languages helped develop a comprehensive materia medica helpful to other civilizations other than the Greeks. The world had become cosmopolitan, and the medical uses of herbs was a shared commodity. 

While in The Odyssey, Homer describes the use of a pharmakon, a special drug to ease grief that the legendary Helen of Troy adds to a crater of wine upon lamenting the absence of Odysseus (Homer, ca. 750 BCE/1995). This drug, which came from Egypt, was called nepenthe, a single herb or a combination of herbs that were pain-killing and soothing. Theophrastus also mentions this herb; in Egypt, “there the grain-bearing earth produces most drugs, many that are good, and many baneful. Among these he says was nepenthes, the famous drug which cures sorrow and passion, so that it causes forgetfulness and indifference to ills” (Theophrastus, ca. 300 BCE/1916, 9.15.1). Modern scholarship has not yet definitely determined what herb or herbs these were, but it is speculated to be opium.

As Greek physicians slowly began to shift their beliefs in the supernatural origin of healing starting in the 5th century BCE, they also began to lose interest in other cultures that continued to do so. They were no longer impressed by Egyptian medicine as their practices began to seem harsh (Jouanna, 2012, p. 14). With the rise of Hippocratic thought, no longer would physicians “need to resort to purifications and magic and all kinds of charlatanism” (Riddle, 2013, p.66). Their knowledge of the human body and the natural world was taking a different shape.

 

INFLUENCES FROM INDIA

With new trade routes extending to the Indus Valley established by Alexander the Great, the Greeks now had easier access to spices and herbs that expanded their apothecaries for culinary, medical, and religious purposes. “Frankincense, myrrh, cassia, balsam of Mecca, cinnamon and all other such plants” would become popular trading commodities, as well as nutmeg, clove, and pepper for their enhancement to foods (Theophrastus, ca. 300 BCE/1916, 4.4.13). These herbs had been used in Ayurveda as early as 4,000 BCE, written in the Rig Veda and Atharva Veda, the earliest Sanskrit writings. Some scholars suggest that the doshas of Ayurveda, pitta, vata, and kapha, were influential on the theory of humours, which had similar defining traits (Osborn, 2015).

Due to expanded trade routes that emerged in the region after Alexander the Great’s conquest, the local Indian populations integrated the written Greek materia medica with approximately 2,000 of their own plant medicines. The Indians, like the Egyptians, also adopted the theory of the four humours, establishing schools throughout South Asia for what would later be known as Unani medicine. (Unani is the Arabic word for Ionian, the ancient Greek tribe from where the theory originated (Ansari, 2013)). Today, both Ayurvedic and Unani medical universities are subsidized by the Indian government, “but whereas Ayurveda has enjoyed a phenomenal surge in popularity, Unani Medicine still lags behind in recognition, perhaps due to its minority Islamic associations” (Ansari, 2013, p. 7). 

HISTORICAL GREEK PHYSICIANS

“Where there is love for mankind, there is love for art of healing.” – Hippocrates

Hippocrates (460-370 BCE): Considered the “father of medicine,” his legacy, the Hippocratic Oath, continues to bind modern-day physicians to “first do no harm.” Hippocrates was also instrumental in advancing the theory of the four humours – blood, phlegm, yellow bile, and black bile – which helped ancient healers explain and treat disease more effectively. He was considered to be the 18th generation descendant of Asclepius, the Greek god of healing, and was considered “the first to banish magic and superstition from medicine” (Budge, 1978, p. 56). His extensive writings included physiological actions for 300 medicinal plants, and he and his students are credited with writing The Hippocratic Corpus, a collection of 70 medical works. 

Theophrastus (c. 370 – c. 287 BCE): Considered the “father of botany,” he wrote 227 books about trees, shrubs, flowers, fruits, and animals, including Enquiry into Plants (Historia Plantarum). This nine-volume text helped create a new frontier in scientific botanical terminology, and included plants from the known world, including Egypt and India. He was a pupil and friend of Aristotle, although unlike his teacher, Theophrastus believed that “the forests, fields, seas and farms were the cockpit of all ‘facts’ and the experts were the fishermen, gatherers of wild plants and their parts, and farmers” (Scarborough, 2006, p. 11). He eagerly sought to understand plant folklore, which provided him a wider range of information than the philosophical leanings toward which his teacher was more inclined. Fortunately, his writings capture some of the traditions and rituals of these early herbalists. 

Agnodice (4th century BCE): The first known female physician during a time when it was forbidden for women to practice medicine. After witnessing countless women dying as a result of refusal by male doctors to treat them (or women refusing to have men as their doctors), Agnodice dressed as a man and enrolled in medical school in Alexandria, Egypt. Her medical practice thrived among women who knew her secret until male authorities discovered her identity and put her on trial. A massive group of her female patients, including the wives of men who wanted her dead, defended her, decrying to her accusers: “You men are not spouses, but enemies, since you are condemning her who discovered health for us” (Oakes, 2001, p. 6). Their efforts were successful, and women were permitted to openly study and practice medicine – but only on each other. As her story is documented in only one surviving text, some scholars doubt the historical accuracy of it.

Pedanius Dioscorides (c. 40-90 CE): A Greek medical botanist who wrote De Materia Medica, a five- volume manuscript describing approximately 600 plants for more than 1,000 traditional medicines. It would be extensively referenced by physicians, botanists, and herbalists for the following 1,500 years, and greatly contribute to European and Western pharmacopoeia. Dioscorides served as a medical doctor with the Roman army and traveled extensively throughout Greece, Italy, Asia Minor, and modern-day France. The yam genus Dioscorea was named in his honor by a French botanist in the early 1700s. 

Galen (c. 131-200 CE): A prolific Greek writer who became a distinguished physician and surgeon in Rome and revived the theory of the four humours established by Hippocrates centuries earlier. He also studied in Alexandria and introduced new plant medicines not included in De Materia Medica, including “Uvae ursi folium, used as an uroantiseptic and a mild diuretic even in this day and age” (Petrovska, 2012, p. 3). Galen’s work includes De Simplicibus, a text regarding medicine and the uses and locality of plants. 

THE FOUR HUMORS

“Must I give way and room to your rash choler? Shall I be frighted when a madman stares?” – Shakespeare, Julius Caesar IV, 3

The theory of four humours, commonly credited to Hippocrates, was applied to diagnosing and treating patients based on the balance of blood, phlegm, yellow bile, and black bile. From the Greek word χυμός / chymos meaning “bodily fluid,” the four humours helped ancient healers explain disease by identifying which humour was in excess (kind), where in the body it was located (part), and how much was present (degree). Once these characteristics were identified, physicians applied the appropriate healing therapy, typically a combination of dietary and lifestyle changes, herbal medicines, purification (such as sweating, vomiting, purging, or bloodletting), or surgery. 

The four humours were further understood by recognizing the combination of energetics, elements, and temperament, readily relating to the characterizations still understood by herbalists today: the four natural elements – air (blood), water (phlegm), fire (yellow bile), and earth (black bile), and the four energetics – hot, cold, dry, and moist. Herbs that are considered hot are stimulants and adaptogens, which help stimulate metabolism and warm the body. Cold herbs, including anti-inflammatories and sedatives, slow down metabolism and remove heat from the body. Herbs that are wet help moisten and soothe the body, and include demulcents and nutritive tonics. Dry herbs include expectorants, astringents, and diuretics, which help remove excess dampness and phlegm.

This schema provided an explanation of health that helped standardize the ancient Greek medical practice and provide specific individual care. As is the case with herbalism today, this early medicine focused on determining how a disease manifested and the appropriate personalized treatment. These four humours were considered agents of metabolism, each corresponding to a specific ancient concept of vital fluids in the body and its effect on physical and mental health.

Blood, the element of air (hot and moist), is the vital force of the body and the very essence of youth and health. Rich in hemoglobin, it is responsible for the foundation of life and was often compared to sap in plants. This is the humour of regeneration and was supported by herbs that were either cooling and drying to help regulate blood, or herbs that were warming and moistening to nourish and increase blood flow. Its characterization is sanguine, meaning optimistic even in challenging situations, patient, balanced, and thoughtful. Blood-cooling herbs include agrimony (Agrimonia spp.), stinging nettle (Urtica dioica), and yarrow (Achillea millefolium), and blood-nourishing herbs include linden (Tilia spp.), chamomile (Matricaria chamomilla), and hawthorn (Crataegus spp.).

Phlegm, the element of water, is characterized as cold and moist and includes all clear fluids of the body, including mucus, plasma, saliva, and lymph. The phlegmatic temperament is generally slow, sensitive, sleepy, and unemotional, although when in balance, ensures a sense of serenity and good judgment. Herbs that support this imbalance include pungent, stimulating, and aromatic herbs, including thyme (Thymus vulgaris), hyssop (Hyssopus officinalis), fenugreek (Trigonella foenum-graecum), bay laurel (Laurus nobilis), and fir (Abies spp.).

Yellow Bile, the element of fire (hot and dry), corresponds to bilirubin in the liver, which helps flush out impurities and power healthy digestion. It reflects the principle of digestion and transformation. When not flushed out properly, it is characterized as choleric, meaning bad-tempered or irritable. Bitter herbs, which can cool and detoxify the body, are generally applied to help remove impurities. These include centaury (Centaurium erythraea), gentian (Gentiana lutea), and barberry (Berberis vulgaris). 

Black Bile, the element of earth (cold and dry), corresponds to platelets and clotting factors and has a retentive and solidifying force on metabolism and bone building. It is characterized by melancholy (from έλας / melas, “black” and χολή / kholé, “bile”) or the feeling of pensive sadness. Most prone of the humours to cause sickness when not in balance, black bile can be identified as the dried blood found in stool, very dark urine, or serious fevers caused by malaria, or more abstractly as constipation, bloating, and arthritis. A healthy balance ensured appreciation of the arts and thoughtful introspection. Nervines, carminatives, and antispasmodic herbs, including lemon balm (Melissa officinalis), rosemary (Salvia rosmarinus) and lavender (Lavandula angustifolia), provide an uplifting and balancing effect on this humour. As Hippocrates said, “Grief and fear, when lingering, provoke melancholia.”(Hippocrates, ca. 185 BCE/1931).

Understanding these concepts of the four humours helped ancient healers categorize sickness and identify the proper herbal remedy. This practice, later called Unani Tibb or Graeco-Arab medicine, became widely known throughout antiquity and refined in the Middle Ages by Arabian and Persian physicians. Still practiced today, it is considered “the reformed and scientific inheritance of Arabs and continuance of Grecian philosophies in the field of science and art of healing” (Ansari, 2013, p. 11). While the term was not used by Greeks to describe their own practices, it is worth mentioning as it demonstrates that medicine in the ancient world was widely shared and refined across cultures.

CONCLUSION

Spanning millennia, the history of herbal medicine in ancient Greece is enchanting, complex, and woven with influences. From the Mediterranean to the Nile to the Indus, plants knew no boundaries and their medicinal applications were shared freely, just as they are today. Our work as herbalists reflects this rich exchange of timeless herbal applications which helps us remember all the wisdom that came before us. 

In the next session, we will explore the fantastical myths of ancient Greece that have riveted audiences for thousands of years. These stories—full of passion, pain, victory, and defeat—did not separate the divine world from the natural world, and often featured the sacred origin or use of plants. Each of the ancient Greek gods and goddesses had their own plant allies and their mystical powers included an enchanting touch of herbal magic. Learning about these myths can ignite the sacred powers of these timeless plants and greatly enhance our experience with herbalism.
