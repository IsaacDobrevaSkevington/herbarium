Ancient Greek Herbalism: Session 3

INTRODUCTION

The magical essence of Greece is captured through the imagination, the heart, and the taste buds. For anyone who has ever visited, the country is rich in picturesque landscapes, freshly prepared foods, and welcoming people. The spice of the Greek life is this very combination, paired with the delightful feeling of being part of a story thousands of years old.

As we have explored in Sessions 1 and 2, the ancient Greeks studied the natural world and told fantastical tales about it, leaving behind a treasure trove of ideas, remedies, and myths. Their imaginations led them to closely examine plants in their natural habitat and to write extensively about their observations within nature. Notably, Theophrastus (ca. 370 BCE – 287 BCE), considered the “father of botany,” examined plants with a scientific and botanical lens in his book, Enquiry into Plants, a nine-volume text that included plants from the known world, including Egypt, India, and Arabia.

A few centuries later, Dioscorides (ca. 40 – 90 CE), a Greek medical botanist, documented the wide range of medicinal applications of approximately 600 plants from Greece and other parts of the ancient world in his book De Materia Medica, which would later become a celebrated reference for European and Western pharmacopeia. Amidst the hundreds of plants he writes about, Dioscorides, too, had his favorites, including sage, chamomile, willow, garlic, marshmallow, nettle, coriander, and parsley (Petrovska, 2012). These texts, along with others that have fortunately survived, are rich in descriptions that can help us imagine the ancient life and create our own practices for rituals and self-care. 

In this final Session of this series on ancient Greek herbal medicine, we will explore the historical applications of popular herbs written about in celebrated ancient texts, including by Theophrastus and Dioscorides. How were these herbs used thousands of years ago in ancient Greece? And can we apply them the same way today? The popular plants we will explore include olive, sage, chamomile, and mint.

Next, we will take a trip to the rich and varied landscape of the Greek countryside, where herbs endemic to Greece grow wild on the mountainsides. These plants have been treasured since ancient times and remind us of the power of nature to persevere through the harsh conditions of time. These native herbs include Greek oregano, mastic, Cretan dittany, and mountain tea.

POPULAR HERBS IN ANCIENT AND MODERN GREECE

As in modern Greece, herbs in ancient Greece played a significant role in both cultural and medical traditions. They were formulated into medicines, included in recipes, and woven into rituals. While the ancient Greeks had to contend with frequent snake bites and battle wounds from iron weapons, the healing properties of the herbs they employed can be applied to present day. Ancient writers describe these powerful plants and bring to light some of these timeless applications. While there are hundreds of plants in the Greek materia medica that were applied to everyday life, these are a select few that were cherished either for their medicinal applications or their sacred connotations. Let us now explore the vital role of these plants.

Olive (Olea europaea)

Ancient Greek: ἐλαία (pronunciation: elaía)

Modern Greek: ελιά (eliá)

Ancient History: The olive tree is considered the oldest cultivated plant on earth, with archaeological excavations estimating its use as far back as the 4th Millennium BCE (Heilmeyer, 2007; Goor, 1966). The tree has sacred connotations in numerous religious texts, including the Bible, Torah, and Koran, and many ancient writers describe its crucial role to the culture, health, and commerce of early civilizations in the Mediterranean and Levant regions. 

In Greece, the tree has stood as a symbol of divine peace ever since Athena, the goddess of wisdom, touched her hand upon the Acropolis in Athens and sprouted the very first one. The myth of her creating the olive tree during a contest with Poseidon, god of the seas, stands immortalized in the west pediment of the Parthenon temple, built in her honor in ca. 438 BCE. The tree became a representation of her divinity, strength, and courage throughout the ancient Greek world. Crowns of olive sprigs were bestowed upon victors of the Olympic games and during celebrations of victory, and branches were extended as signs of supplication to bring peace to a challenging situation and by religious pilgrims on their way to a sacred temple. To the Greeks, the olive tree was the quintessential representation of divine glory.

The tree was also immortalized in other myths. To ensure that no one would go hungry, Dionysus, god of wine and pleasure, crowned three sisters with a divine role: Oeno as goddess of wine, Spermo as goddess of wheat, and Elais as goddess of oil (Apollodorus, ca. 1st century BCE/1921). Together the trio created the staples of ancient Greek agriculture. The olive tree was also associated with the temple of Zeus in Epirus in northwestern Greece, where a dove from Phoenicia (primarily modern Lebanon) delivered an olive leaf. The tree and the temple became a symbol of health and healing. Later, in ancient Roman villages, an olive branch was hung above doors to drive away evil spirits (Goor, 1966).

Although the tree was never the most important crop of the ancient Greeks, it did produce a fruit that could be both cured as a food staple and pressed into liquid gold. Trade of olive oil was extensive throughout the region, despite being expensive and labor intensive, and had begun as early as the Minoan (ca. 3,000-1,100 BCE) and Mycenaean (ca. 1,600-1,200 BCE) eras. Large jars called amphoras were used to store and transport olive oil, either pure, as a preservative for other foods, or in perfumes. The practice of pressing olives for oil continues and is now a worldwide commodity.

Ancient Uses: While olives are generally eaten as a good source of healthy fats, the extraction of olive oil offers far more applications to health. In ancient Greece, as elsewhere, the oil was used both internally and externally. It was considered warming to the body, especially helpful in the winter to protect the skin from cold and damp temperatures. After hot baths, the Greeks coated their bodies with oil to collect the accumulated dirt and sweat, and then scraped it off with a strigil or stlengis, a curved bronze scraper (Cartwright, 2016). Before and after exercise, athletes were massaged with a mixture of oil and sand to exfoliate the skin (Elliott, 2007). It was applied as both soap and conditioner for skin and hair, and as a carrier oil in perfumes. For wounds, it was mixed into a paste with other ingredients to cleanse and heal infections and burns (Heilmeyer, 2007). For oral health, it was rubbed onto the gums to soothe inflammation and cleanse the mouth. 

In cultural and religious contexts, olive oil sparked the flame of the famed Olympic torch. Victorious male athletes at the Panathenaic Games, held every 4 years in Athens, were awarded the prize amphora filled with hundreds of gallons of olive oil extracted from a specific sacred grove of trees called moriai (Thomas & Sneed, 2018). In sanctuaries, oil was burned in lamps offered as votive offerings (Hornblower & Spawforth, 2005). The dead were washed and anointed with the oil before their final burial to both delay decay and perfume their bodies. In domestic contexts, the oil was burned as fuel for lighting, the wood burned for fires, and the tree resin applied to wounds (Heilmeyer, 2007).

Both Theophrastus and Dioscorides recommend using newly pressed olive oil, “for that which is kept above a year is useless, having become thick and viscous” (Theophrastus, ca. 300 BCE/1916, p. 4.2). And new “oil from unripe olives is the best to use for health” as it has a sweet smell (Dioscorides, ca. 60/2000, p. 34). It was beneficial “for the stomach because it is therapeutic for the bowels,” in the mouth to strengthen gums and teeth, and to dull strong medicines (Dioscorides, ca. 60/2000, p. 34). Old oil, on the other hand, because it was more viscous, was recommended as an antidote to poisons, drunk with equal parts barley water “taken immediately and vomited up again” (Dioscorides, ca. 60/2000, p. 34). It was helpful in expelling intestinal worms by drinking six glassfuls boiled with rue (Ruta graveolens). Theophrastus mentions that olive oil was effective topically as a carrier with marshmallow (Althaea officinalis), birthwort (Aristolochia clematitis), or germander (Teucrium chamaedrys) for body sores and headaches. 

Wild olive trees (Olea oleaster), which Dioscorides identifies as the Ethiopian olive tree, were also harvested, although their oil was more astringent and not as commonly used. Dioscorides mentions that this oil was effective for dry conditions, including psoriasis, dandruff, and hair loss, and “it keeps grey hair away for a long time from those who are rubbed with it daily” (Dioscorides, ca. 60/2000, p. 35).

While the oil enjoyed medicinal popularity, the fruit did have several applications. The olives themselves, “roasted and applied as a poultice… prevents gangrenous ulceration” and helped infected boils and skin tumors (Dioscorides, ca. 60/2000, p. 142). The liquid of the brine, like the oil, served as a mouthwash to strengthen gums and loose teeth. In foods, olives were pickled with other herbs, such as fennel, celery, rue, and spearmint with green olives, or anise and bay leaves with black olives (Heilmeyer, 2007). Imagine the delectable taste with a slice of freshly baked bread!

Finally, an interesting observation by Dioscorides describes how goat milk was more easily digestible and less disturbing to the intestines because the goats fed on olive and oak leaves, both of which are astringent and helpful for their digestion (Dioscorides, ca. 60/2000). He recognized that what the animals ate could affect how well humans digested their milk, and most likely their meat.

Modern Uses: While the ancient Greeks may not have known about monounsaturated fatty acids, they did know how to address many ailments with a single plant. Today, studies have shown the benefits of olive oil to support healthy cardiovascular function, strengthen bones, and improve brain function, as well as help prevent certain cancers (Alech & Le Galliard, 2017). Oleuropein, a polyphenol present in all parts of the olive tree, but found to be highest in the leaves, has been linked to lowering blood pressure and has “cardioprotective, anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, anti-cancer, anti-angiogenic and neuroprotective functions” (Sun et al., 2017).  The oil continues to be a nourishing and moisturizing ingredient in body care products and when poured generously on foods, greatly enhances the flavor and nutrition content.

Botany, Growing, & Harvest: The olive tree is native to the Mediterranean. It is resilient and long-living, with a gnarled trunk reminiscent of wise wrinkles. A member of the olive family Oleaceae, the trees thrive best in regions with rainy winters and hot, dry summers. The tree has silvery green leaves, small white flowers, and a strong root system that can thrive in both rich and rocky soils. New trees may take up to 8 years before producing their first harvest; fruit is generally produced every other year, but centuries-old trees can still produce delightful fruit. The olive fruit, which is harvested in autumn and early winter, cannot be consumed raw, and must be cured or pressed for oil. 

Sage (Salvia officinalis) and Greek Sage (Salvia fruticosa)

Ancient Greek: ἐλελίσφακος (elelísphakos)

Modern Greek: φασκόμηλο (faskómilo)

Ancient History: As the word itself means profoundly wise, the herb also bears its divine providence. The ancient Greeks, Egyptians, and Romans believed that sage embodied divine elements and protected against evil. It was considered a sacred herb associated with the god Zeus, who ruled over mortals and immortals alike. The plant, a favorite of Dioscorides, was a symbol of good health and fertility. The Latin botanical name Salvia is derived from the Latin word salvare, meaning “to heal.” A superstition that persisted well into the Middle Ages was that a person would have a long life as long as sage grew in their garden (Ashin, 1976).

As an indication of its cultural importance, sage is depicted in the “Blue Bird” fresco, dating to 1,450 BCE, at the Minoan Palace in Knossos, Crete. Three other plants are also depicted: roses (Rosa pulverulenta), irises (Iris unguicularis), and lilies (Pancratium lily). The palace was a thriving center of trade for hundreds of years and the locus of rule for the legendary King Minos, son of Zeus and Europa. The ancient Greeks extensively traded sage along with other popular Lamiaceae herbs, such as oregano, mint, rosemary, and thyme (Foley et al., 2012).

Ancient Uses: From enhancing mental abilities to increasing women’s fertility to addressing venomous snake bites, sage was employed for seemingly unrelated conditions. Generally, the herb was known as a diuretic, wound herb, and antitussive, strengthening the body and supporting respiratory health. It was also applied as a styptic to stop hemorrhaging and ease itching (Larkin, 2010).  Both the leaves and stems were used in tea. 

As a symbol of fertility and good health, sage tea was often used for women’s health to encourage impeded menstrual flow and strengthen the body for pregnancy. It was also made into a pessary by whom Dioscorides calls the “most wicked women” for use as an abortifacient (Dioscorides, ca. 60/2000, p. 408). Later, Pliny the Elder (ca. 24 – 79 CE), a Roman naturalist and writer, wrote that sage leaves placed on the body could help deliver a stillborn birth (Pliny the Elder, ca. 77/1855, 22.71).

Paired with wine, which was considered a therapeutic agent, sage became an easy-to-ingest drink (Jouanna, 2012). Greek herbalists cooked sage leaves in wine and kept it on hand for flatulence and digestive problems (Heilmeyer, 2007). Sage wine was recommended for kidney ailments, rheumatism, and coughs, and was used for ulcers and dysentery (infectious diarrhea) (Larkin, 2010). Applied with a hot cloth, sage could also soothe itchiness around the genitals. Dioscorides mentions that as an ointment with wax, sage pairs well with rosaceum, or rose oil, presumably from the same species depicted in the Blue Bird fresco (Dioscorides, ca. 60/2000; Tucker, 2004).

For oral health, sage was rubbed on the teeth as a toothbrush to whiten and cleanse the mouth. For hair care, sage tea was used to dye the hair black and provide a brilliant sheen (Heilmeyer, 2007; Larkin, 2010). In ceremony, the leaves were burnt to cleanse a room (Rivera et al., 1994). In culinary recipes, the herb was popular in sauces and was mixed with pomegranate juice to ease vomiting (Ashin, 1976).

Modern Uses: Sage tea, known in Greece as faskomilo, is widely popular in both Greek homes and restaurants, where it is enjoyed as a strengthening tonic. The herb contains a variety of essential oils, flavonoids, tannins, and phenolic acids with a wide array of beneficial properties. High in antioxidants, sage can protect against inflammation in the body, boost immunity, and support respiratory health (Hamidpour et al., 2014). It has antiseptic, digestive, and antidiabetic qualities, and can help the body more easily digest fats. Sage can also help lower high blood glucose levels and LDL cholesterol levels (Hamidpour et al., 2014). 

Scientific studies have shown that regular consumption of sage can improve memory and alleviate depression (Hamidpour et al., 2014). There are also indications that sage can assist with prevention and treatment of Alzheimer’s disease and dementia (Perry et al., 2003).

For women transitioning into menopause, cold sage tea is recommended to help regulate body temperature due to its heat-clearing properties. Sage leaves are commonly used for skin conditions and have shown to be effective against acne, eczema, and psoriasis; as a result, sage essential oil is a key ingredient in many pharmaceutical dermatological products (Chini, 2017). 

Sage, particularly white sage (Salvia apiana), has grown in popularity for use in smudging, a sacred practice in some Indigenous North American tribes. As a result, white sage has become endangered due to overuse, so it is highly recommended to burn other varieties of sage or other aromatic herbs that are not at risk. 

Botany, Growing, & Harvest: There are more than 900 species of sage growing worldwide, with herbal uses cited in many traditional healing practices, including Native American, Traditional Chinese Medicine, and Ayurveda. There are 22 species of sage endemic to Greece, including S. fruticosa, which is often distinguished by its three lobes. Both S. officinalis (common or garden sage) and S. fruticosa (Greek sage or Dalmatian sage) were written about interchangeably in ancient and medieval texts (Larkin, 2010). However, the famous quote “Why should a man die in whose garden grows sage?” likely refers to the S. fruticosa species (Cummins (Ed.), 1976/1480, p. 10).

Common sage, of which there are many varieties growing worldwide, can have white, pink, or purple blossoms that bloom in the late spring and early summer. The gray-green, oblong leaves are characteristically covered in fine hairs called trichomes, referring to the Greek word meaning “hair.” Greek sage is an evergreen shrub that grows best in hot and dry climates on rocky landscapes, making the hilly Greek countryside a prime setting. At least 50 percent of sage sold in the United states is S. fruticosa. The herb is easily cultivated and the leaves can be harvested throughout the growing season, although lightly in the first year to encourage growth.

Chamomile, Roman (Chamaemelum nobile, formerly Anthemis nobilis)

Ancient Greek: ἀνθεμίς (anthemís)

Modern Greek: χαμομήλι (chamomíli)

Ancient History: Chamomile is one of the oldest known plants used in herbalism. It was another favorite of Dioscorides and was popular among many ancient civilizations. Other ancient names included eranthemon (“a flower that blooms in spring”), the Roman malium, and the African astertiphe (Dioscorides, ca. 60/2000, p. 527). Dioscorides notes that drinking or bathing in the tea could induce abortion, a practice that both the Romans and Arabs adopted. The Latin word Matricaria, the genus name for German chamomile, may have derived from this usage, referring to mater, “mother,” and matrix, denoting the uterus (Petrovska, 2012). The modern Greek name chamomili refers to the older word chamaimelon, meaning “ground apple” or “low-growing apple” deriving from its association with the scent of apples. Chamomile was considered sacred to Apollo and Ra, the god of the sun in Greece and Egypt, respectively. 

Ancient Uses: Many ancient Greek physicians, including Hippocrates, Dioscorides, Galen, and Asclepiades, studied and wrote about the various beneficial properties of chamomile (Das, 2015). Described as similar in appearance to common rue (Ruta graveolens), chamomile —root, flower, and leaf —was described as having “a warming, relieving strength” (Dioscorides, ca. 60/2000, p. 526). Drinking or bathing in a decoction of all three parts was said to help expel kidney and urinary stones, and the tea was a common remedy for intestinal gas, jaundice, and liver ailments (Dioscorides, ca. 60/2000). The flowers and leaves were chewed for thrush (candida) and when pounded until fine, they were combined with rosaceum (rose oil), shaped into little balls, and administered as a suppository to ease fevers (Dioscorides, ca. 60/2000). To both fragrance the air and ease headaches, garlands of fresh or dried chamomile were hung or worn (Heilmeyer, 2007). Chamomile compresses were placed on infected and inflamed eyes, and the leaves were commonly used to aid in the healing of snakebites, wounds, stings, and burns (Heilmeyer, 2007). For skin and oral care, the tea was helpful as a face wash and mouthwash (Petrovska, 2012). 

Modern Uses: Chamomile, both Roman (Chamaemelum nobile) and German (Matricaria recutita) species, is widely known as a nervine, carminative, and vulnerary, supporting the nervous and digestive systems. The herb is considered an uplifting bitter aromatic, especially helpful when feeling irritable or impatient. Chamomile continues to support imbalances such as insomnia, menstrual cramps, gastrointestinal disorders, muscle spasms, wounds, rheumatic pain, and hemorrhoids, and essential oils of chamomile are used extensively in cosmetics and aromatherapy (Srivastava et al., 2010).

More than 1 million cups of chamomile tea are consumed daily (Srivastava et al., 2010). A study conducted in 2013 on herbs commonly used in villages in the Peloponnese found that chamomile was one of the most cited herbs used for wellness purposes as an infusion, compress, eye wash, steam, and mouthwash (Petrakou et al., 2019). In a village near Mt. Pelion in mid-eastern Greece, villagers often give a tablespoon of tea three times per day to soothe nursing babies (Brussell, 2004). 

Both Roman and German chamomile contain a variety of volatile oils, terpenes, and flavonoids responsible for their beneficial properties, and can be used interchangeably for a variety of uses. German chamomile (Matricaria recutita) is the species more commonly used in herbalism in the U.S., and unlike Roman chamomile, does not have an abortifacient action.

Botany, Growing, & Harvest: Roman chamomile is native to Europe and north Africa. It is a low-growing perennial that can be grown as a lawn substitute and is associated with the old English adage, “the more it is trodden, the more it will spread” (Johnson, 2015, para. 3). The white blossoms with yellow centers are bigger and the finely divided, linear leaves are thicker than those of German chamomile, which grows taller but does not spread as easily. If growing for harvest purposes, German chamomile is recommended as it will yield more blossoms. Harvest chamomile blossoms after the morning dew has dried and when in full bloom, typically throughout late summer and early autumn.

Mint (Peppermint: Mentha x piperita. Spearmint: Mentha spicata. Water mint: Mentha aquatica)

Ancient Greek: ἡδύοσμος (hedýosmos), μίνθα (míntha)

Modern Greek: δυόσμος (diósmos), μέντα (ménta)

Ancient History: In Greek mythology, the mint plant was sacred to both Demeter, goddess of the harvest, and Hades, god of the underworld. Before abducting Demeter’s daughter Persephone, Hades had been intimately involved with Minthe, a river nymph near Mount Minthi in the western Peloponnese. When Persephone, his new bride, learned about the ongoing affair (and perhaps when Minthe boasted about it), she became vengeful and angry and trampled on the water nymph, transforming her into the mint plant. The nymph became an herb easily trampled; to make up for this loss, Hades bestowed upon her a beautiful fragrance, so that when she was stepped upon, her beauty would arise in the aroma. Other versions of the myth describe Demeter, instead of her daughter, trampling on the nymph.

Both the modern and ancient Greek word for mint—hedyosmos or diosmos—translates to “sweet-smelling” or “fragrant.” 

Ancient Uses: Mint was a “well-known little herb” in ancient Greece (Dioscorides, ca. 60/2000, p. 411), whose very smell “reanimates the spirits” (Pliny the Elder, ca. 77/1855, 20.53). Dioscorides describes several species from the genus Mentha, which were generally interchangeable and were commonly used for gastrointestinal issues, bad breath, insomnia, and headaches (Petrovska, 2012). The herb was recommended in various herbal and culinary recipes: mixed with vinegar, it was an aphrodisiac and could kill intestinal worms; blended with pomegranate juice, it could allay vomiting and hiccups; and mixed with honey it could ease earaches (Dioscorides, ca. 60/2000). Alexander the Great (356-323 BCE), aware of the herb’s aphrodisiac effects, forbade his soldiers from having it so that they would not be distracted in battle (American Botanical Council, 2006). Mint was also used as a contraceptive, although it is unknown how it was administered to prevent pregnancy (Dioscorides, ca. 60/2000). Perhaps, as Pliny writes, that because of the herb’s ability to prevent milk from curdling and thickening, the herb could also, in this sense, prevent “the seminal fluids from obtaining the requisite consistency” for pregnancy (Pliny the Elder, ca. 77/1855, 20.53).

As a sacred herb, the ancient Greeks used mint and other aromatic herbs in funerary rituals to both honor Hades, who had bestowed its pleasing aroma, and to mask the smell of the decaying body (Rose, 2020). During the highly secretive Eleusinian Mysteries celebrated in honor of Demeter, a sacred drink called kykeon (meaning “to stir”) was made with barley, mint, and honey. It was believed that Demeter had concocted a puree of mint, flour, and water to help in her search for her daughter Persephone, not knowing that Hades had taken her to the underworld. The recipe had a critical role during these annual mysterious rites held at Demeter’s sanctuary in Eleusis, which included a simulation of Demeter’s harrowing journey to find her daughter (Heilmeyer, 2007). Unbeknownst to the ancient Greeks, the drink may have included ergot, a psychotropic fungus that naturally grows on barley, wheat, and rye (Dean, 2013). While it was forbidden for initiates, which included practically every important influencer in Greek antiquity, to speak or write about the Mysteries, it is not surprising to learn about this hallucinogenic component for such a mysterious and sacred ritual (Mark, 2012).

Kykeon was also popular among the working class as an energy drink (without the ergot, which is visible on the grains), who made a simpler blend of barley and water flavored with mint, pennyroyal, or thyme. In literature, Homer mentions a recipe that includes ground goat cheese and describes the sorceress Circe concocting the drink into an herbal potion (Homer, ca. 762 BCE/1898; ca. 750 BCE/1995). The drink was also common in ancient Rome, where herbs were blended to individual taste (Mark, 2012).

As an aromatic herb, mint was often included in perfumes with spikenard, iris, cistus, rose, myrtle, hyacinth, cinnamon, and narcissus. In ancient Athens, where certain herbs were designated to different body parts, its scent was popular especially for the arms (Grieve, 1931). In his chapter about aromas, Theophrastus (ca. 300 BCE/1916) documents the popularity and necessity of these different fragrances.

Modern Uses: The many different varieties of mint—42 species and hundreds of subspecies—are enjoyed throughout the world (Salehi et al., 2018). The herb’s major plant constituents include phenolic acids, volatile oils (menthol, menthone), tannins, triterpenes, flavonoids, and coumarins. The herb has a wide range of applications for the respiratory, nervous, and digestive systems, acting as a carminative, mild nervine, antispasmodic, cholagogue, and diaphoretic. It is especially helpful for sore throats, coughs, headaches, insomnia, dizziness, indigestion, and motion sickness (Justis, 2016). In Greece, both peppermint and spearmint are enjoyed as tea and included in many recipes, including in variations of tzatziki sauce, summer salads, and with meat dishes.

Botany, Growing, & Harvest: Greece has four native species of mint, which thrive in the rich soil and warm temperatures of the Mediterranean. Botanically, mint is a perennial herb in the Lamiaceae family recognizable by its square-shaped stems, opposite and serrated leaves, and purple, white, or pink tubular flowers. Harvesting is recommended just before flowering in late summer, and because of its prolific growth, the plant can be fully cut up to three times.  

HERBS NATIVE TO GREECE

To know the Greek landscape is to know the wild herbs that fragrance the air. With 6,500 species and subspecies of Greek flora, of which 1,300 are indigenous, the mountains, valleys, and seasides are abundant with living colors. The prime areas where many of these native plants grow include the island of Crete, the Peloponnese, and central Greece (Convention on Biological Diversity, 2016; Petrakou et al., 2019). We will explore four of these aromatic herbs: Greek oregano (Origanum vulgare), mastic (Pistacia lentiscus), Cretan dittany (Origanum dictamnus), and mountain tea (Sideritis spp.). 

Greek Oregano (Origanum vulgare) and Cretan oregano (Origanum onites)

Modern Greek: ρίγανη (rígani)

  • Greece has ten species of the genus Origanum, including Greek oregano, Cretan oregano, and dittany (Origanum dictamnus), which is endemic to Crete (discussed below). Among all oregano species, Greek oregano is considered “true oregano” and is the most pungent and strongest medicinally. The herb is cultivated and wildcrafted on mountainsides across the country.
  • The botanical name Origanum is derived from two Greek words meaning “delight of the mountain” or “mountain of happiness” (McNair, 1930; Uyterhoeven, 2014).
  • In mythology, Aphrodite, goddess of beauty, created oregano as a symbol of happiness. The ancient Greeks crowned newly married couples with garlands of oregano as a blessing for happiness together. Oregano also symbolized good luck and good health. It was often planted near homes to ward off evil spirits and when worn on the head during sleep, encouraged psychic dreams.
  • Scientific studies have demonstrated oregano’s potent antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, antibacterial, and antifungal properties. The herb contains high amounts of mono- and tri-terpene phenolic acids and flavonoids that support a strong immune system response (Association for the Advancement of Restorative Medicine, n.d.).

Mastic (Pistacia lentiscus)

Modern Greek: μαστίχα (mastíkha)

  • This evergreen shrub or tree in the Anacardiaceae family produces a resin from its bark that has been harvested for at least 2,500 years. The tree grows throughout the Mediterranean but is only cultivated for its sap on the island of Chios, where there are well-established and highly sustainable harvesting practices. There are several famed mastic villages on the island, intact since medieval times, which now includes the Chios Mastic Museum, opened in 2020. The resin produced there is exported to more than 45 countries (Bruni, 2019).
  • Several ancient healers, including Hippocrates, Dioscorides, and Theophrastus, wrote about its use for digestive problems, colds, bad breath, wounds, and blood conditions. Dioscorides also describes mastic as good “for disorders in the womb – gently warming, astringent, softening” and for clearing blemishes on the skin (Dioscorides, ca. 60/2000, p. 47).
  • Mastic contains powerful antioxidants and has anti-inflammatory, antibacterial, and antifungal properties. A current medical trial is exploring the efficacy of mastic on repairing ravaged nerves, as experienced by those with vision impairment, with possible implications for treating Alzheimer’s disease and stroke (Bruni, 2019; U.S. National Library of Medicine, 2018).
  • Mastic resin, also called mastikha or gum mastic, is included in many culinary recipes, chewing gum, liquor, and botanical skin care products. It has a slightly sweet, earthy taste with vanilla and pine undertones.

Cretan Dittany (Origanum dictamnus)

Modern Greek: δίκταμο (díktamo)

Cretan dialect: έρωντας (erontas, referring to the great “love” a gatherer would have for another to risk their life when harvesting on the mountainsides)

  • Endemic to the island of Crete, dittany has enjoyed a reputation of magical powers since antiquity. A cave in the Dicte mountain range was considered the birthplace of Zeus, and the Latin botanical name Dictamnus is related to this myth. In Virgil’s Aeneid, Venus (Aphrodite) plucked the herb from Crete to heal the battle wounds of her son, Aeneas.
  • Aristotle (384 – 322 BCE) wrote about its herbal action as an antidote to poison, as he observed wild goats eating it after being hit by poisoned hunting arrows (Heilmeyer, 2007). Dioscorides also mentions it as an antidotal medicine and describes it with pleasant-smelling leaves similar to sage (Dioscorides, ca. 60/2000). Pliny describes it as resembling pennyroyal (Mentha pulegium) with the highest quality only growing in Crete (Pliny the Elder, ca. 77/1855).
  • Dittany is also referred to as burning bush, gas plant, or fraxinella. Traditionally, it is enjoyed as a tea to recover from colds, ease headaches, and soothe stomach ailments. It was also used to facilitate childbirth, as a poultice for wounds, and chewed to improve oral health (Krigas et al., 2015).
  • Scientific studies indicate that dittany has antibacterial, antifungal, antispasmodic, and expectorant actions rich in volatile compounds (Krigas et al., 2015). It can still be found growing wild in rocky crevices throughout the island.

Mountain Tea (Sideritis spp.) 

Modern Greek: τσαϊ του βουνού (tsai tou vounou, “tea of the mountain”) and σιδηρίτις (siderítis) 

  • Greece has seventeen indigenous mountain tea species, including those found on Mt. Athos, Mt. Taygetos, Mt. Olympus, and Mt. Parnassus. The herb is also known as ironwort or shepherd’s tea. The Latin botanical name sideritis translates from the Greek to “he who is made of iron” or “he who has the iron” and may refer to the plant’s use for wounds caused by iron arrows and swords.
  • Traditionally, the tea is enjoyed as a strengthening tonic before or during sickness, especially for colds and respiratory ailments, and is used for digestive issues and anemia (Committee on Herbal Medicinal Products, 2016). It was often enjoyed by shepherds in the mountains while tending to their flocks. Hippocrates and Aristotle both wrote about its healing qualities, and Dioscorides mentions its use as a poultice “to close open cuts and sore wounds, and reduce inflammation” (Dioscorides, ca. 60/2000, p. 575).
  • Mountain tea has effective antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and antimicrobial properties that support the immune, respiratory, nervous, and digestive systems (Committee on Herbal Medicinal Products, 2016). While there still lacks well-established clinical studies demonstrating the efficacy of the herb to support these systems, traditional uses continue to be practiced and recognized. One study with mice has shown that drinking mountain tea can reduce anxiety and provide effective antioxidant protection for faster recovery from illness (Vasilopoulou et al., 2013).  
  • Due to unregulated overharvesting, the Sideritis genus is at risk. Purchasing the herb from certified sustainable growers is highly recommended.

CONCLUSION

Through myths and mountain herbs, the magic of ancient Greece continues to inspire timeless traditions. We have learned about popular and native herbs—olive, sage, chamomile, mint, Greek oregano, mastic, Cretan dittany, and mountain tea—written about in celebrated ancient texts and that continue to provide their traditional healing powers today. The rugged and magnificent landscape of Greece and the flavors and aromas of a thriving civilization can ignite in us a sense of nostalgia and remind us of the impressive perseverance of nature. May we always remember that the stories of the plants can be our stories, too.
