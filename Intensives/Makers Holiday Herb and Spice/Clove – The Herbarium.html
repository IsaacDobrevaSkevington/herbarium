Clove

60 minutes

Introduction

The spicy scent of clove is sure to evoke warm holiday memories—from spiced pies to baked ham. Clove’s pungency is so powerful that recipes typically call for very small amounts, otherwise this spice could overwhelm the entire dish. This pungency gives clove its highly aromatic and warming quality, which may explain its popularity in wintertime recipes.

Cloves are the dried immature flower buds of Syzygium aromaticum, an Indonesian evergreen tree. Buds are hand-picked while still green or reddish, before the crimson flowers open. The unopened petals of the immature flower buds give an appearance of a central spherical mound. Upon drying, the cloves turn brown and resemble small, dark nails, inspiring their common name “clove” from the Latin and French terms for nail, clavus and clou (Sumner, 2014).

Syzygium aromaticum is native to the Maluku Islands of Indonesia and was introduced to Europe as early as 634 A.D (Sumner, 2014). However, European imports did not begin to grow until the late 1300s, and in the following centuries the Portuguese and later the Dutch held monopolies on clove imports to Europe until the French successfully smuggled plants to other tropical colonies in the 18th century (Royal Botanic Gardens of Kew, n.d.). 

This spice has a long tradition of use in the East. In China, clove was used historically as a breath freshener by upper class Chinese society members during court ceremonies and featured in perfumes and incense (Ptak, 1993). In modern day Chinese medicine, clove is associated with the Kidney, Spleen, and stomach organ meridians and is valued for warming the interior and expelling cold. It is said to warm the middle burner (a concept in Chinese medicine that encompasses the functions of the Spleen and stomach) and direct qi downward, warming the Kidney and boosting yang. Chinese medicine practitioners have used clove in combination with cinnamon (Cinnamomum cassia) and morinda (Morinda officinalis) to address male impotence and clear vaginal discharge caused by Kidney yang deficiency (Tierra & Tierra, 1998). In medieval Japan, clove (called chōji) powder was used in battlefields to address maggots that appeared in rotting wounds (Goble, 2005). In traditional Tibetan medicine, clove (called li shi) is used as support for the blood vessels and nerves (Cardi, 2005). 

In Ayurvedic tradition, clove is understood to increase agni (digestive fire), providing appetite-stimulating and carminative effects, helping to support weak or sluggish digestion characterized by abdominal pain, gas, diarrhea, or lack of appetite. By settling excess vata and kapha, it can ease nausea and can soothe children’s stomachaches when used in combination with other masala chai type spices such as cinnamon (Cinnamomum verum), cardamom (Elettaria cardamomum), and ginger (Zingiber officinale) (Pole, 2006; Bove, 2001). 

Cloves are so high in volatile oils that high quality cloves can actually be pressed out with the edge of a knife or squeezed with the finger nail. The phytochemical vanillin is present in clove and can be extracted as a substitute for true vanilla. 

One of the most common uses of clove is for dental care. The volatile oil has potent analgesic and antimicrobial actions, making clove a popular choice to address toothaches, as a local anesthetic and antiseptic. For this it is commonly applied in the form of a tincture or highly diluted essential oil directly to the affected area. Clove tea or the diluted tincture can be used as a mouth rinse to help soothe inflammation and improve circulation in the gums. Sucking on whole cloves is a good way to freshen the breath after a meal.

In modern Western herbalism, clove is recognized as a warming and drying circulatory stimulant. Due to these qualities, as well as its antiviral, antibacterial, and expectorant actions, clove is often included in respiratory system formulas addressing colds and the flu, especially when mucous congestion is present in the lungs and sinuses. In addition, clove’s antispasmodic and antiasthmatic actions make it useful for addressing coughs, asthma, bronchitis, and pleurisy (Frawley & Lad, 1988; Pole 2006). Clove can also be found in throat sprays for sore throats, laryngitis, and tonsillitis, and is sometimes included in chest rubs, steams, inhalants, and liniments. 

From culinary and herbal use to creative crafts, clove offers powerhouse aroma, cozy warmth, and potent benefits! 

Safety: Note that only culinary amounts of clove should be used during pregnancy.

Culinary Uses

A highly prized spice in the culinary world, clove offers a unique blend of warmth, sweetness, and subtle bitterness that elevates a wide array of dishes. Its versatility shines in both sweet and savory applications, from enhancing the flavors of baked treats to adding depth to complex spice blends and hearty stews. When used whole, cloves can impart their distinct flavor to beverages like mulled wine, cider, and broths. Ground cloves, on the other hand, are a staple in many baked goods, lending their robust flavor to cakes, cookies, and various marinades. The bold and unmistakable taste of cloves makes them particularly popular in seasonal recipes. They also play a crucial role in renowned spice mixtures like garam masala (see recipe in the previous session) and the beloved pumpkin spice blend.

Spiced Cranberry Hot Toddy

Hot toddy recipes are wonderful additions to your winter herbal cocktail lineup. Whether you are planning a holiday party or simply relaxing by the fire with loved ones, this cranberry hot toddy is perfect to make—or share!

The ingredients feature warming herbs, like clove (Syzygium aromaticum), cinnamon (Cinnamomum spp.), and ginger (Zingiber officinale), which also provide digestive support. Digestive health is essential year-round, but it becomes especially valuable in winter when richer, heartier foods are more common. Enjoy the blend of flavor and wellness benefits in this seasonal treat!

Yield: 4 drinks

Ingredients

4 cups unsweetened cranberry juice
¼ cup granulated sugar or honey
1 tsp whole allspice (Pimenta dioica) berry
2 cinnamon (Cinnamomum spp.) bark sticks
1-inch piece fresh ginger (Zingiber officinale) rhizome, peeled and thinly sliced
½ teaspoon clove (Syzygium aromaticum) bud, or more or less depending on preference
2 whole star anise (Illicium verum) seeds, optional
¾ cup dark liquor (rum, whiskey, or bourbon)

Directions

      • Add cranberry juice, sugar, allspice, cinnamon sticks, ginger, cloves, and star anise to a large saucepan and bring to a boil. Immediately reduce to a simmer, stirring to dissolve sugar.
      • Cover and simmer on low for about 10 minutes. Reduce heat and keep warm until ready to serve.
      • Divide into individual mugs, stir in your desired amount of liquor, and enjoy warm!

Berbere

Berbere could be described as the National Spice of Ethiopia (and Eritrea), whose cuisines depend heavily on this rich, spicy, reddish-brown blend. Berbere is used to season meats, beans, and is sometimes mixed with oil and used as a dip for the spongy teff-based sourdough bread known as injera which accompanies meals.

Yield: Approximately 1 cup

Ingredients

½ cup red chile pepper (Capsicum annuum) fruit, powdered 
⅓ cup smoked paprika (Capsicum annuum) fruit, powdered
3 tablespoons coarse sea salt
1 tablespoon coriander (Coriandrum sativum) seed, powdered
1½ teaspoons ginger (Zingiber officinale) rhizome, powdered
1 teaspoon cardamom (Elettaria cardamomum) seed, powdered
1 teaspoon fenugreek (Trigonella foenum-graecum) seed, powdered
¾ teaspoon nutmeg (Myristica fragrans) seed, powdered
¾ teaspoon allspice (Pimenta dioica) berry, powdered
½ teaspoon clove (Syzygium aromaticum) bud, powdered

Directions

      • Mix all ingredients and store in an airtight container in the refrigerator.

To Use

      • Add to stews or lentil soups for a warm, aromatic kick.
      • Toss root vegetables or cauliflower in oil and berbere, then roast for a bold side dish.
      • Stir a spoonful into yogurt to create a dip for flatbreads, crackers, or vegetables.

Spiced Molasses Cookies

Perfect for the cooler months, these spiced molasses cookies are bursting with seasonal spices! Soft and chewy with a hint of molasses, they’re perfect for enjoying with a cozy cup of tea or coffee. Make a batch for holiday gatherings, or keep them on hand as a treat to share with friends and family. Sprinkle with a bit of sugar before baking for a touch of sparkle and sweetness.

Yield: 2 dozen cookies

Ingredients

½ cup plus 2 tbsp (1¼ sticks) unsalted butter or non-dairy alternative, softened
⅓ cup granulated sugar
½ cup brown sugar or coconut sugar
¼ cup molasses
¾ teaspoon sea salt
½ tablespoon cinnamon (Cinnamomum spp.) bark, powdered
1 teaspoon ginger (Zingiber officinale) rhizome, powdered
¼ teaspoon clove (Syzygium aromaticum) bud, powdered
¾ teaspoon baking soda
1 large egg
2 cups unbleached white flour or cup-for-cup gluten-free flour blend
Extra sugar for sprinkling

Directions

      • Preheat oven to 350 degrees F. Line two or three heavy-duty cookie sheets with parchment paper.
      • Using a stand mixer, cream together the butter, granulated sugar, and brown sugar on medium/low speed for about 3 minutes.
      • On low speed, add the molasses, salt, cinnamon, clove, ginger, and baking soda.
      • Add eggs one at a time at medium/low speed, beating until just combined. Scrape down the sides of the bowl.
      • Gradually add flour and mix on low speed until incorporated, being careful not to overmix.
      • Using a large cookie scoop, scoop dough and shape into balls. 
      • Roll the tops of the cookie dough balls in granulated sugar, and place them sugar-side-up 2 inches apart on the prepared cookie sheets. Press gently to flatten slightly.
      • If freezing, place the cookies in the freezer on the parchment-lined baking sheets for several hours or overnight.
      • Bake the cookies for 16-18 minutes, rotating the pans halfway through. Bake until the edges are set, but the centers are slightly underdone.
      • Remove from the oven and slide the cookies, parchment and all, onto a cooling rack.
      • Store cooled cookies in an airtight container for up to 3 days. 

Notes

      • While these cookies can be made with coconut sugar, the texture will be a little more flat and “rustic.”

Wellness Uses

Known for its powerful antiseptic, anti-inflammatory, and analgesic properties, clove has been a trusted wellness ally for centuries. It is especially helpful for easing sore throats and toothaches. Clove’s warming nature helps promote digestion and stimulate circulation, making it especially useful during the colder months when our bodies may need extra support. In this lesson, we explore how to harness the benefits of clove with two simple, yet effective, herbal preparations: Clove and Honey Throat Lozenges and Toothache Tea.

Clove and Honey Throat Lozenges

Making your own herbal cough drops requires a bit of time, but the results are worth it. This recipe combines the warming and expectorant properties of clove with the subtle bitterness of orange peel and the soothing, demulcent qualities of licorice root. Crafted to calm a dry, scratchy throat and ease a lingering cough, these drops are effective and naturally flavorful. They can be made with honey, which is easily accessible, or choose rice syrup for a vegan option.

Ingredients

2 teaspoon licorice (Glycyrrhiza glabra) root
1 teaspoon clove (Syzygium aromaticum) bud
1 teaspoon orange (Citrus spp.) peel
1½ cup water
½ cup granulated sugar
¼ cup honey or rice syrup
Powdered confectioner’s sugar or rice flour, optional for coating

Directions

      • Make a strong herbal decoction by placing the licorice root, cloves, orange peel, and water in a covered pot and lightly simmer for 20 to 40 minutes, until reduced by half (¾ cup). Monitor the pot closely and turn down heat if water starts to boil. Remove from heat and strain through a fine-mesh strainer into a heat-safe vessel. 
      • While herbs are decocting, place the silicone mold on a rimmed, heat-proof baking sheet and set aside.
      • Strain herbs by pouring the mixture through a fine-mesh strainer or several layers of cheesecloth over a glass measuring cup. Press the plant material (marc) to squeeze out every last drop of the decoction. Compost the strained herbs. 
      • In a 4-quart saucepan, clip a candy thermometer to the side of the pan. Combine ½ cup of herbal decoction, cane sugar, and honey in the pan and heat over medium-high heat. Continuously stir the mixture until the sugar is dissolved. 
      • Once the sugar is dissolved, allow the mixture to come to a boil. Occasionally stir the mixture, being sure to scrape the bottom of the pan well to prevent the sugar from burning. Just be sure to avoid scraping the sides of the pot as you don’t want to re-incorporate any crystalized pieces into your mixture as it will make your final product too brittle. Bringing the mixture to temperature can take a while. During this time, the mixture will expand quite a bit and become darker in color. However, once the mixture comes to temperature and gets to the hard crack stage, you have to move quickly, so keep a close eye on your candy thermometer and be sure your measuring cup and silicone molds are close by and ready to go! 
      • When the mixture reaches 250 degrees F, begin to stir constantly, stirring the bottom and avoiding the sides. When it reaches 302 degrees F, carefully pour the mixture into a glass measuring cup to make pouring easier. Pour the mixture into the silicone molds. Use your silicone spatula to help spread the mixture into the molds more easily. This will help save you time as you don’t have to pour the cooling mixture into the molds so carefully. 
      • Once the molds are filled, allow them to cool for several hours or overnight before unmolding. Immediately wash all supplies that have come into contact with the cough drops with hot, soapy water to make cleaning easier. Otherwise, you’ll need to soak the supplies for a couple of hours.
      • Once the cough drops have cooled, remove them from the molds by pressing on the back of the mold to pop the pieces out. 
      • Toss the cough drops in confectioner’s sugar or rice flour to help keep them from sticking to each other, or wrap each drop in parchment paper. 
      • Store in a single layer in an airtight labeled container. If you did not wrap each drop in parchment, place parchment or waxed paper between layers of cough drops to help prevent sticking.  
      • Store at room temperature for 6 months.
      • To use, suck on a cough drop anytime you experience a dry, scratchy throat or have a cough that won’t go away!

Toothache Tea

Need help with a toothache? Look no further than your kitchen cupboard! Clove (Syzygium aromaticum) is an analgesic herb that helps to ease pain and reduce inflammation. Clove also has antimicrobial properties, which may help reduce bacteria that are contributing to dental issues. Pair clove with ginger (Zingiber officinale) and peppermint (Mentha x piperita) for a powerful tea that will reduce swelling and pain.

Ingredients

¼ teaspoon clove (Syzygium aromaticum) bud, crushed
2-3 slices fresh ginger (Zingiber officinale) rhizome 
¼ teaspoon peppermint (Mentha x piperita) leaf 
1 cup hot water

Directions

      • Crush clove buds in a mortar and pestle, then scoop the crushed clove into a measuring spoon, and place it into a tea infuser or  small pot.
      • Peel the ginger and slice a few fresh pieces from the knob; place them into your infuser or pot.
      • Measure out peppermint and add it to the infuser or pot.
      • Pour hot water into the infuser and allow to steep for 10 minutes.
      • If using a pot, heat the water and allow to simmer covered for 10 minutes.
      • Pour into a cup and enjoy!

Clove-Studded Oranges (Pomanders)

This herbal craft offers a perfect way to relax and unwind, whether you’re sharing time with family and friends or taking a peaceful moment alone amid the holiday rush. Bring this delightful activity into your own celebrations for a touch of joy, calm, and festive aroma this season!

Ingredients

Fresh oranges (choose fruit free of blemishes to prevent spoilage of your pomander)
Whole cloves (per pomander: plan on using about 1 ounce of cloves for every orange depending on fruit size)
Ground spices, optional for preservation of pomander (per pomander: 1–2 tbsp mixed ground spices such as cinnamon, nutmeg, cardamom, and clove 
Powdered orris (Iris germanica) root, optional for preservation of pomander (per pomander: ½-1 tbsp)
Skewer, toothpick, or even a small nail to use to poke holes
Ribbon for hanging, optional

Directions

      • Poke holes for the cloves in the fruit using the skewer, toothpick, or nail. You can create a pattern with cloves such as stripes, spirals, or stars if you wish. Or simply place the cloves as close together as possible in order to cover the whole fruit. Better coverage of cloves will help to preserve your pomander.
      • Firmly press a clove into each hole that you have made.
      • If your fingertips get sore, from pressing cloves into the fruit, cover them with bandages or tape or use a thimble for protection.
      • When you have finished filling the fruit with cloves you can add a ribbon for hanging or place it somewhere special in your home to enjoy. Or you can roll your pomander in a mix of ground spices and orris root to help preserve your newly made clove pomander. Mix together 1–2 tablespoons of spices and ½–1 tablespoon of orris root. The orris root is optional, so if you don’t have any on hand just use ground spices. Roll the pomander in this mixture, completely covering the outside of the fruit.
      • Finish up by setting pomander aside to dry for a couple of weeks which will help it last even longer! To dry your pomander, simply keep it in the bowl with the spices and toss it with the spices every day or so while turning it to ensure that all sides of the pomander get exposed to the air.
      • Another option is to place the clove pomander and spices in a paper bag and shake the bag every day or so to keep the pomander covered with spices.
      • Tap your clove pomander occasionally—you will know that the pomander is dry when it makes a hollow sound.

Dried Orange and Clove Garland

Decorating for the holiday season with natural decor is a fun and budget-friendly way to bring warmth and charm to your home. A dried orange and clove garland is delightful all on its own, but can be enhanced with cinnamon sticks, pine, rosemary, thyme, and/or sage, if desired. This lovely project offers a beautiful burst of color that will fill your space with a cozy holiday aroma.

Ingredients

4–5 navel oranges 
Whole clove (Syzygium aromaticum) buds
Cinnamon (Cinnamomum spp.) bark sticks, optional
Pine, rosemary sprigs, thyme, or sage, optional
Twine or jute cord
Large needle (for threading)
Thin metal or wooden dowel

Directions

      • To create your garland, start by drying your orange slices. It’s best to do this step a day or two in advance so you can dive right into the project once you’re ready to create. Oranges can be dried in two ways:

Using a Dehydrator:

      • Slice oranges into ¼ inch slices. Pat down the orange slices between clean towels to remove some of the moisture. This will help reduce drying time. 
      • Place the slices in the dehydrator and add a few cloves to each slice by pressing the pointed end of the clove into the orange flesh. 
      • Dry at 140 degrees F for about 12 hours, or until the oranges are dry and slightly browned.

Using an Oven:

      • Preheat the oven to 200 degrees F and line a baking sheet with parchment paper. 
      • Slice oranges into ¼-inch slices and pat down the slices between clean towels to remove some of the moisture. This will help reduce drying time. 
      • Lay orange slices on the parchment-lined baking sheet.
      • Bake for 4-5 hours, turning slices over every 30 minutes or so to prevent the edges from curling. Check periodically for dryness. The oranges should turn a little brown and feel dry to the touch.
      • Let the slices cool completely before use.
      • Once the orange slices are dried and cooled, it’s time to gather the rest of your materials. For this demonstration, we will be using cloves, pine twigs, and rosemary sprigs. The combination of citrus, cinnamon, pine, and cloves creates an incredible holiday scent!

Assembling the Garland:

      • To assemble the garland, cut a long piece of twine or jute cord to the desired length of the garland. 
      • Use a wooden or metal dowel to poke one hole on each side of the orange of the top third of the orange slice, ensuring they are parallel. You’ll be threading twine through these holes later. 
      • Using a large needle, thread the cord through the flesh of each orange slice. You can choose to stack the slices closely or space them out evenly along the cord.
      • If you find threading through the orange flesh too cumbersome, another option is to thread the cord through the center of each orange and tie around the top of the slice.
      • After threading an orange slice, tie a cinnamon stick and a small pine twig (or rosemary sprig) onto the cord, if using. Alternate between the dried oranges and cinnamon-pine bundles to give your garland a balanced and varied appearance.
      • Once the garland is complete, you’ll want to hang it securely. To do this, tie a small loop at each end of the twine or jute cord. These loops make it easy to hang the garland on nails, hooks, or even drape it over a mantel or window frame.

Gift Giving

Cloves add warmth and wellness to your homemade gifts, not to mention bringing a unique herbal touch that’s sure to delight the recipient. In this section, you’ll learn to make two simple clove-inspired creations: Clove and Coconut Oil Pulling Chews—a unique, refreshing gift for oral health, and Clove-Scented Sachets for Drawers—aromatic sachets that freshen up spaces and naturally deter insects. These homemade gifts are perfect for the holidays or any time you want to share the cozy charm of cloves.

Clove and Coconut Oil Pulling Chews

Give the gift of a healthy mouth with these clove-infused oil pulling chews! Oil pulling chews are solid, serving-size chunks of coconut oil, and are a convenient way to pre-measure coconut oil for oil-pulling purposes. They are kept in the fridge or freezer to preserve their shape and texture. “Chew” is the most common term used to describe these nuggets, even though they are only “chewed” for a few moments before melting in the mouth. Many who find the initial sensation of oil pulling uncomfortable often find this is much more tolerable. Just pop one in your mouth and you’re oil pulling!

We’ve taken these oil pulling chews up a notch by infusing the coconut oil with clove (Syzygium aromaticum) bud and other herbs beneficial to oral health. Feel free to mix and match the suggested herbs as desired.  

To create these chews you will need a silicone mold with small cavities. If you make homemade chocolates or gummies, you may already have one on hand. Some silicone molds have cavities that hold ½ teaspoon of liquid, while others reach up to 1 tablespoon. If your mold has very small cavities, you’ll need more chews per oil pulling session. However, one bonus to having small chews is that it allows beginners to build up to a full dose, which is approximately 2-3 teaspoons per session. 

Ingredients

1 cup coconut oil 
¼ cup spearmint (Mentha spicata) leaf
2 tbsp cinnamon (Cinnamomum spp.) bark
1 tbsp clove (Syzygium aromaticum) bud

Directions

      • Place coconut oil and herbs in a sanitized heat-safe glass jar. 
      • Set jar in a saucepan or crockpot that has been filled with 2-3 inches of water and place a few jar lids under the jar to protect the base. Heat on very low heat for 4-8 hours (or more), making sure oil does not get warmer than 120-140 degrees F and refilling water as necessary as it evaporates. 
      • When measuring the temperature of the oil, be sure not to introduce water into the oil jar.
      • Remove jar from saucepan or crockpot and allow to cool briefly, then decant the oil by placing a cheesecloth-lined fine-mesh strainer in a large bowl with a spout. Pour the contents of the jar into the strainer. Gather the ends of the cheesecloth together and squeeze the remaining oil from the herb material into the bowl with clean, dry hands. Squeeze as hard as you can to get every last drop.
      • Pour into silicone molds. Place in the fridge or freezer until firm. 
      • Once solid, remove chews from the tray and store in a sealed container in the fridge or freezer. 
      • To use, place the equivalent of 1-2 teaspoons of chews in the mouth before brushing teeth. Swish around the mouth for up to 20 minutes. Spit oil into trash can to avoid any issues with coconut oil clogging the drain! Swish with water and brush teeth as usual. 
      • To gift these chews, place them in a jar or tin and add a label. Be sure to include instructions for use as well as a note to store in the fridge.

Clove-Scented Sachets For Drawers

Clove (Syzygium aromaticum) bud provides a warm, spicy fragrance that freshens up drawers and closets. Whole clove is particularly beneficial as it can maintain its scent for an extended period of time. Its natural insect-repelling properties may also be effective against moths, ants, and other pests, helping to deter these unwanted guests from clothing and linens. 

Use the following tutorial to create clove-scented sachets for yourself or your loved ones. To gift these sachets, pair them with a note on how to use them. Gift individual sachets, or place a few sachets in a jar for a sweet-smelling and practical present.

Ingredients

¼ cup clove (Syzygium aromaticum) bud
⅛ cup cinnamon (Cinnamomum spp.) bark chips (or sticks, broken into pieces)
⅛ cup orange (Citrus spp.) peel pieces
2 teaspoons orris (Iris germanica) root, powdered (to enhance and prolong the fragrance of your sachets)

Directions

      • Combine all the ingredients in a mixing bowl and stir until thoroughly blended.
      • Scoop ¼ cup of mixture into muslin bags and close tightly. Don’t overfill the bag, as you’ll want to leave enough room to close it easily.

Chai Syrup

This chai syrup recipe is a great way to get the benefits of this brew into daily life and makes a wonderful holiday gift option. Add to tea or drizzle over yogurt parfaits, pancakes, or ice cream.

Yield: 2 cups syrup

Ingredients

3 cinnamon (Cinnamomum vernum) sticks
1 star anise (Illicium verum) seed pod
20 clove (Syzygium aromaticum) buds
4-inch piece fresh ginger (Zingiber officinale) rhizome, chopped
3 cardamom (Eletteria cardomomum) pods, opened
¼ teaspoon nutmeg (Myristica fragrans) seed, powdered 
3 reishi (Ganoderma lucidum) mushroom strips
2 cups water
1 cup honey or cane sugar

Directions

      • Place the cinnamon sticks, star anise, clove, ginger, and cardamom seeds in a saucepan, and turn the heat to medium. 
      • Gently stir the spices as they begin to toast in the saucepan until fragrant. 
      • Once the spices have begun to release aromatics, add the ground nutmeg and reishi mushroom strips. 
      • Cover the herbs with 2 cups of cold water.
      • Place the lid on the saucepan. 
      • Turn the heat to low.
      • Allow the contents of the pan to slowly come to a simmer. If pot does not have an air vent in the lid, allow for ventilation by setting the lid slightly askew. We want the pot covered to collect as much of the aromatics as possible, but we also want evaporation to occur, reducing the percent of water as it simmers. Allowing small amounts of steam to escape slowly will do just this. 
      • Once the boil starts gently rolling, allow it to simmer until the liquid is reduced by half to just about 1 cup of decoction, remove from heat.
      • While the decoction is still warm, carefully strain the liquid through a fine-mesh strainer into a pint-sized jar. 
      • Add honey (or cane sugar) to the warm spiced liquid. 
      • Stir the contents of the jar until the honey (or sugar) has completely dissolved. 
      • Label the jar with the name of the contents and the date of bottling.
      • Store in the fridge, for up to 1 month.

The versatility of this warm, vibrant spice makes it a valuable addition to your spice drawer and herbal toolkit!
