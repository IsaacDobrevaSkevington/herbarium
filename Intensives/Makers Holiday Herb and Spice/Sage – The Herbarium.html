Sage

60 minutes

Introduction

Sage is one of the many herbs from the mint family that many think of as a kitchen herb; and while it is a delicious, savory addition to many dishes, sage also has a plethora of supportive health benefits! 

Wise sage is an herb of wisdom and its powerful properties have inspired much folklore. One medieval aphorism proclaimed, “Why should a man die if sage grows in his garden?” Its genus name, Salvia, comes from the Latin salvere meaning “to save,” which points to its traditional use as an herb that promotes long life and increased wisdom, like that of the “wise sage.” 

Salvia officinalis is the species that has been historically used in Western herbalism and is often referred to as garden sage, culinary sage, or simply, common sage. Salvia officinalis is recognisable by its oblong, soft furry leaves that are grayish-green and sometimes purplish in color. Its leaves grow in opposite pairs on a square stem, as is typical of all mint family plants. It has bluish-purple, irregular flowers are tubule and have upper and lower “lips,” and grow in whorls around the stems. 

Salvia officinalis is native to the Mediterranean region of southern Europe and coastal areas of northwestern Africa. It has a rich history and long tradition of use in this area. The ancient Greeks and Romans used sage as a meat preservative, and the Romans were known to use sage in foods to help better digest fatty foods. In France, sage was popularly grown for tea. In Egypt, sage has a history of use for fertility. Dioscorides, the father of pharmacognosy, noted sage as one of the most appreciated and important herbs of the time, using it on ulcers and wounds to stop bleeding, and as a tea for sore throats and hoarseness (Outlaw, 2016).

Herbalists use sage’s drying and warming properties for sore throats—in particular with conditions generating excessive fluid and mucus. As an anticatarrhal and expectorant, sage helps modulate the production and flow of mucus in the sinuses, nose, and throat, while also helping the body cough up excess mucus in the lungs. It is especially useful for drying out excess mucus in the sinuses, a runny nose, and post-nasal drip. 

In addition, as an anti-inflammatory and astringent herb, sage is used for canker sores, inflamed tonsils, laryngitis, and pharyngitis, and any inflamed condition of the throat or mouth. Sage can be prepared as a tea, a steam for respiratory congestion, and as a gargle or mouthwash to soothe oral and dental inflammation. Due to its antimicrobial properties, it is especially indicated when any of the above conditions are caused by infection.

In terms of digestion, sage is used to stimulate appetite and as a carminative to ease gas and digestion—perfect for a big holiday feast! Its bitterness also helps to stimulate digestive secretions, improving the digestion of fats and oils by encouraging the release of bile. 

Sage’s affinity for the head and the brain result in its effectiveness as a nootropic, cognition-enhancing herb helpful for memory and even for those with dementia and Alzheimer’s disease. In the 16th century, John Gerard noted that “Sage is singularly good for the head and brain, it quickeneth the senses and memory, strengtheneth the sinews, restoreth health to those that have the palsy, and taketh away shakey trembling of the members” (Grieve, 1971). 

Sage has much to offer in the kitchen and beyond, as we’ll explore below!

Safety: Note that only culinary amounts of sage should be used during pregnancy.

Culinary Uses

Sage is a versatile herb that adds a unique depth of flavor to a wide array of dishes. Its earthy, slightly peppery profile makes it a perfect companion for meats and hearty vegetable dishes, and it’s commonly used in stuffing, soups, and sauces. In this lesson, we explore a few creative recipes featuring sage. Soon, you’ll be savoring the crispy goodness of fried sage leaves, enhancing your meals with whipped sage-infused butter, and elevating your holiday gatherings with delicious herbal stuffing. Embrace the culinary potential of sage and transform your cooking with this beloved herb!

Fried Sage Leaves

Crispy fried sage leaves are a simple yet flavorful garnish that adds a pop of savory, aromatic crunch to a variety of dishes. These delicate leaves have a bright, earthy flavor and can be crumbled over pasta, sprinkled on soup, or enjoyed as a light snack. They also pair beautifully with roasted vegetables, especially pumpkin and squash. This quick recipe is a tasty way to use up extra sage and elevate your meals with an herbal touch.

Ingredients

Fresh whole sage (Salvia officinalis) leaves
Extra-virgin olive oil (enough to coat the bottom of the skillet)
Sea salt, for sprinkling

Directions

      • Pour enough extra-virgin olive oil to generously coat the bottom of a medium skillet and heat over medium until shimmering or the oil reaches about 330 degrees F. 
      • Add sage leaves in a single layer, avoiding overcrowding, and fry for about 20 seconds until they turn a deeper green and crisp up. Avoid frying too many at once, as you will need to move quickly to remove them from the pan once cooked. 
      • Remove the sage leaves with a slotted spoon and place them on a plate lined with a paper towel or cloth to absorb any excess oil. Sprinkling immediately with salt to taste.
      • Repeat with remaining sage leaves until all are crisped and seasoned.
      • Fried sage leaves are best enjoyed fresh but can be stored in an airtight container for a day or two at room temperature.

To Use

      • Crumble over pasta dishes
      • Use as a garnish for soups
      • Add to salads for a flavorful, crispy contrast
      • Enjoy as a standalone snack or as part of an appetizer or charcuterie board

Herbal Whipped Butter

This herb-filled butter is lip-smacking delicious, and perfect for adding herbs to any meal that calls for butter. You can make this ahead of time and store it in the fridge or freezer to have on hand when cooking or entertaining. Use it to cook in, spread it over squash before baking, dot the butter over veggies in a savory galette, or spread it on bread, biscuits, and cornbread—yum!

Yield: Approximately ½ cup

Ingredients

½ cup softened butter or butter substitute
2-4 cloves fresh garlic (Allium sativum), minced
1 heaping tablespoon fresh sage (Salvia officinalis) leaves, finely chopped
2 teaspoons fresh rosemary (Salvia rosmarinus) leaves, finely chopped
½ teaspoon salt
1-2 tablespoons honey

Directions

      • Crush and mince the garlic. Combine with salt. Exposing crushed garlic to air before combining with the butter will help activate a beneficial compound of garlic called allicin.
      • Whip the butter or butter substitute with a mixer or food processor. 
      • Add the sage, rosemary, minced garlic and salt, and honey to the butter. 
      • Whip the butter again until all the ingredients are incorporated. 
      • Store in a tightly-closed container in the fridge until ready for use. You can even shape the butter using small molds or as a rolled log to slice pieces from and make serving easy! 
      • This butter should last for up to a week properly stored in the refrigerator. It can be wrapped tightly and frozen for up to 6 months as well.

Herbs de Provence

The herbs of Provence in southeastern France (there are many!) are a mainstay in kitchens of that region and are used to flavor everything from cheeses to savory main dishes to desserts (Brennan, 1998).  This is one interpretation of the blend, but feel free to adjust the proportions to your liking or incorporate other regional herbs such as chervil (Anthriscus cerefolium), fennel (Foeniculum vulgare), mint (Mentha spp.), or even orange zest for a personal touch.

Yield: Approximately ½ cup

Ingredients

3 tablespoons oregano (Origanum vulgare) leaf
3 tablespoons thyme (Thymus vulgaris) aerial parts
2 tablespoon summer savory (Satureja hortensis) aerial parts
1 teaspoon basil (Ocimum basilicum) leaf
1 teaspoon tarragon (Artemisia dracunculus) leaf
1 teaspoon sage (Salvia officinalis) leaf
2 teaspoons lavender (Lavandula spp.) flower bud
1 teaspoon rosemary (Salvia rosmarinus) leaf
2 crushed bay (Laurus nobilis) leaves

Directions

      • Combine ingredients well in a small mixing bowl. 
      • Store in an air-tight container.
      • Use within 6 months.

To Use

      • Sprinkle over poultry, lamb, or fish before baking or grilling
      • Include in marinades for goat cheese
      • Add to hearty stews
      • Infuse in olive oil to use as a dipping oil or drizzle over dishes
      • Sprinkle on pizza or incorporate into bread dough for an herbal twist
      • Package in a pretty jar and give as a gift!

Herb Stuffing with Sage and Thyme

There’s nothing quite like the comforting aroma of freshly baked stuffing wafting through the kitchen. This herb stuffing recipe celebrates the robust flavors of fresh herbs, particularly the star of the show—sage. Known for its earthy and slightly pungent taste, sage (Salvia officinalis) brings a warm, aromatic quality that perfectly complements the sweetness of sautéed onions and celery. Combined with thyme and a medley of soft bread pieces, this stuffing offers a delightful texture and flavor that will elevate your holiday feast. Enjoy it hot from the oven for the best experience!

Ingredients

¾ cup minced onion
1½  cups chopped celery
1 cup unsalted butter (or dairy-free alternative)
9 cups soft bread pieces, ripped up into small pieces (approx. 1 inch cubes)
2 teaspoons salt
1½ teaspoons sage (Salvia officinalis) leaf, crushed
1 teaspoon thyme (Thymus vulgaris) aerial parts
½ teaspoon black pepper (Piper nigrum) fruit, ground

Directions

      • Preheat oven to 350 degrees F. 
      • Add onion, celery, and butter to a large skillet and sauté over medium heat until tender. 
      • Stir in one third of the bread pieces. 
      • Transfer the mixture to a large bowl.
      • Add sage, thyme, salt, pepper, and remaining bread, and toss well to coat. 
      • Place stuffing in an oiled casserole dish.
      • Bake for 45 minutes.
      • Remove from the oven when stuffing is heated through and crisp on top.
      • Serve immediately for the best texture and flavor.

Wellness Uses

Known for its warming and purifying properties, sage has long been used to soothe sore throats, clear congestion, and cleanse spaces. This section includes a tutorial for making a comforting sore throat tea recipe and a guide to creating a cleansing herb bundle, but you may also enjoy sipping a digestive tea made with sage after meals or inhaling its calming steam to help clear your mind and sinuses—there are many ways to embrace this herbal ally and incorporate sage’s benefits into your seasonal wellness routine.

Abuelita’s Sore Throat Tea

This special tea blend draws inspiration from a beloved family tradition. Here’s a note from our team member about this family recipe: “When I was growing up, my abuelita (grandmother) would prepare honey and lime juice in hot water anytime someone in the family had a hoarse or sore throat. It’s a traditional recipe used in Latin America and across the world. Early in my herbal journey, I learned sage is especially useful for a sore or irritated throat, and it seemed like a perfect addition to my abuelita’s preparation. Now it’s become one of my go-to’s for a sore throat, and one day, I’ll share it with my nietos (grandchildren)!” – Cristina Asensio Foley 

This blend is shared from Herbal Academy’s Botanical Teas Recipe Book.

Yield: One 1 cup serving

Ingredients

3-4 fresh sage (Salvia officinalis) leaves
1 tablespoon lime juice, freshly squeezed (about 1⁄2 a lime) 
1 tablespoon honey
1 cup water

Directions

      • Place sage and lime juice in a heat-safe container. 
      • Pour freshly boiled hot water over the sage and lime juice, cover, and steep for 5-10 minutes. 
      • Strain the infusion through a tea strainer and compost or discard plant material. 
      • Add honey.
      • Serve and enjoy!
      • Drink up to four servings per days to embrace this herbal ally and incorporate sage’s benefits into your seasonal wellness routine.

Smoke Cleansing with Sage

Smoke cleansing, or the practice of burning dried herbs to clear and purify a space, is a powerful way to promote positive energy and create a peaceful environment. White sage (Salvia apiana) leaf is ceremonially burned in Indigenous communities in regions it is native to, but this plant is now endangered due to overharvesting and the practice of smudging with white sage is most appropriately done within or with permission from its cultural traditions of origin. 

However, its European counterpart Salvia officinalis is a great herb for smoke cleansing rituals, as it is widely available and easy to grow. And while sage leaf has long been celebrated for its air purifying properties, other herbs in the mint family, such as lavender (Lavandula spp.) flower bud, thyme (Thymus vulgaris) leaf, and rosemary (Salvia rosmarinus) leaf, can also be used effectively in herb bundles for this purpose. 

These herb bundles can be easily made at home by tying together dried herbs with natural twine. As the herbs burn, they release fragrant smoke that is believed to uplift the atmosphere and enhance the energy of the room.

Materials

Fresh or dried herbs (like sage, lavender, rosemary, and/or thyme)
Natural twine or string
Scissors
Optional: a small bowl to catch ashes

Directions

      • If using fresh herbs, make sure they are not too wet.
      • Gather a handful of herbs and lay the stems side by side, aim for a bundle about 1-2 inches in diameter.
      • Use twine to tie one end of the herbs tightly. Wrap the twine around the bundle, moving towards the other end, and tie it off at the top.
      • Cut any extra twine or uneven herb ends with scissors.
      • If using fresh herbs, hang the bundle upside down in a dark, dry place until fully dried.

To Use

      • Light one end and let it smolder. Be careful and make sure the area is well-ventilated.
      • When finished, be sure to extinguish safely in a fireproof bowl or sand to ensure the bundle is completely out before storing or discarding.

Decorative Uses

Sage is not only a culinary delight but also a beautiful herb to incorporate into holiday decor, adding both aesthetic appeal and a delightful fragrance. Its soft green leaves and unique texture can be used to create festive wreaths, garlands, and centerpieces that enhance your seasonal celebrations. In this lesson, you’ll find tutorials for making both hanging sage bundles and botanical imprint ornaments, perfect for adding a handmade touch to your holiday decor.

Hanging Sage Bundle

These simple botanical bundles add charm and an herbal touch to your holiday decor. Hang them from a cabinet knob, hook, or even as part of a wreath for a festive and fragrant decoration. The earthy tones and aromas create a cozy atmosphere, perfect for the season!

Materials

Fresh sage sprigs (choose sage with longer stems for easy bundling)
Optional herbs for pairing: rosemary, thyme, lavender, or small pine branches for a holiday feel
Natural twine or string for tying
Scissors

Directions

      • Start by gathering a few sprigs of fresh sage as the main base for your bundle. Choose 2–3 complementary herbs, such as rosemary or lavender, to add color, fragrance, and contrast.
      • Layer the herbs with sage as the main element in the center, and add sprigs of rosemary or lavender along the sides. Add a small pine branch or a sprig of thyme for a more holiday-themed look. Arrange the herbs so that the tops align and the stems taper naturally.
      • Using natural twine or string, tightly wrap the stems about 1–2 inches from the top to secure them. Tie a knot, leaving a loop if you’d like to hang it from a hook or knob. For a rustic holiday touch, add a decorative knot or bow at the top.
      • Hang your sage herb bundle from a cabinet knob, hook, or even add it to a holiday wreath or garland for an added festive touch.
      • The herbs will shrink slightly as they dry, but the bundle will retain much of its shape and fragrance.

Botanical Imprint Ornaments with Sage

Bring a touch of nature into your home with these simple botanical imprint ornaments made from air-dry clay and sage leaves. This project is easy and perfect for all ages, making lovely decorations that can be displayed individually or hung together in a cluster or garland. Sage leaves have a wonderful shape and vein structure that looks beautiful imprinted into clay; however, feel free to have fun and experiment with other botanicals, as many will work for this project.

Materials

Air-dry clay 
Rolling pin 
Circle cookie cutter (or any round shape you prefer)
Fresh sage leaves (and/or other botanicals)
Jute twine or thin ribbon 
Acrylic paint in colors of your choice
Paintbrushes
Straw or wooden skewer (for making holes)
Silicone baking mat or parchment paper

Directions

      • Lay down a silicone baking mat or parchment paper to make working with the clay easier and to prevent sticking.
      • Knead a portion of the air-dry clay until it’s soft. Use the rolling pin to roll it out to about 1/4 inch thick. This thickness allows the ornaments to be sturdy while still drying relatively quickly.
      • If you prefer to add a soft, even color to your ornaments, instead of painting after drying, knead a small amount of acrylic paint into the clay until fully blended before rolling it out.
      • Gently place fresh sage leaves on the surface. You can arrange them however you like—overlapping or spaced apart for a varied look. 
      • Roll a pin over the leaves to achieve a uniform impression. 
      • Then, use a cookie cutter to cut shapes, making sure to strategically position the cutter to include the botanical imprints in your design. Carefully remove the leaves. 
      • Use a straw or wooden skewer to make a hole at the top of each circle for hanging later. This is best done right in the center at the top of the ornament.
      • If needed, dip your finger in the small cup of water and run it along the edges of the ornaments to smooth any rough spots or cracks.
      • Place the cut-out circles on a drying rack or a flat surface where air can circulate. Allow them to dry completely according to the manufacturer’s instructions (usually 24-48 hours).
      • Once the ornaments are fully dry, leave as-is or use a paintbrush to apply your chosen paint. If painting, add a few drops of water to your paint on a palette or in a small dish and mix thoroughly until it reaches a slightly diluted consistency. Apply paint to the entire ornament or just the imprints.
      • Cut lengths of jute twine or ribbon to your desired size for hanging and thread through the hole in the ornament, and pull the ends through to secure it. Adjust the length according to whether you want the ornaments to hang separately or in a cluster. 
      • If you’re making a set, gather an odd number of ornaments and stagger their lengths for visual interest when hanging.
      • Smaller ornaments can be tied to homemade gifts for an added botanical touch!

Gift Giving

With its lovely fragrance, rich flavor,  and calming properties, sage makes a thoughtful addition to homemade gifts that nourish the body and spirit. In this section, we’ll share how to create two unique bath soaks infused with sage to soothe the senses and rejuvenate the skin. Plus, we’ll share a flavorful spice blend featuring sage—a perfect gift for the culinary enthusiast in your life.

Herbal Bath Soak with Sage – Two Ways

Bath soaks are simple to create and make wonderful homemade gifts. By blending mineral-rich salts with sage and other botanicals, you can create a soothing and personalized bath experience. 

The two recipes below begin with a base of Epsom salt and sea salt. Epsom salt, known for its muscle-relaxing magnesium content, pairs perfectly with sea salt, which is naturally rich in skin-supporting minerals. 

Sage is the star in both of these recipes and is paired with other aromatic herbs from the mint family (peppermint, rosemary, lavender) as well as skin-supporting calendula to create two unique blends. These two recipes offer calming and revitalizing options, but feel free to mix and match the herbs to develop your own favorite blends.

Soothing Herbal Bath Soak

This blend combines sage with calming lavender and soothing calendula, offering a relaxing and skin-nourishing bath soak perfect for unwinding.

Yield: 2¼ cups

Ingredients

½ cup Epsom salt
½ cup sea salt 
¼ cup baking soda
½ cup calendula (Calendula officinalis) flower
¼ cup sage (Salvia officinalis) leaf
¼ cup lavender (Lavandula spp.) flower bud

Directions

      • Combine all ingredients in a large bowl and mix thoroughly.
      • Place mixture into a glass jar, label, and store in a cool, dark place for 12-18 months.
      • Use 1-2 handfuls of salt per bath. Before draining, place a sink/bath strainer over the drain to catch the herb material. Alternatively, place salts in a muslin bag and hang over the faucet while filling the bath.

Revitalizing Herbal Bath Soak

This refreshing blend combines sage with invigorating rosemary and peppermint, creating an energizing bath soak to restore and recharge.

Yield: 2¼ cups

Ingredients

½ cup Epsom salt
½ cup sea salt 
¼ cup baking soda
½ cup peppermint (Mentha x piperita) leaf
¼ cup sage (Salvia officinalis) leaf
¼ cup rosemary (Salvia rosmarinus) leaf, crushed

Directions

      • Combine all ingredients in a large bowl and mix thoroughly.
      • Place mixture into a glass jar, label, and store in a cool, dark place for 12-18 months.
      • Use 1-2 handfuls of salt per bath. Before draining, place a sink/bath strainer over the drain to catch the herb material. Because the rosemary leaves can be a bit “pokey” to the bather, it is suggested to place salts in a muslin bag and hang over the faucet while filling the bath.

Culinary Spice Blend

Packaged in a pretty jar with a decorative label, and you have a thoughtful, homemade sage-forward dry rub blend to give as a gift for herb-loving chefs!

Spicy Dry Rub

This dry rub offers a fantastic way to infuse meats and vegetables with bold flavors and makes a unique gift for the food lovers in your life! Especially those who love to grill. Use dried herbs in this recipe to ensure a shelf-stable blend.

Yield: ¾ cup

Ingredients

2 tablespoons oregano (Origanum vulgare) leaf 
2 tablespoons basil (Ocimum basilicum) leaf
2 tablespoons thyme (Thymus vulgaris) aerial parts
1 tablespoon sage (Salvia officinalis) leaf
1 tablespoon red pepper (Capsicum annuum) fruit, flakes 
1 teaspoon smoked paprika (Capsicum annuum) fruit, powdered
2 tablespoons raw/turbinado sugar
Coarse sea salt (to taste)
Freshly ground black pepper (Piper nigrum) fruit (to taste)

Directions

      • Mix all ingredients and store in an airtight container.
      • Wait for 3-4 days before using or gifting this blend to allow flavors to marry.

Conclusion

From savory dishes and thoughtful gifts to festive decor and wellness rituals, sage offers a multitude of ways to enhance your holiday traditions. With its deep roots in herbal tradition, sage supports digestion, respiratory health, and mental clarity—making it the perfect ally for this time of year. Whether you’re frying sage leaves for a crispy treat, creating herbal bath soaks, or crafting botanical ornaments, this timeless herb brings warmth, wisdom, and beauty to your seasonal rhythms.
