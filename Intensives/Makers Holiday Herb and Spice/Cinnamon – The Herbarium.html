Cinnamon

60 minutes

Introduction

Cinnamon, derived from the bark of Cinnamomum verum (Ceylon cinnamon) and Cinnamomum cassia (cassia cinnamon), is a versatile spice with a rich history and numerous applications. Cinnamon’s aromatic, pungent, and sweet taste makes it a popular ingredient in both culinary and herbal preparations. As we explore the uses of cinnamon, we’ll uncover how both species can elevate holiday traditions while promoting wellness throughout the year.

Used for thousands of years in Eastern and Western herbal traditions (Leung & Foster, 1996), cinnamon is traditionally prized for its warming properties in energetically cold conditions and its support for digestion. In ancient Egypt, cinnamon use dates back as far as 2000 BCE, where it served not only as a food flavoring but also as use in embalming practices (de la Forêt, 2017).

Cinnamon was incredibly valuable in ancient times, to the extent that Pliny the Elder noted it was prized by the Romans at a value fifteen times greater than silver by weight (de la Forêt, 2017). The high price of cinnamon made it a luxury item mainly accessible to the wealthy, driving the development of extensive trade routes and sparking conflicts over control of its supply.

Historically, cassia was used as a food preservative due to its phenol content, which inhibits the growth of spoilage-causing bacteria. In traditional Chinese medicine, it was used to warm the body and aid in resolving issues like diarrhea, vomiting, and indigestion.

Among cinnamon varieties, Cinnamomum cassia, known for its strong, spicy flavor, is one of the most widely used forms of cinnamon in North America, making up at least 50% of the cinnamon available on the market (Clare, 2020). Native to southern China, this evergreen tree can grow up to 49 feet tall and features grayish bark, yellow flowers, and black berries. Cinnamomum burmannii, or Indonesian cassia, is another variation that is commonly grown in Indonesia.

Cassia cinnamon contains several active constituents, including cinnamaldehyde, flavonoid derivatives, and phenolic compounds, which are responsible for its wellness-supporting properties. The spice is known for its antimicrobial, antifungal, and antioxidant effects. 

Harvested from a harder tree bark, Cinnamomum cassia has higher levels of coumarin compared to softer-bark varieties. While the coumarin levels in typical food amounts are not a major concern, some countries, like Germany, have restricted the use of Cinnamomum cassia due to these levels (Clare, 2020). For occasional use in small amounts to enjoy the flavor, C. cassia works well. However, if regularly taking cinnamon in larger doses—such as 1 to 2 grams per day—it’s better to choose Cinnamomum verum, which is considered a safer option (Clare, 2020).

Cinnamomum verum, commonly known as Ceylon cinnamon, is also called “true” cinnamon. It is native to Sri Lanka and southern India, though it’s now cultivated in other tropical regions, including Southeast Asia, Central America, Madagascar, and parts of Africa. This tropical evergreen tree can grow up to 59 feet and is harvested for its soft, aromatic bark, which is used to make cinnamon sticks, or “quills.” The inner bark, harvested every two years, is peeled, dried, and curled into quills, while the older, more flavorful bark is often used for ground cinnamon. Cinnamomum verum is considered superior to Cinnamomum cassia for herbal support due to its lower coumarin content and more delicate, sweet profile. This type has a soft, thin bark and is known for its sweet, delicate, and complex flavor. 

Ceylon cinnamon contains a variety of compounds, including tannins, cinnamaldehyde, eugenol, and coumarin. It’s regarded as a premium variety of cinnamon due to its mild flavor and softness compared to the more common cassia cinnamon. Fair-trade sourcing is recommended to support ethical production practices, ensuring farmers receive fair compensation.

In modern studies, Ceylon cinnamon has been researched for its potential benefits in managing blood glucose levels, particularly in post-meal glycemic responses, though results have been mixed, and more research is needed (Singh et al., 2021). 

Known for its anti-inflammatory, antibacterial, antifungal, and antioxidant properties, cinnamon is often used to support overall wellness, particularly in digestive health. Its astringent and carminative actions help reduce bloating, gas, and other digestive discomforts, while its circulatory and stimulant qualities can boost circulation and invigorate the body. The warming energy of cinnamon makes it especially useful in colder seasons or for individuals who tend to feel cold and sluggish.

There are many ways to incorporate cinnamon into your daily routine. As a decoction, simmering cinnamon sticks in water extracts its beneficial compounds, making a warming tea that can soothe digestion or support circulation. Cinnamon can also be taken as a fluid extract, tincture, or glycerite. In powdered form, it can be easily added to food or beverages to support digestion, blood sugar regulation, and overall well-being. Infused honey is another delightful way to use cinnamon, blending its sweetness with the warming spice for a soothing preparation.

No matter how you choose to incorporate cinnamon—whether in teas, extracts, or culinary creations—it provides a range of benefits, from its stimulating, warming properties to its antimicrobial and digestive support.

Safety: Note that cinnamon should not be used in pregnancy, in large doses for those on blood-thinning medications, and those using insulin should monitor blood sugar levels. Using culinary amounts is generally regarded as safe.

Culinary Uses

Cinnamon enhances both sweet and savory dishes, adding warmth and depth while supporting digestion and helping to regulate blood sugar levels. Incorporating cinnamon into culinary creations is an enjoyable way to harness its health benefits while enhancing the flavor of your meals. 

Whether sprinkled over oatmeal, blended into smoothies, or infused in beverages, cinnamon can easily fit into your daily routine. The following recipes offer delicious options for incorporating cinnamon into meals, snacks, and special treats.

Savory Spice Blends with Cinnamon

Cinnamon isn’t just for sweets! In herbalism, cinnamon is valued for its warming and circulatory benefits, supporting digestion and even immune health. Explore the warming, aromatic side of this spice in savory dishes with homemade spice blends, like garam masala and Chinese five spice. These blends are ideal for those who want to enjoy cinnamon’s flavor without adding sugar or sweetness—perfect for sauces, stews, marinades, and roasted vegetables.

Garam Masala

Garam masala is a Northern India dried spice blend that adds warmth and depth to meals and works beautifully as a seasoning for curries, soups, vegetables, and more. While traditional garam masala is made by toasting whole spices and grinding them, this quick version uses powdered herbs and can be made in just a few minutes. For an extra boost of flavor, try toasting the spice mix briefly in a dry pan before use. 

Yield: Approximately ¼ cup

Ingredients

1 tablespoon cumin (Cuminum cyminum) seed, powdered
2 teaspoons coriander (Coriandrum sativum) seed, powdered
2 teaspoons cardamom (Elettaria cardamomum) seed, powdered
1½ teaspoons cinnamon (Cinnamomum spp.) bark, powdered
1 teaspoon black pepper (Piper nigrum) fruit
½ teaspoon nutmeg (Myristica fragrans) seed, powdered
½ teaspoon clove (Syzygium aromaticum) bud, powdered
¼-½ teaspoon cayenne (Capsicum annuum) – optional, for extra heat

Directions

      • In a small bowl, mix all ingredients until well combined.
      • Store in an airtight container in a cool, dark place for up to 3 months.

To Use

Add a teaspoon or two to curries, stews, or lentils for depth and warmth. Sprinkle over roasted vegetables or stir into yogurt sauces for an aromatic twist.

Chinese Five Spice Powder

This origins of this fragrant blend are unknown, but its history of use in China extends back hundreds of years. It is theorized that is was created in partnership with traditional Chinese medicine to combine the five flavors —sweet, salty, sour, bitter, and pungent—making it a therapeutic and versatile seasoning blend. It can be used in stir-fries, braises, and marinades. It pairs beautifully with vegetables and adds a flavorful kick to soups and stews. 

Yield: Approximately ¼ cup

Ingredients

6 star anise (Illicium verum) seed pods
1½ teaspoons clove (Syzygium aromaticum) buds (or 1¼ teaspoons ground cloves)
1 cinnamon (Cinnamomum spp.) bark stick (about 3 inches) or 2 tablespoons ground cinnamon
2 tablespoons fennel (Foeniculum vulgare) seed
2 teaspoons Sichuan pepper (Zanthoxylum simulans) fruit (can sub with black peppercorns if needed, but the blend won’t have the same kick)

Directions

      • In a dry skillet over medium heat, toast the whole spices until they become fragrant, about 2-3 minutes.
      • Remove from heat and allow to cool. 
      • Once cooled, transfer the toasted spices to a spice grinder or coffee grinder and blend to a fine powder.
      • Store the mixture in a sealed, airtight jar. For optimal flavor, use within 6 months.

To Use

While this blend is not fiery in terms of heat, it does pack a strong, complex flavor, so start with a small amount and build from there. Sprinkle over roasted meats like duck, pork, or chicken,  incorporate it into marinades, and add to stir-fries. It’s even been known to be used as a secret ingredient in apple pie!

Cinnamon-Spiced Delights

All that said, cinnamon is a well-loved complement to sweet flavors, bringing its own sweetness balanced with pungency and spiciness to elevate a dish. From honey and apples to the humble bowl of oatmeal, cinnamon is the perfect spice companion.

Cinnamon Honey 

Delight in the warm spice of cinnamon-infused honey—pairing the two makes for one delicious sweet treat that is perfect for teas, toast, yogurt, and more! This easy-to-make herbal recipe requires just two ingredients and a little bit of time. 

Yield: Approximately ¾ cup

Ingredients 

4-5 cinnamon (Cinnamomum spp.) bark sticks
¾ cup raw honey

Directions 

      • Place cinnamon sticks in a mortar or wrap them in a cloth. 
      • Using a pestle in the mortar or a wooden spoon or kitchen mallet with the wrapped cinnamon, gently break up the cinnamon bark. Leave the pieces big enough that they are easy to strain out. 
      • In a small glass jar, add the crushed cinnamon sticks and pour honey over top. Cap jar and allow it to infuse for 2-4 weeks. 
      • Cinnamon is such a wonderfully strong herb that the burst of flavor can be tasted right away— so feel free to dip into your stash as you allow it to infuse. 
      • When the 2-4 weeks have passed, warm the jar of honey in a pan of hot water to thin a bit, then strain out the cinnamon sticks and reserve the honey. 
      • Use cinnamon honey to add a flavorful boost to anything you like, from oatmeal and chai lattes to fire cider, spiced marinades, sauces, and salad dressings! Cinnamon honey also makes a great glaze for roasted vegetables—drizzle over roasted carrots, sweet potatoes, or butternut squash, along with a touch of olive oil and sea salt.

Spiced Apple Cider with Orange, Ginger, and Cinnamon

This particular recipe calls for a stovetop preparation, but you could just as easily add the ingredients to a slow cooker and keep it on low throughout the day. Try whatever is easiest for your busy day! 

Yield: 4 drinks

Ingredients

4 cups apple cider
1 apple, cut into slices
1 orange, cut into slices
3 cinnamon (Cinnamomum spp.) bark sticks
2 slices fresh ginger (Zingiber officinale) rhizome
1 teaspoon whole, dried allspice (Pimenta dioica) berry

Directions

      • Add all the ingredients into a large pot. 
      • Bring to a simmer over medium-high heat.
      • Once simmering, cover the pot and allow to simmer for 10-15 minutes.
      • Note: If using a slow cooker preparation, simply add all the ingredients to the slow cooker and place on low. Your spiced apple cider will be ready to serve within the hour, but it can be kept on low or warm throughout the day.

Spiced Morning Oats

Simple and satisfying, this comforting recipe combines the cozy flavors of warming cinnamon, nutmeg, and vanilla. Customize with your favorite toppings like coconut flakes, seeds, nuts, and fresh berries to suit your taste. For an optional adaptogenic boost that supports balance and calm throughout your day, add ashwagandha (Withania somnifera) or astragalus (Astragalus mongholicus) root. 

Yield: 2 servings 

Ingredients

1½ cups dairy or non-dairy milk of choice
1 cup rolled oats
1 teaspoon cinnamon (Cinnamomum spp.) bark, powdered
½ teaspoon nutmeg (Myristica fragrans) seed, powdered
1 tablespoon maple syrup
½ teaspoon vanilla extract
Pinch of sea salt
Optional toppings: toasted coconut flakes, pumpkin seeds, fresh blueberries or raspberries, chopped pecans or walnuts
Optional add-in for adaptogen support: 1 teaspoon ashwagandha (Withania somnifera) root, powdered or astragalus (Astragalus mongholicus) root, powdered

Directions

      • In a small saucepan, combine the milk, rolled oats, cinnamon, nutmeg, vanilla extract, sea salt, and maple syrup.
      • Bring to a gentle simmer over medium heat, stirring occasionally.
      • Cook for about 5-7 minutes, stirring frequently, until the oats are soft and the milk is absorbed. Adjust the consistency by adding more milk or water if needed.
      • If using ashwagandha powder, stir it in during the last minute of cooking.
      • Once the oats are thickened to your liking, divide between two bowls.
      • Top with toasted coconut flakes, pumpkin seeds, berries, and/or chopped nuts.
      • Optionally, drizzle with additional maple syrup and serve warm.

Wellness Uses

Cinnamon is celebrated not only for its rich, warm flavor but also for its numerous wellness benefits. This versatile spice is revered for its ability to support many aspects of health, particularly in promoting digestive wellness and aiding in blood sugar balance.

Incorporating cinnamon into your routine can be as simple as enjoying a comforting cup of cinnamon tea or utilizing it in herbal preparations designed to help support healthy blood glucose levels. The following recipes offer two helpful ways to make use of this wonderful herbal ally. 

A Slice of Apple Pie Tea

Apple pie tea is a sweet, warm, and exquisite little treat. This tasty tea is a great way to use leftover pie apples or any type of apples before they turn. This recipe is from Herbal Academy’s Botanical Teas Recipe Book. 

Yield: Approximately two 1 cup servings

Ingredients

3 fresh apples, sliced
2 cups water
2 tablespoons honey (or sweetener of choice)
1 teaspoon cinnamon (Cinnamomum spp.) bark, powdered
1 pinch ginger (Zingiber officinale) rhizome, powdered
1 pinch clove (Syzygium aromaticum) bud, powdered
1 pinch nutmeg (Myristica fragrans) seed, powdered, for garnish (optional)

Directions

      • Place sliced apples in a saucepan with water. 
      • Bring to a boil, cover, then reduce the heat to a simmer until the apples are soft.
      • Remove from heat, strain mixture into a heat-safe container using a tea strainer, and compost or discard fruit material.
      • Add remaining ingredients and stir well. 
      • Serve with a sprinkle of nutmeg, if desired, and enjoy!.
      • Consume immediately or within 48 hours after straining, if refrigerated.
      • Drink up to four servings per day.

Cinnamon and Tulsi Blood Sugar Balance Vinegar

Cinnamon is a delicious and powerful blood sugar regulator by helping to improve insulin resistance and blood glucose. Tulsi has a hypoglycemic effect and studies have shown it can help to decrease blood glucose levels and manage other symptoms associated with diabetes. Since these herbs may interfere with blood glucose regulation, people with diabetes should only use this formula under the guidance of a qualified healthcare practitioner.

Ingredients

Apple cider vinegar
2-3 cinnamon (Cinnamomum spp.) bark sticks 
⅓ cup tulsi (Ocimum tenuiflorum) aerial parts

Directions

      • Place cinnamon sticks and tulsi into a sanitized ½ pint glass jar.
      • Cover herbs with apple cider vinegar, filling to the top.
      • Place lid securely and let infuse for 2 weeks.
      • Strain out the herbs and reserve the liquid.
      • Store liquid in glass jar and use 1-2 teaspoons in water before meals.
      • Pro tip: use a straw when drinking the ACV infusion, this will help protect your teeth enamel from the acidity.

Decorative Uses

Cinnamon is a beloved spice for holiday cooking and baking, but it’s also a charming addition to home decor. Its warm, inviting aroma and rich, earthy tones help to create a cozy atmosphere, especially during the colder months. Using cinnamon in decorative projects adds a touch of natural beauty while infusing your space with a comforting, spicy scent. The following projects offer a simple way to add herbal accents to your home with cinnamon.

Cinnamon Ornaments

The holiday season is a great time to explore creative ways to personalize your decorations, especially by incorporating a few herbs. One delightful and aromatic idea is to make cinnamon ornaments, and this fragrant squash cinnamon dough is the perfect base for creating your festive ornaments. It’s a fun, hands-on activity for the whole family to enjoy while filling your home with warmth and an inviting aroma.

Ingredients

2-2½ cups cinnamon (Cinnamomum spp.) bark, powdered
1-2 cups cooked squash such as pumpkin or butternut, sweet potato, or applesauce
4 tablespoons white school glue, optional—this helps to strengthen the ornaments once dry
Up to 20 drops total essential oil, optional—choose from citrus such as sweet orange (Citrus sinensis), grapefruit (Citrus x paradisi), or lemon (Citrus x limon), or choose lavender (Lavandula angustifolia), rosemary (Salvia rosmarinus), rose geranium (Pelargonium graveolens), or evergreens such as Siberian fir (Abies sibirica) and white pine (Pinus strobus)

Directions

      • Mix 2 cups of cinnamon into a bowl with 1 cup of cooked squash, sweet potato, or applesauce.
      • Add the glue or essential oils if you are using either of these.
      • Your cinnamon dough should be thick and pliable without being sticky. If it is too dry, add more of the cooked vegetable or applesauce to the mix. If it is too wet, add more ground cinnamon.

Cutting out shapes: 

      • Dust a countertop or table with cinnamon to work with your dough and keep it from sticking.
      • Using either a rolling pin or your hands, spread or pat the dough out flat. You will want it to be about ¼ inch thick.
      • Use cookie cutters to cut out shapes, make a hole with a drinking straw for hanging if desired, and set aside on a cookie sheet lined with wax paper to dry.

Making rolled beads: 

      • Dust your hands with ground cinnamon if desired. Working with small amounts of dough, roll the dough into balls, then poke a hole through the center and set aside to dry.

Cinnamon Vase

Elevate your floral arrangements by creating a charming cinnamon stick vase! This easy project combines the comforting scent of cinnamon with a rustic aesthetic, perfect for any season. Plus, it’s a fantastic way to use up cinnamon sticks that might be past their prime, allowing you to repurpose them into a decorative piece rather than discarding them.

If you want to reduce the expense or usage of cinnamon, consider alternating the cinnamon sticks with similar-diameter sticks collected from your yard or a nature walk. This adds variety and can also be more cost-effective.

Materials

Cinnamon sticks
Glass jar or can 
Hot glue gun
Twine
Scissors
Ribbon
Fresh or dried flowers

Directions

      • Glue the cinnamon sticks vertically around the jar or can, placing them snugly next to each other. Or, alternate the cinnamon sticks with similar-diameter sticks collected from your yard or a nature walk, if desired.  Press each stick firmly for a secure hold.
      • Wrap a piece of twine around the cinnamon sticks multiple times, tying it tightly to ensure everything stays together. 
      • Place fresh or dried flowers inside the jar. Use as place settings or incorporate into a centerpiece arrangement. 

Gift Giving

Cinnamon’s warm and inviting aroma makes it an ideal choice for holiday gifting. From aromatic body scrubs to fragrant potpourri kits, incorporating this beloved spice into your gift-giving is a wonderful way to spread holiday cheer while celebrating the comforting essence of this seasonal herb. You can also enhance the presentation of your wrapped gifts and jars by tying cinnamon sticks to packages, adding a lovely touch of seasonal charm.

Chai Body Scrub

This luxurious sugar scrub combines several winter chai spices with moisturizing oils for a warming, decadent body scrub. This can be placed in a decorative jar and tied with a ribbon to make a delightful gift. 

Ingredients

½ cup coconut oil
½ cup sweet almond or grapeseed oil
1 tsp cinnamon (Cinnamomum spp.) bark powder
1 tsp ginger (Zingiber officinale) rhizome powder
1 tsp nutmeg (Myristica fragrans) seed powder
2 cups cane sugar

Directions

      • Combine the oils and spices in a double boiler over low heat (or in a heat-safe bowl over a small saucepan of simmering water), and let infuse for 4 hours, stirring occasionally and monitoring the temperature with a candy thermometer to be certain it does not exceed 120-140 degrees F. Be careful not to let water splash into the bowl/jar! 
      • Remove from heat and strain the spice-infused oil through cheesecloth or a fine mesh strainer. Allow to cool.
      • In a bowl, combine the cooled spice-infused oil with the sugar and stir thoroughly to combine.
      • Transfer scrub to a clean, dry, sanitized jar. Close tightly with a lid. 
      • Label, and store in a glass jar in a cool, dark place for up to 6-12 months.
      • Use as an all-over body scrub during a bath or shower. The oil from the scrub will make for a slippery tub, so be careful and wipe down the tub after use. If sharing this scrub as a gift, be sure to include this safety information on the label. 

 

Apple Cinnamon Stovetop Potpourri Kit

This recipe calls for dried ingredients so that your blend will be shelf-stable and you can share it as a gift. However, if you plan to simmer this blend yourself immediately, you can save yourself some trouble and use fresh apple slices instead. 

Yield: one 12 fluid ounce glass jar

Ingredients

2 apples
4 star anise (Illicium verum) pods
¼ cup ginger (Zingiber officinale) rhizome
6 cinnamon (Cinnamomum spp.) bark sticks
⅓ cup clove (Syzygium aromaticum) bud

Directions

Dehydrate the Apples

      • Preheat the oven to 225 degrees F. 
      • While you’re waiting for the oven to come to temp, slice the apples very thinly and lay the slices on a lined baking sheet in a single layer. (To help prevent the apple slices from browning, dip them in a solution of 1 tablespoon vinegar or lemon juice per quart of water and then pat dry before placing them on the baking sheet.)
      • Bake for about 1 hour, then flip the slices and bake for another hour. Cooking time will vary based on your apple slices’ thickness, but they’re done when they have a leathery texture. 
      • If you have a food dehydrator, you can use that instead and follow the instructions in your manual.
      • Let apple slices cool completely before adding them to your potpourri jar. 

Blend the Potpourri

      • Because these are designed as gifts, presentation is key! It’s best to layer the ingredients directly in your jar in a way that’s visibly pleasing to the eye. The apple slices and cinnamon sticks take up the most space, and you can fill in the gaps with spices.
      • For a unique finishing touch, thread some twine or decorative ribbon through a dried apple slice and tie it around the outside of the jar. 

To Use

      • To use the potpourri blend, add the entire mix to a stockpot or slow cooker, cover with water, then simmer on and off for a few days. Turn the heat off any time the scent becomes overpowering or if you feel your home smells sufficiently delightful. After a few hours have passed, and the smell has faded, turn the heat back on for a fresh infusion. This blend will last 3-4 days and should be stored in the refrigerator overnight.
      • After you’ve extracted as much of the smell as possible, compost your spent herbs and spices and use any remaining water to give your houseplants a taste of apple pie, if desired.

Conclusion

Exploring the many uses of cinnamon can open up a realm of possibilities in your kitchen and wellness routine. This versatile spice elevates the flavors of main dishes, sweet treats, and festive beverages, all while imparting health benefits that support digestion and help manage blood sugar levels. Whether sipping a warming tea, crafting homemade decorations, or preparing thoughtful holiday gifts, cinnamon brings a comforting presence, rustic charm, and healthful benefits, making it a valuable companion throughout the year.
