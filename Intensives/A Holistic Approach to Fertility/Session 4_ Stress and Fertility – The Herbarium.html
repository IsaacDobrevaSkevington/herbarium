Session 4: Stress and Fertility

In this session, we will discuss how the journey to conceive can affect stress levels and natural strategies to support a person’s stress response and overall wellbeing.

Join Liane in the following video as she talks about how stress can impact fertility and shares approaches for supporting the body’s stress response. You can further explore these topics in the written lesson below the video.

Introduction

When working with people who are trying to conceive, it is important to support a healthy stress response as part of a holistic plan. People who are actively trying to get pregnant often worry that their stress could be impacting their ability to get pregnant, which can lead to feeling stressed about managing their stress! Stress is one of the most prominent reasons for the discontinuation of fertility treatments (Domar et al., 2010), and people trying to conceive on their own can feel alone and overwhelmed. Going through infertility and associated treatments can be one of the most stressful events in a person’s life.  

But the big question is does stress really impact our fertility? While there are several potential pathways by which stress may affect fertility that will be discussed in this session, the studies are not conclusive regarding their overall impact on fertility. Some studies suggest that stress-relief interventions may increase the chances of pregnancy (Rooney & Domar, 2016), but other studies suggest that stress is unlikely to reduce the chance of pregnancy among those undergoing fertility treatments (Boivin et al., 2011). Shifting the goals of a stress-reduction protocol toward overall health and wellbeing without implying that stress is negatively affecting fertility is likely a more productive way to frame the importance of stress management.  

The Stress Response and the Hormone Cascade

Stress is a natural part of life. The human nervous system evolved to trigger the “fight, flight, or freeze” response, originally to save our primitive ancestors from life-threatening situations. Today, this same mechanism can be activated in the face of an imminent physical danger (e.g., a car swerves in your lane of traffic) or as a result of a psychological threat (such as public speaking) or perceived threat (e.g., you are running late and worried you will miss your appointment).

The Stress Response

The body’s swift and intricate response to stress starts in the brain when the hypothalamus activates the sympathetic nervous system by sending a signal to the adrenal glands via the pituitary. This is the beginning of a feedback loop that occurs along what is often referred to as the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis. The adrenals are two tiny organs that sit one atop each kidney and serve as one of the regulators of energy, immunity, hormone balance, blood sugar balance, and much more. They produce and release hormones and neurotransmitters involved in the stress response.

When the body first detects a stressor, the brain stem releases norepinephrine, which sends a signal to the amygdala (a region in the brain responsible for the perception of emotions such as anger, fear, and sadness). The amygdala responds by sending a signal to the hypothalamus, activating the HPA axis. The hypothalamus passes this information on to the pituitary via corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH). The pituitary then releases adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH), which signals the adrenals to release stress hormones, including epinephrine (adrenaline), cortisol, and dehydroepiandrosterone (DHEA) (Waugh & Grant, 2014).  

Adrenaline causes the heart rate to increase, air passages to dilate, blood to move to the heart, brain, lungs, and muscles, mental alertness to increase, and nonessential functions (e.g., digestion, libido, reproduction, wound healing, immune system, and detoxification) to decrease. All of these reactions create the fight, flight, or freeze reactions and help us physically react in moments of danger. 

Cortisol is a steroid hormone that signals the liver to convert stored energy into sugar, and elevated cortisol means that sugar floods our bloodstream so we can have an immediate burst of energy. Cortisol also keeps our blood pressure elevated during a crisis (Sapolsky, 2004).

In most cases, when the perceived danger (or stressor) is gone, our system quiets back down and we quickly recover from the stress. 

The Stress Response and Reproductive Hormones

The HPA axis can affect the menstrual cycle through the hypothalamic-pituitary-ovarian (HPO) axis. When the adrenals release cortisol, in some cases the hypothalamus responds by decreasing the secretion of gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH). GnRH is responsible for triggering the pituitary to release follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) and luteinizing hormone (LH). Lower levels of FSH and LH may be associated with a decrease in the production of estrogen, progesterone, and testosterone (Acevedo-Rodriguez et al., 2018), all of which can affect the menstrual cycle and ovulation , but the degree of clinical significance will vary depending on the person and other compounding factors.

In Session 2 we discussed the HPO axis in more detail, and how stress plays a role in hypothalamic amenorrhea (HA) and luteal phase deficiency (LPD). While stress is a contributing factor to HA and LPD, it is important to remember there are other variables at play as well. Often LPD develops before a person progresses into hypothalamic amenorrhea, so managing stress can help balance HPO axis issues earlier when relevant.

Stress can also affect sleep cycles. Cortisol plays a critical role in sleep-wake cycles (also called the circadian rhythm) by stimulating wakefulness in the morning while gradually decreasing later in the day to allow melatonin to rise and help us sleep in the evening. Since rising cortisol levels cause a drop in melatonin, chronic elevation of cortisol may suppress melatonin and contribute to difficulty sleeping. As discussed in Session 1, disruptions in sleep can negatively impact fertility and early pregnancy outcomes (Mills & Kuohung, 2019).  

Our bodies are well equipped to handle occasional, short-term stress, but long-term stress can have a negative impact on our health. The good news is that lifestyle modifications and herbs can be very supportive when managing the body’s response to stress when trying to conceive. 

Herbs to Support a Healthy Stress Response

Adaptogens

Adaptogens are a group of herbs that help the body adapt to stress, mainly by regulating stress-related hormones and neurotransmitters (Panossian & Wikman, 2010). As herbalists David Winston and Steven Maimes outline in the book Adaptogens: Herbs for Strength, Stamina, and Stress Relief, adaptogens meet the following criteria:

  • Relatively nontoxic and safe in normal doses
  • Produce a nonspecific defense response to stress, having a beneficial effect on a range of body systems (not just one specific body system)
  • Have a normalizing effect on the body, so they can increase or decrease the body’s production of certain hormones depending on what is needed (also referred to as a modulating effect)
  • Work by affecting the stress response and the HPA axis (Winston & Maimes, 2019)

While there are several adaptogens to choose from, the two that I find myself reaching for most often when working with people who are trying to conceive are eleuthero (Eleutherococcus senticosus) and reishi (Ganoderma lucidum). They are considered safe for use when pregnant (for more on herbal safety during pregnancy see Session 2 of this intensive), but I typically suggest discontinuing at a positive pregnancy test to err on the side of caution. As always, consider a person’s energetics and full health profile when selecting herbs. Adaptogens work best when taken long term for at least 3 months.

Eleuthero (Eleutherococcus senticosus): A slightly warming and semi-stimulating herb, eleuthero is one of the most researched adaptogens (Panossian & Wikman, 2010). Eleuthero is used to increase mental alertness and performance, improve concentration, increase energy and stamina, reduce stress and fatigue, and enhance immunity (Romm, 2010). It may reduce blood glucose levels and improve insulin sensitivity which can be beneficial when trying to conceive (Reilly, 2021). I find eleuthero in combination with lifestyle interventions particularly useful for people who show signs of perfectionistic tendencies that push them to overwork and overextend themselves.  

Although it is commonly recommended that people with hypertension avoid eleuthero, a 2014 meta-analysis found no evidence to support this contraindication (Schmidt et al., 2014). There may be a dose-dependent effect, with high doses (4.5-6 mL of tincture 2 times per day) raising blood pressure, but low to moderate doses may have a modulating effect (i.e., reduce blood pressure in people with hypertension but raise blood pressure in people with hypotension) (Groves, 2016).  

Reishi (Ganoderma lucidum): This mushroom has a long history of use in Chinese medicine where its name translates to “tree of life mushroom.” David Winston lists it as a possible adaptogen (Winston & Maimes, 2019), and its adaptogen-like actions help support the immune system, modulate the stress response, build energy, and calm nerves.

In Chinese medicine, reishi is often used to help support the shen. Shen can be described as a person’s mind or consciousness and emotional balance. Disturbances in shen can present as anxiety, insomnia, bad dreams, moodiness, confusion, irritability, and poor memory (Winston & Maimes, 2019). I find this warming and drying mushroom most helpful for supporting people who tend to feel anxious, nervous, forgetful, or have disturbed sleep.  

Caution is advised for people taking blood-thinning medication (Posadzki et al., 2013).   

Nervines

Nervines are tonics for the nervous system. They help support calmer feelings and can be used to enhance the effects of adaptogens. Like all herbs, each nervine has its own personality and should be chosen to support the individual person. The following five nervines are safe to use when trying to conceive and during pregnancy.

Lemon balm (Melissa officinalis): This mint family herb is revered by herbalists for its mood-elevating effects. It is calming but not sedating. I particularly like lemon balm for people who are experiencing a low mood or feel angry and irritable. Lemon balm works well as a tea or tincture and can be combined with the other nervines listed below. Its energetics are cool and dry. 

Milky oat (Avena sativa): Fresh milky oats are the oat grains harvested when they are in their milky stage, during which the oat grains release a white, milky exudate when squeezed. Fresh milky oat is nourishing and supportive to the nervous system. It helps soothe frayed nerves and irritability. I find this moistening and cooling herb is a good fit for people who may be overworked, burnt out, agitated, or feel like their “nerves are shattered.” For people trying to conceive and also decreasing or cutting out caffeine, nicotine, or alcohol, milky oat can be supportive as it may ease the side effects of drug withdrawal (Sinadinos, 2020). For this purpose it is often combined with passionflower (Passiflora incarnata) and skullcap (Scutellaria lateriflora).

Passionflower (Passiflora incarnata): A more sedating nervine, this cooling herb can be helpful for insomnia as well as easing anxiety and agitation. It can be particularly supportive for people who tend to overthink or worry, particularly if their worrying is getting in the way of sleep (Winston & Maimes, 2019).  

Chamomile (Matricaria chamomilla): Chamomile is sometimes overlooked as a common tea, but don’t underestimate this familiar herb. It is a powerful nervine and boasts a long list of herbal actions such as anti-inflammatory, antispasmodic, antiviral, antibacterial, anxiolytic, bitter, carminative, and sedative. A clinical study showed chamomile reduced emotional symptoms associated with PMS better than a non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug (NSAID) (Sharifi et al., 2014). I find chamomile helpful for PMS mood disturbances, anxiety, or irritability, particularly in people who have a “nervous stomach.” Its energetics are cool and dry.

Skullcap (Scutellaria lateriflora): This cooling herb is a gentle, mildly sedating nervine. I find skullcap especially supportive for people who are agitated, anxious, nervous, or feel overstimulated. The freshly dried herb loses its potency fairly quickly, but the tincture can last for a few years. Purchase only from reputable sources to avoid adulteration.  

While combining relaxing nervine herbs with adaptogens can have a positive effect, combining herbs with lifestyle interventions will have an even bigger impact in supporting a person through their fertility journey. 

Lifestyle Interventions to Support a Healthy Stress Response

Mindfulness and Meditation

Over the past 15 years, research has confirmed that mindfulness-based practices (like meditation) can help minimize stress and support overall health and wellness. 

In one study, participants in an 8-week mindfulness meditation program reported reductions in stress, and brain scans showed a decrease in density in the amygdala, an area of the brain associated with anxiety and stress (Hölzel et al., 2010). Meditation appears to offer a way to quickly flip the switch from stress (sympathetic nervous system activation) into relaxation (parasympathetic nervous system activation) and all the chemical, physical, hormonal, and metabolic changes that go along with these responses.

A clinical study evaluating the effect of a yoga- and meditation-based lifestyle intervention (YMLI) in couples with unexplained infertility showed a significant reduction in depression in the YMLI group (Tolahunase et al., 2018). 

There are many different ways to incorporate mindfulness and meditation into one’s daily life. Experimenting with different forms can help one find a sustainable practice. Here are a few examples:

  • Guided meditations on apps like Headspace or Insight Timer
  • Breathing exercises such as four-by-four breathing: inhale four counts, hold four counts, exhale four counts, hold four counts. Repeat 3-4 times.
  • Mindful walking: walk in silence, paying attention to all five senses (what do you see, hear, feel, smell, taste?)
  • Adult coloring
  • Prayer

Sleep

Getting a good night’s sleep is an important part of managing stress, and as discussed in Session 1, disruptions in sleep can negatively impact fertility. When stress levels are elevated, as they often are when people are trying to conceive, sleep can be affected. This often creates a vicious cycle; stress makes it difficult to fall asleep, and the inability to fall asleep may, in turn, increase feelings of stress.

Not getting enough sleep can lower mood (Grèzes et al., 2021) and impair a wide range of cognitive functions and alertness (Lo et al., 2016). Studies suggest we need at least 7 hours of sleep per night (Watson et al., 2015), but many people need more. 

The nervine herbs discussed previously are all good choices to help support healthy sleep, and alongside herbs healthy sleep habits can be effective in helping break a sleep disruption cycle. Here are a few simple tips to help:

  • Stick to a good sleep routine, going to bed and waking at approximately the same time throughout the week and weekend: keep this schedule 7 days a week—avoid sleeping in on weekends when possible! 
  • Get outside in the morning: just 10 minutes of natural sunlight in the morning will boost melatonin levels that evening (Mead, 2008). Melatonin is a hormone that helps us sleep.
  • Avoid large meals and snacks before bedtime: eating before bed and eating late at night may help you fall asleep initially, but when blood sugar crashes in the middle of the night, cortisol levels rise, and melatonin production diminishes. 
  • Avoid caffeine after noon, or entirely if you are sensitive to caffeine.
  • Limit alcohol to one serving in the evening, or eliminate. Consider swapping out your evening glass of wine with a cup of relaxing herbal tea.
  • Unplug yourself from electronic devices (blue light affects melatonin) at least 1 hour before bedtime, including smartphones, tablets, and the television.
  • Chill out in the evening with relaxing activities and dim lights. 
  • Exercise earlier in the day: while exercise is good for healthy sleep-wake cycles, try to avoid late-night exercise, which can be overly stimulating.
  • Keep the bedroom temperature between 60-65 degrees F.
  • Meditate, do breathing exercises, or read a calming book before bed to help quiet your mind.

Exercise

Exercise is an important part of a healthy lifestyle and can help manage stress (Jackson, 2013) and depression (Netz, 2017). But how much exercise is considered safe when trying to conceive and during pregnancy has been debated with studies showing mixed results. 

A recent meta-analysis concluded that being physically active more than 2.5 hours per week before an IVF cycle was associated with improved birth rates (Rao et al., 2018). However, another study including more than 2,000 IVF patients showed a 40% reduced chance of live birth for people exercising more than 4 hours per week for the past 1-9 years (Morris et al., 2006). Another review study showed that heavy exercisers (those who exercised more than 60 minutes per day) had an increased risk of anovulation, while those who exercised vigorously for 30-60 minutes per day had a reduced risk of anovulatory infertility (Hakimi et al., 2017). As discussed in Session 2, excessive exercise can send a signal to the brain that it isn’t safe to reproduce and may affect ovulation in some people. 

The goal is to find the balance that is right for the individual. What is considered “moderate” exercise for one person may be “strenuous” for another. A person should be able to talk while exercising (this is called the “talk test”) and not feel exhausted or depleted after exercising. If a person is showing signs of extreme exercise, consider cutting back on the intensity and duration of workouts. Incorporate gentle yoga, Pilates, walking, tai chi, strength training, and other less aerobic forms of exercise. For someone who is currently not exercising, these activities can be a great way to safely ease into an exercise routine that can help support overall health and wellness. 

Conclusion

Deciding you are ready to have a baby but being unable to achieve a pregnancy can be one of life’s biggest stressors. The goal when supporting people on this journey is to help them navigate and move through this stage of life with as much ease and stability as possible. Providing tools to help manage the stress response can have a big impact on overall health and wellness. Herbs are wonderful at modulating the stress response and supporting mood. Lifestyle interventions not only help during this particularly difficult time but can provide life-long tools and healthy habits to support a person as they navigate through this time and wherever the journey to parenthood may lead them.
