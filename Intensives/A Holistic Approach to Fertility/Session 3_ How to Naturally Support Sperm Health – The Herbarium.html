Session 3: How to Naturally Support Sperm Health

Session 3: How to Naturally Support Sperm Health

In this session, we will explore the factors affecting sperm quality and its impact on fertility. We will cover herbs and natural strategies to support sperm parameters and discuss lifestyle changes that can impact sperm when trying to conceive.

Introduction

When couples are having trouble conceiving, the focus is often on egg quality, ovulation, and uterine health. But the data shows that issues related to sperm are a factor in 40-50% of infertility cases (Kumar & Singh, 2015). Sperm quality is declining globally, and the current “normal” ranges of semen parameters may not be optimal for achieving a pregnancy. The good news is that nutrition, supplements, herbs, and lifestyle changes have been shown to improve sperm quality and pregnancy rates.  

Global Trends in Sperm Parameters

Sperm health made headlines when a recent meta-analysis of studies from North America, Europe, Australia, and New Zealand reported a significant decline in sperm parameters. This meta-analysis showed that sperm concentration declined 52.4% (−1.4% per year) between 1973 and 2011. There was no sign of “leveling off” of the decline when looking at data from 1996–2011 (Levine et al., 2017). 

In 1940, the average sperm concentration was 113 million sperm per milliliter of semen (million/mL). In 1990, the average had fallen to 66 million/mL (Carlsen et al., 1992). 

The high proportion of people with sperm concentration below 40 million/mL was particularly concerning to Levine et al. Concentrations below this threshold are associated with a decreased monthly probability of conception (Bonde et al., 1998). 

The reason for the global decline in sperm parameters has not been proven, but sperm concentration and count have been associated with multiple environmental and lifestyle influences, both prenatally and in adult life. In particular, endocrine disruption from chemical exposures or maternal smoking may play a role in prenatal life, while lifestyle changes and exposure to pesticides may play a role in adult life (Levine et al., 2017). A decrease in overall nutrition and a trend toward poor diet may also play a role (Nassan et al., 2018).

Semen Analysis

A semen analysis is the most common test to evaluate sperm parameters and is considered the gold-standard assessment for the workup of sperm-related infertility (Jarow et al., 2011). Parameters that are reported include the total number of sperm (count), the number of sperm per milliliter of semen (concentration), sperm motion (motility), and sperm shape (morphology). Most labs report these numbers using values established by the World Health Organization (WHO), based on data collected and published in 2010 (Cooper et al., 2010).

Semen Analysis: Normal Versus Optimal

There is a wide range for what is considered normal. The current normal range represents people whose partner became pregnant within 12 months of stopping use of contraception. This includes 5% of the fertile people who provided data used in the calculation of the reference range up to 95% of the fertile people (Cooper et al., 2010). But there is a difference between falling within the normal range and what could be considered optimal for achieving pregnancy (Wang & Swerdloff, 2014). 

Let’s take a closer look at the lower end of the normal range, which is represented by the 5th percentile. The WHO found that 5% of the people who conceived within 12 months of stopping contraception had sperm concentrations that were 15 million/mL (Cooper et al., 2010). That means that 95% of people who conceived during the same timeframe had sperm concentrations higher than 15 million/mL.

Moving up the percentile chart we can see that 50% of the people who achieved pregnancy during the same timeframe had sperm concentrations that were 73 million/mL, and 95% of people who achieved pregnancy had sperm concentrations of 213 million/mL (Cooper et al., 2010). 

Many experts advocate that optimal sperm parameters are values in the 50th percentile or higher. 

A study looking at Intrauterine Insemination (IUI) procedures found that the best chance of pregnancy occurred when both motility and morphology values were above the 50th percentile (69% motility, 16% morphology) (Butcher et al., 2016). 

Of course, this data does not take into account the partner’s health or fertility. But it is a helpful guide when looking at sperm parameters and their impact on fertility.

Knowing where a person falls on the percentile chart above can help someone understand if their parameters are normal, but in the lower percentile, or optimal, in the 50th percentile or higher. 

Many herbs, nutrients, supplements, and lifestyle factors have been studied and found to safely and gently support sperm parameters. It takes approximately 64 days for spermatogenesis to occur (shorter than the previously suspected 70- to 80-day period) (Misell et al., 2006), so changes that a person starts today would affect sperm that is released 2 months later.   

Nutrition


Good nutrition is critical for optimal fertility. Before we discuss herbs or supplements to help support a holistic fertility plan, it’s important to build a strong foundation in nutrition. Proper nutrition provides many of the nutrients needed for optimal development of sperm. 

In a recent study, people who adhered to the Mediterranean diet had lower likelihood of having low sperm concentration, count, and motility (Karayiannis et al., 2017). Other diets similar to the Mediterranean diet that include high intakes of seafood, poultry, whole grains, legumes, skim milk, fruits, and vegetables have been consistently associated with better sperm parameters (Eslamian et al., 2012). 

While our goal should always be to optimize nutrition through our diet, it can be difficult to ensure adequate nutritional levels on a consistent basis. Taking high-quality supplements daily can help. 

Supplements

Multivitamin

Antioxidants have been shown to have a significant impact on sperm quality and pregnancy rates (Ross et al., 2010). As sperm are produced, a healthy amount of oxidation takes place. Antioxidants are needed to protect the sperm from oxidative damage. Oxidative damage is thought to be a contributing factor in up to 80% of infertility cases that are due to suboptimal sperm parameters (Bisht et al., 2017). 

The nutrients that have been studied and shown to be beneficial to prevent oxidative damage, improve sperm parameters, and increase pregnancy rates are vitamin C, vitamin E, zinc, folate, and selenium (Ross et al., 2010). Most high-quality multivitamins will have adequate levels of antioxidants and other vitamins necessary for optimal sperm production. 

Omega-3 Fatty Acids (Fish Oil)

Omega-3 fatty acids play an important role in sperm quality. Fish and fish oils are the most common source of the omega-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) docosahexaenoic acid (DHA) and eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) (Nassan et al., 2018). DHA and EPA have been shown to improve sperm membranes and protect them from oxidative damage. DHA in particular appears to play a large role in the formation of a sperm’s pointy cap, called the acrosome. This crucial cap contains enzymes responsible for piercing the egg, causing fertilization (Nassan et al., 2018). 

Higher levels of omega-3s have been associated with healthier sperm parameters (Safarinejad, 2011) and even short-term supplementation of DHA and EPA over 10 weeks improved sperm concentration, motility, morphology, and DNA fragmentation (Martínez-Soto et al., 2016). 

Omega-3-rich foods include cold-water fatty fish, eggs, meat, and organ meat. While it is possible to meet your omega-3 requirements through food alone (aim for 2-3 servings of cold-water fish per week), many of us will need to supplement to ensure adequate amounts. Supplement between 1.5-2 grams per day of omega-3 fatty acids (DHA and EPA) (Safarinejad, 2011). 

The plant source of omega-3, alpha-linolenic acid (ALA), has also been shown to improve sperm parameters. 75 grams per day of whole-shelled walnuts (about 18 walnuts) for 12 weeks resulted in improvements in sperm vitality, motility, and morphology compared with control subjects (Robbins et al., 2012). 

Coenzyme Q10

As an antioxidant, coenzyme Q10 (CoQ10) helps combat oxidation damage. It is also a critical component of mitochondrial energy production, which is essential in maintaining the efficient energy system of sperm, particularly sperm motility (Balercia et al., 2009). Sufficient energy in the form of adenosine triphosphate (ATP) is absolutely critical for sperm production and motility, and cells can only make ATP when they have enough CoQ10 (Crane, 2001).

People with lower CoQ10 levels tend to have lower sperm count and motility (Mancini et al., 1994) and numerous studies have shown supplementing for 3-6 months can improve sperm parameters (Alahmar et al., 2021; Balercia et al., 2009; Vishvkarma et al., 2020). CoQ10 appears to have a particular effect on sperm motility. One study showed a 19% increase in motility after 6 months of supplementation (Balercia et al., 2009), and a recent meta-analysis from three placebo-controlled randomized clinical trials concluded that “CoQ10 has a profound effect on sperm motility” (Vishvkarma et al., 2020, p. 1). 

When choosing a CoQ10 supplement, look for the more easily absorbed form ubiquinol. Most studies use 200 mg per day (Vishvkarma et al., 2020).  

Herbs to Support Sperm Quality

Green tea (Camellia sinensis) leaf: Antioxidants have been discussed multiple times throughout this intensive because they have such a positive impact on fertility. Green tea polyphenols, especially epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG), have antioxidant properties (Carlsen et al., 2010) that may improve the quality of sperm due to the ability of EGCG to decrease oxidative stress (Roychoudhury et al., 2017). An in vitro study showed that EGCG can increase sperm motility and viability  (De Amicis et al., 2012).

Incorporating 2-4 cups of organic green tea per day is a great way to support fertility. To make the perfect cup of green tea, use slightly below-boiling water and steep for no more than 3 minutes. While green tea has less caffeine than coffee, it does contain caffeine so you may want to avoid green tea in the late afternoon or evening depending on your sensitivity level. 

Ashwagandha (Withania somnifera) root: Ashwagandha, also discussed in Sessions 1 and 2 of this intensive, is an adaptogen with a history of use for reproductive health and thyroid function (Winston, 2019). Ashwagandha is also high in antioxidants (Ahmed et al., 2018).

Ashwagandha has been shown to increase semen volume, sperm concentration, count, and motility (Ahmad et al., 2010; Ambiye et al., 2013), increase serum testosterone and luteinizing hormone (LH) levels (Ambiye et al., 2013) and improve levels of vitamin A, vitamin C, and vitamin E (Mahdi et al., 2011). Consider using ashwagandha for people who are also looking for support with stress, anxiety, and sleep (Deshpande et al., 2020).

Ashwagandha is available as a powder, tincture, or in supplement form. It can be easily added to smoothies or golden milk. Typical dose is 1-3 grams per day. Ashwagandha is part of the nightshade family, so if you react negatively to nightshade plants, use with caution (most people with nightshade sensitivities can tolerate ashwagandha without issue).

Maca (Lepidium meyenii) root: Maca is a nutritive herb that has a long history of use to increase libido, sexual function, and fertility (Winston, 2019). Maca is rich in essential amino acids, iodine, iron, and magnesium (Romm, 2010). 

Maca root can have a variety of colors, and the color of the root has been found to influence its pharmacological and biological action. Yellow maca is the most common (about 60% of all maca harvested in Peru) and is the most widely used and researched. Red maca accounts for about 25% of the annual harvest and is known as the most effective type for menstrual and menopausal health (Peres et al., 2020). Black maca accounts for about 15% of the annual harvest and is often associated with improved sperm parameters and libido (Gonzales, 2012; Peres et al., 2020).  

One small study on healthy people who used black maca for 4 months showed an increase in seminal volume, sperm count, and sperm motility (Gonzales et al., 2001).  A more recent double-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled trial in healthy people used yellow maca and found an increase in sperm parameters (20% increase in sperm count, 14% increase in concentration, 14% increase in motility, 21% increase in morphology) (Melnikovova et al., 2015). Studies ranged in dose from 1.7-3 grams per day (Melnikovova et al., 2015). 

Caution: there are no reported contraindications for maca and it has been traditionally consumed safely as food in traditional South American cultures. However, some concerns of heavy metal contamination highlight the importance of quality sourcing (Meissner et al., 2006).  

Tribulus (Tribulus terrestris) fruit: Tribulus (also known as gokshura in Ayurveda) is considered a rejuvenating tonic for the reproductive system and is used for impotence, infertility, and libido (Khalsa & Tierra, 2008).  

Tribulus encourages the production of luteinizing hormone (LH). When LH levels are increased, the natural production of testosterone also increases. LH is a hormone that also influences libido. Tribulus has been used to increase fertility and help to address impotence (Sanagoo et al., 2019). 

Studies have shown that tribulus supplementation can improve sperm concentration (Sellandi et al., 2012) and motility (Salgado et al., 2017; Setiawan, 1996). One study showed a significant decrease in body fat percentage and increase in lean mass, as well as increase in testosterone levels after supplementing with tribulus (Salgado et al., 2017). A recent study of a small group of CrossFit athletes suggests that tribulus could potentially stimulate recovery by raising serum testosterone levels (Fernández-Lázaro et al., 2021). 

Typical dosage of tribulus is 3-9 grams per day.

Astragalus (Astragalus mongholicus) root: Astragalus is most commonly known for its immune-supportive properties. As an immunomodulator, astragalus balances the response of the immune system by either increasing or decreasing its activity as needed for a particular situation, supporting the body’s natural capacity to maintain wellness (Winston, 2019. 

Preliminary studies have shown astragalus improves sperm motility in vitro (Hong et al., 1992; Liu et al., 2004). It is high in antioxidants and contains vitamins and minerals such as folate, nicotinic acid, choline, betaine, iron, magnesium, silicon, and zinc (Sinadinos, 2020), which may contribute to its beneficial effect on sperm motility.

Typical dosage is 4-8 mL of tincture, three times per day, or 2-4 tsp dried root in 8 oz boiling water as a decoction, three times per day. It is also available in capsule form. 

Lifestyle

Simple lifestyle modifications can positively influence fertility. The factors outlined below have been linked to improved fertility or sperm parameters, and often contribute to an overall improvement in health. 

Reduce Exposure to Pesticides

Eating fruits and vegetables with high amounts of pesticide residue—such as strawberries, spinach, peppers, or grapes—may decrease a person’s chances of conceiving.

The term “organic” refers to the way agricultural products are grown and processed. While the regulations vary from country to country, in the U.S., organic crops must be grown without the use of synthetic pesticides, bioengineered genes (GMOs), petroleum-based fertilizers, and sewage sludge-based fertilizers (Code of Federal Regulations, 2021).

Eating fruits and vegetables with high levels of pesticide residues was associated with a lower total sperm count and a lower percentage of morphologically normal sperm among people undergoing fertility treatment (Chiu et al., 2015). In this study, subjects with the highest levels of pesticide intake had 49% lower total sperm count and 32% lower percentage of morphologically normal sperm than people with the lowest intake of pesticides. 

The good news is that just one week of eating mostly organic foods reduces urine pesticide levels by nearly 90% (Oates et al., 2014). 

Try to eat organic food whenever possible. For times when it’s not possible or affordable to obtain organic foods, check out the Environmental Working Group’s Dirty Dozen and Clean 15 lists to help you prioritize which conventionally grown foods have the highest levels of pesticides and should be avoided and which have lower pesticide levels.

Reduce Exposure to Bisphenol A

Bisphenol A (BPA) is a resin used in the lining of cans, water bottles, food package containers, thermal receipts, and more. BPA is an endocrine disruptor, meaning it binds to hormone receptors and interferes with the normal function of reproductive hormones like estrogen and testosterone (Vandenberg et al., 2009).  

A study on people exposed to high levels of BPA in the workplace found that detectable levels of BPA in urine were significantly associated with the decrease of sperm concentration, count, vitality, and motility (Li et al., 2011).

In a subsequent study on 149 couples undergoing in vitro fertilization (IVF) or intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI) procedure, an increased urinary BPA concentration was associated with lower sperm count, concentration, and vitality (Knez et al., 2014).  

Taking the time to replace plastic containers for glass, reduce the use of canned foods, swap coffee pod systems (the plastic coffee pods contain BPA) for a French press or drip-style non-plastic coffee maker, and avoid paper receipts and other thermal paper, when possible, can all work together to decrease the overall exposure to BPA.   

Reduce Exposure to Phthalates

One thing that cologne, body wash, air fresheners, scented deodorants, and takeout food containers all have in common is a group of chemicals called phthalates. Phthalates, like BPA, are known hormone disruptors (Kabir et al., 2015). They are used in plastics to make them soft and flexible and in artificial fragrances to make the scent last longer. 

Elevated phthalate levels have been shown to decrease sperm concentration (Bloom et al., 2015) and motility (Axelsson et al., 2015) and increase the time it took for a couple to conceive (Louis et al., 2014).

Avoiding artificial fragrances is one of the most effective ways to decrease phthalate exposure. One study tested the urine of pregnant people and found those who used perfume had phthalate concentrations 167% higher than non-users (Braun et al., 2014). 

Phthalates are rarely listed as an ingredient on labels, however, because the FDA does not require the listing of the individual components of fragrances. Any time you see the ingredient “fragrance” listed on a label, assume phthalates are present. 

Consider cutting out anything with artificial fragrance (cologne, air fresheners, scented body lotion, detergent, deodorant, aftershave) and soft plastics (shower curtains, plastic toys, disposable food storage) which are all high in phthalates. You can find more natural options that use essential oils or are fragrance free. 

Choosing unscented products does not mean that they will be phthalate free because these products often contain masking fragrances that may contain the chemical. Always read the label and look for the ingredient “fragrance.”

Limit Close Contact With Cell Phone and Wireless Laptop

Although often dismissed as a myth, there are studies that suggest keeping a cellphone or laptop close to the groin area could negatively affect sperm development and function. Cell phones and Wifi-enabled laptops both emit radio-frequency electromagnetic radiation (RF-EMR) which has been shown to damage sperm, but the question is: does “typical” use actually cause harm?

In vitro studies have shown that sperm exposed to radio-frequency electromagnetic radiation (RF-EMR) levels similar to those resulting from wearing a cell phone on the belt, in the pants pocket, or using a wireless laptop computer on the lap resulted in significantly lower sperm motility and viability while increasing oxidative damage (Agarwal et al., 2009). In a human study, when mobile phones were moved 50 centimeters or more away from the groin area there was less sperm damage (Zilberlicht et al., 2015).

The amount of time spent talking on a mobile phone was also found to impact sperm parameters. One hour of cell phone radiation had a significant negative impact on sperm motility, sperm acrosin activity, and sperm DNA fragmentation in an in vitro study (Zalata et al., 2015), while a human study found that talking less than 1 hour had less damage (Zilberlicht et al., 2015). And the use of a cell phone while charging was associated with a higher percentage of abnormal sperm concentration compared to when it was not charging (Zilberlicht et al., 2015).

Similar results were seen with Wifi-enabled laptops. Sperm samples exposed to a wireless internet-connected laptop for 4 hours showed a significant decrease in progressive sperm motility and an increase in sperm DNA fragmentation (Avendano et al., 2012). 

There is enough data to suggest taking a precautionary approach to cell phone and laptop use. A simple strategy to minimize negative effects is to remember that distance matters. Whether it’s coming from a Wifi laptop or a cellphone, RF-EMF drops off the further you get away from the antenna or source.

Conclusion

The goal of a natural approach to fertility is to enhance overall wellness in both partners while addressing specific problems if they exist. When couples who are trying to conceive work together to address as many factors as possible, their chances of conceiving can greatly increase. We know that sperm factors contribute to fertility struggles in approximately 50% of cases, and that sperm parameters are declining in general. The good news is that nutrition, herbs, supplements, and lifestyle changes have been shown to help and success rates improve when addressed by both partners.  

Because sperm takes about two months to go through the cycle of development, changes implemented today will affect the quality of the sperm that is released 2 months from now. That is why the preconception period is so important, and it is never too soon to start. These strategies can help support optimal sperm quality and a natural fertility plan.
