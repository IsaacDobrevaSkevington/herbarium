Session 2: Egg Quality, Hormone Balance, and Common Conditions Affecting Fertility

In this session, we will take a deeper look at the factors affecting egg quality and hormone balance. We will cover herbs and natural strategies to support fertility, and discuss herb safety when trying to conceive and in pregnancy.

Menstrual Cycle Overview

A typical menstrual cycle ranges between 26-35 days (Mihm et al., 2011). Twenty-eight days is the average, but not the rule. Starting with the first day of menstruation, a cycle is broken down into the following phases:

  1. Follicular phase (includes menstruation)
  2. Ovulation
  3. Luteal phase

The Follicular Phase (approximately cycle days 1-13 and includes the menstrual phase)

The follicular phase starts on the first day of menstruation, also called cycle day 1. During this phase the brain (hypothalamus and pituitary gland) communicates with the ovaries to develop and grow follicles, ultimately resulting in one dominant follicle (with an egg inside, ready to ovulate) (Messinis et al., 2014). The hypothalamus communicates with the pituitary gland by secreting gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) in pulses which instruct the anterior pituitary gland to release follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) (Arroyo et al., 2020). The developing follicles release estrogen, the dominant hormone in this phase, which helps thicken the uterine lining and stimulates the cervix to produce more cervical fluid (Arroyo et al., 2020).

Ovulation (approximately cycle days 14-16)

The estrogen rise that occurs as the dominant follicle develops triggers the hypothalamus to increase the pulse frequency of GnRH. This increased pulsation is what signals the pituitary gland to switch from secreting FSH to secreting luteinizing hormone (LH). LH quickly rises until it reaches the surge that is needed to initiate ovulation. Right before the LH surge, estrogen levels fall. This abrupt hormonal shift of high LH compared to estrogen causes the dominant follicle to rupture and an egg is released (ovulation) (Arroyo et al., 2020). The egg is available for fertilization for only about 24 hours, but because sperm can survive for 3-5 days, the most fertile period is from about 5 days before until 1-2 days after ovulation (Wilcox et al., 2000).

The Luteal Phase (approximately cycle days 15-35)

After ovulation, LH transforms the granulosa cells of the empty follicle into a new, temporary endocrine gland called the corpus luteum. The same cells that were making estrogen in the follicular phase are now making progesterone in the luteal phase. Progesterone will steadily climb and remain high throughout the rest of the luteal phase, while estrogen, FSH, and LH all remain relatively low (Midgley & Jaffe, 1968). Progesterone’s main function is to support a potential pregnancy by supporting the uterine lining.

If an egg is fertilized and an embryo implants into the uterine lining, the embryo forms a very early placenta. This early placenta releases the pregnancy hormone human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG), which signals the corpus luteum to continue secreting progesterone for the next several weeks until the placenta can take over the job of making progesterone. Progesterone prevents the endometrium from shedding (menstruation). The luteal phase needs to last at least 11-12 days, or there won’t be enough time for an embryo to implant before the uterine lining is shed (Oliver & Pillarisetty, 2020).

If pregnancy doesn’t occur, the corpus luteum will stop the secretion of progesterone within 14 days after ovulation, and it then degenerates into a scar within the ovary, known as the corpus albicans. The drop in progesterone stimulates the uterus to contract and shed its lining as menstruation begins (Oliver & Pillarisetty, 2020). 

The drop in progesterone signals the pituitary and hypothalamus glands to increase the production of GnRH, FSH, and LH. This restarts the menstrual cycle, and the follicular phase starts anew.

Optimal hormone balance and cycle regulation start with healthy communication between the brain and ovaries, more specifically referred to as the hypothalamic-pituitary-ovarian (HPO) axis. The sophisticated dance of hormones is what helps develop healthy follicles, trigger ovulation, and maintain the pregnancy in the early weeks if fertilization occurs.

Ovarian Health and Egg Quality Support

There are many factors that contribute to egg quality, including hormone balance, nutrition, and oxidative stress. We will discuss each of these factors in more detail and how to support each of them in this section. 

Egg Quality

It takes 3-4 months for an egg to develop, mature, and ovulate. During this time there are many factors that can contribute to overall egg quality. Egg quality naturally declines with age and is associated with a decrease in fertility (Chronopoulou et al., 2021), but declining egg quality can be seen in younger people as well (Messinis et al., 2014).  

Nutrients and Supplements

Good nutrition is the foundation for optimal egg quality and fertility. It takes an enormous amount of energy and nutrients to develop a healthy egg. As discussed in Session 1, the Mediterranean diet has been shown to benefit fertility, likely due in part to its high nutrient content. 

In addition to a healthy diet, supplements can help ensure adequate levels of nutrients that are needed for optimal egg quality. 

For example:

  • Folate improves egg quality and supports ovulation (Ebisch et al., 2007).
  • Vitamin B12 can improve embryo quality (Gaskins et al., 2015).
  • Vitamin B6 is associated with higher pregnancy rates and less risk of miscarriage (Ronnenberg et al., 2007).
  • Coenzyme Q10 (CoQ10) improves egg quality and pregnancy rates (Akarsu et al., 2017).
  • Vitamin D is associated with higher pregnancy rates and decreased miscarriage rates (see Session 1).

A high-quality prenatal supplement will have adequate levels of most vitamins, minerals, and antioxidants but you will likely need to supplement CoQ10 and vitamin D3 separately.

Oxidative Stress and Egg Quality

Oxidative damage can cause problems for egg quality. Antioxidants play a vital role in egg quality by protecting against oxidative stress (Shaum & Polotsky, 2013). Research has found that people with a higher total antioxidant level during in vitro fertilization (IVF) cycles had higher pregnancy rates (Ozatik et al., 2013) and that antioxidant supplementation was associated with a shorter time to pregnancy (Ruder et al., 2014).

Antioxidants can be found in colorful fruits and vegetables, especially those with purple, blue, red, orange, and yellow hues. Many herbs have antioxidant properties as well. 

Antioxidant Herbs to Support Egg Quality

Green tea (Camellia sinensis) leaf: In Session 1 we discussed how green tea is high in antioxidants (Carlsen et al., 2010). Green tea is also lower in caffeine than coffee (Chin et al., 2008), providing a healthy alternative for people trying to decrease daily caffeine consumption which may be beneficial when trying to conceive (Homan et al., 2007).

Ashwagandha (Withania somnifera) root: Ashwagandha, also discussed in Session 1, is an adaptogen with a history of use for reproductive health and thyroid function (Winston, 2019). Ashwagandha is also high in antioxidants (Ahmed et al., 2018). It is traditionally used in Ayurveda to help with infertility, amenorrhea, and low libido (Sinadinos, 2020). It improves blood flow to the reproductive organs which may also contribute to its effect as a reproductive tonic (Sinadinos, 2020).    

Turmeric (Curcuma longa) rhizome: As discussed in Session 1, turmeric is a potent antioxidant (Kocaadam & Şanlier, 2017) with free radical scavengers (Khalsa & Tierra, 2008) helping to protect the body from oxidative stress, thereby improving overall egg quality. 

Rooibos (Aspalathus linearis) leaf: Rooibos is a popular herbal tea originally from South Africa with a fruity, sweet taste that is naturally caffeine free and antioxidant rich (Gadow et al., 1997). It has been shown to significantly improve the balance between oxidants and antioxidants and lipid profile in adults (Marnewick et al., 2011). Rooibos tea is another great option to help boost antioxidants. I like to add a teaspoon of cacao (Theobroma cacao) nibs and a dash of vanilla (Vanilla planifolia) extract or 1 inch of vanilla pod to rooibos for a delicious tea that also satisfies my sweet tooth. Cacao is also high in antioxidants and some studies have shown that chocolate can decrease oxidative stress (Martins et al., 2020). 

Culinary herbs: Simply adding more herbs to the diet can have a big impact! Culinary herbs like cinnamon (Cinnamomum spp.), clove (Syzygium aromaticum), oregano (Origanum vulgare), thyme (Thymus vulgaris), mint (Mentha spp.), and lemon balm (Melissa officinalis) are all high in antioxidants (Paur et al., 2011).

Ensuring adequate nutrient and antioxidant levels is crucial for optimal egg health. Changes made today will benefit an egg that is ovulated 3-4 months from now. Supporting a healthy diet with a prenatal supplement, CoQ10, Vitamin D, and lots of antioxidant-rich herbs are all part of a holistic approach to fertility. 

Common Conditions Affecting Fertility

Hypothalamic Amenorrhea

Hypothalamic amenorrhea (HA) is diagnosed when a person who was previously menstruating has a lack of period for 6 months or more and all other medical causes have been ruled out (Sophie Gibson et al., 2020). This type of secondary amenorrhea occurs when there is a disconnect in the conversation between the hypothalamus and the ovaries (HPO axis). 

When the hypothalamus perceives that something is wrong and that it may not be safe to reproduce, it stops producing (or decreases the pulse frequency of) GnRH, which affects the entire cascade of reproductive hormones responsible for growing a healthy follicle, ovulating, and holding onto the pregnancy in the early weeks (Sophie Gibson et al., 2020). 

The three most common causes of hypothalamic amenorrhea are weight loss, stress, and excessive exercise (Meczekalski et al., 2014). Addressing the root cause is critical to restoring a healthy menstrual cycle (Ryterska et al., 2021). Herbs can be helpful and supportive, but the root cause needs to be addressed as well. 

Nourishment

The hypothalamus functions best when it senses there are enough calories and nutrients to keep up with the activity level of the individual. It is important to eat enough of all the macronutrients, including protein, fat, and carbohydrates. Undereating carbohydrates can impair hypothalamic signaling, even if caloric intake is adequate (Loucks & Thuma, 2003) and dietary fat restriction is associated with amenorrhea even in normal weight, nonathletic people (Loucks, 2003).

Exercise

Strenuous exercise also sends a signal to the brain that it isn’t safe to reproduce. Researchers used to think this was related to low body fat, but it turns out that the effects on reproduction are independent of body fat stores (Loucks, 2003).

Stress

Stress can have a significant impact on fertility and will be covered in more detail in Session 4 of this intensive. But within the context of HA, it is important to understand that stress increases the release of cortisol, and cortisol has a dampening effect on the secretion of GnRH (Prokai & Berga, 2016). 

HA is not a disorder but is the body’s normal response to perceiving an unsafe environment for reproduction. This means that fortunately, HA is reversible, and usually resolves after energy and nutritional balance is restored or the underlying stress resolves (Roberts et al., 2020). Herbs can also help support HA.

Herbs to Support HA

Vitex/chaste tree (Vitex agnus-castus) berry: Vitex was introduced in the first session of this intensive for its use in improving cycle regularity, PMS symptoms, and fertility. Now that we have deepened our understanding of the HPO axis we can see how vitex may help support HA. 

Vitex influences the pituitary gland to increase the production of LH (the hormone that surges and causes the ovaries to release an egg during ovulation) (Milewicz et al., 1993) and in doing so can help support ovulation and development of the corpus luteum. This helps support healthy progesterone levels during the luteal phase (Milewicz et al., 1993). 

Think of vitex as an herb to support the communication between the pituitary gland and the ovaries to help regulate ovulation and support healthy progesterone levels.

Caution: while vitex is considered safe to consume during the first trimester of pregnancy (Gardner & McGuffin, 2013) and is often used to help guard against a miscarriage due to low progesterone levels (Romm, 2010) by experienced practitioners, vitex is contraindicated with oral contraceptives and other hormone replacement therapies, including assisted reproductive technologies. Vitex typically needs to be taken for 3 months or more to see effects. It may aggravate depression in some people (Groves, 2016). 

Maca (Lepidium meyenii) root: Maca is a nutritive herb that has been used traditionally to increase libido, sexual function, and fertility (Winston, 2019). Maca is rich in essential amino acids, iodine, iron, and magnesium (Romm, 2010). Based on preliminary studies, maca appears to promote optimal functioning of the hypothalamus and the pituitary, thereby improving fertility (Sánchez et al., 2017). In addition, a pilot study has shown that maca increases secretion of FSH by the pituitary gland (Meissner et al., 2006). 

Both its high nutrient content and its effect on the hypothalamus and pituitary glands make maca a supportive ally for people with HA.

Caution: there are no reported contraindications for maca and it has been traditionally consumed safely as food in traditional South American cultures. However, caution should be used in pregnancy as there are no safety studies and some concerns of heavy metal contamination highlight the importance of quality sourcing (Meissner et al., 2006).

Tribulus (Tribulus terrestris) fruit: Tribulus was also discussed in the first session of this intensive for its use to help normalize and stimulate ovulation. Tribulus contains steroidal saponins that compete with endogenous estrogens for hypothalamic estrogen receptors. They have a weaker effect on hypothalamic estrogen receptors than endogenous estrogen, causing the body to respond as if estrogen levels are lower than they really are, stimulating the pituitary to increase FSH secretion, which in turn increases estrogen production (Brice-Ytsma & McDermott, 2020). 

In cases of HA, tribulus is often given on cycle days 5-14 to support an increase in estrogen in the follicular phase (Romm, 2010), which can help improve uterine lining and trigger the pituitary gland to release LH which triggers ovulation. 

Caution: do not use in pregnancy or in the luteal phase if pregnancy is possible (best to use on cycle days 5-14 for ovulation support) (Romm, 2010).

Adaptogens: consider adaptogens to help support the stress response in HA. Herbs like shatavari (Asparagus racemosus) root and ashwagandha (Withania somnifera) root are adaptogens that are used to help support fertility (Winston, 2019).

Shatavari in particular can help support the follicular phase. The steroidal saponins found in shatavari’s roots act as an estrogen modulator (Boonsom et al., 2012) in addition to its use as an adaptogen.

Luteal Phase Deficiency (LPD)

The average luteal phase length is 14 days, with a normal variation of 11-17 days. Generally, luteal phase deficiency is considered a luteal phase that is 10 days or less, but some sources use the parameters of less than 9-11 days (American Society for Reproductive Medicine, 2021).

Having a short luteal phase impacts fertility because it doesn’t allow enough time for embryo implantation before menstruation begins. 

Inadequate production of progesterone is typically associated with luteal phase deficiency. Inadequate progesterone production could mean that the corpus luteum isn’t producing enough progesterone or that it is not producing progesterone for a long enough period of time (or both). 

Initial thoughts for support of LPD may be to use herbs or pharmaceuticals that simply increase progesterone. But understanding the entire HPO axis helps us understand a healthy luteal phase depends on a healthy corpus luteum. A healthy corpus luteum forms from a healthy follicle after ovulation, and a healthy follicle develops from adequate estrogen levels. And the entire cycle starts with the healthy pulsation of GnRH from the hypothalamus. So working on the first half of the cycle can help strengthen the second half of the cycle (de Souza et al., 2003).

Herbs to support LPD

Vitex/chaste tree (Vitex agnus-castus) berry: Vitex, as previously discussed, helps support the communication between the pituitary gland and the ovaries to help regulate ovulation and support healthy progesterone levels. Vitex has been specifically studied and shown to benefit LPD by increasing cycle length, increasing progesterone levels (Milewicz et al., 1993), and increasing pregnancy rates when combined with green tea and nutritional supplements (Westphal et al., 2006).

Biphasic Formulas

While vitex helps support the communication between the pituitary gland and the ovaries throughout the entire cycle, the additional use of specific herbs in the follicular phase and different herbs in the luteal phase (biphasic formulas) can be helpful to reestablish a healthy cycle. 

When creating biphasic formulas, choose herbs in the follicular phase to help support estrogen and herbs in the luteal phase to support progesterone. For example:

  • Follicular Phase: estrogen-supporting herbs day 1 through ovulation (or day 14 if no ovulation). Herbs to consider: fennel (Foeniculum vulgare) seed, black cohosh (Actaea racemosa) root, dong quai (Angelica sinensis) root, shatavari (Asparagus racemosus) root, wild yam (Dioscorea villosa) rhizome, hops (Humulus lupulus) strobile, licorice (Glycyrrhiza glabra) root, maca (Lepidium meyenii) root.
  • Luteal Phase: progesterone- and uterine tone-supporting herbs from ovulation (or day 14 if no ovulation) until menstruation. Herbs to consider: maca (Lepidium meyenii) root, damiana (Turnera diffusa) leaf, cramp bark (Viburnum opulus) bark, black haw (Viburnum prunifolium) bark. 

If a person isn’t currently menstruating and therefore doesn’t have a “day 1” you can consider following the moon cycle to establish a rhythm. Start day 1 and the follicular phase herbs with the new moon, and day 14 (or ovulation) and the luteal phase herbs on the full moon.

Stress and LPD

We discussed how stress affects the HPO axis in hypothalamic amenorrhea. Stress and stress hormones (like cortisol) signal to the brain that it is not an ideal time to reproduce. In response, the hypothalamus decreases the frequency and/or the speed of GnRH pulsation which kicks off a domino effect that results in lower estrogen, decreased LH surge, weaker corpus luteum, and ultimately lower progesterone. Often LPD develops before a person progresses into hypothalamic amenorrhea, so managing stress can help catch HPO axis issues earlier. 

The same adaptogens that were discussed for HA can be beneficial to support LPD. The effects of stress on fertility will be discussed in more detail in session 4. 

Polycystic Ovarian Syndrome (PCOS)

Polycystic Ovarian Syndrome, or PCOS, is the most common hormonal condition in people who menstruate and is one of the most common causes of anovulatory infertility (Gambineri et al., 2002). People with PCOS often experience irregular or infrequent cycles, acne, hirsutism, male-pattern baldness, blood sugar imbalance, and weight gain. Because PCOS is a syndrome, symptoms vary depending on the person.

A holistic approach to supporting PCOS involves tailoring suggestions to the individual. Herbs are chosen as needed to help support androgen levels, ovulation, blood sugar balance, and stress. A more comprehensive review of PCOS is covered in the Herbal Academy Intensive Natural Approaches to PCOS: Finding Balance by Sara Rooney. 

Endometriosis

Endometriosis is the most common cause of chronic pelvic pain and affects 35%-50% of people with infertility (Bulletti et al., 2010). Endometriosis is defined as the presence and growth of endometrial tissue in locations outside of the uterus. The endometrial tissue responds to the cyclic hormonal changes, building up under the influence of estrogen in the follicular phase and then shedding during menstruation (Bulletti et al., 2010). This bleeding outside of the uterus leads to inflammation and pain. 

Endometriosis can affect many aspects of fertility, including hormone balance, egg quality, anatomical issues, and inflammation (Rogers et al., 2017). A holistic approach to endometriosis includes pain management, immunomodulation, inflammation support, hormone balance, diet, and decreased exposure to endocrine-disrupting chemicals. Endometriosis often takes time and a multifaceted approach to support. 

Herbs to support endometriosis

Pain: analgesic herbs such as cramp bark (Viburnum opulus) bark, black haw (Viburnum prunifolium) bark, dong quai (Angelica sinensis) root (only in follicular phase if actively trying to conceive), ginger (Zingiber officinale) rhizome, California poppy (Eschscholzia californica) aboveground parts, and black cohosh (Actaea racemosa) root may be supportive.

Immunomodulation: studies have suggested that an alteration in the immune system and upregulated inflammatory processes play a significant (maybe pathogenic) role in endometriosis (Agostinis et al., 2020). Herbs such as echinacea (Echinacea spp.) root, ashwagandha (Withania somnifera) root, astragalus (Astragalus mongholicus) root, calendula (Calendula officinalis) flower, reishi (Ganoderma lucidum) mushroom, and dong quai (Angelica sinensis) root (follicular phase only) can be used to help modulate the immune system (Ilhan et al., 2019). While endometriosis is not classified as an autoimmune disease, there are similarities between endometriosis and autoimmune diseases such as rheumatoid arthritis, Crohn’s disease, and psoriasis. The most central of these similarities is the elevated levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including tumor necrosis factor (TNF) (Nothnick, 2001). While caution should be taken when using echinacea with autoimmune diseases, echinacea is associated with a decrease in pro-inflammatory cytokines, including TNF (Aucoin et al., 2021), and in my experience is well tolerated in endometriosis. 

Anti-inflammatories: anti-inflammatory herbs to consider include: ginger (Zingiber officinale) rhizome, turmeric (Curcuma longa) rhizome, licorice (Glycyrrhiza glabra) root, calendula (Calendula officinalis) flower, St. John’s wort (Hypericum perforatum) aerial parts, and omega-3 fatty acid supplements.

Hormonal balance: hormone modulators such as vitex (Vitex agnus-castus) berry and white peony (Paeonia lactiflora) root may be supportive.

Vitamin D: vitamin D deficiency is associated with endometriosis (Qiu et al., 2020), so testing levels and supplementing to achieve optimal levels is important.

Limit exposure to endocrine-disrupting chemicals (EDCs): endometriosis is considered an estrogen-dependent disorder (Rumph et al., 2020) so decreasing exposure to exogenous estrogen (or EDCs) can help. Simple steps like choosing organic foods, using fewer plastic containers, avoiding cans with BPA lining, and cutting out artificial fragrances (phthalates) can have a big impact.

Herbs and Assisted Reproductive Technology

For people undergoing assisted reproductive technologies (ART0 such as intrauterine insemination (IUI) and in vitro fertilization (IVF), it is important to use herbs wisely so as not to interfere with these medicated cycles.

ART cycles use medications to facilitate ovulation, fertilization, or both. We have seen how herbs can help support the HPO axis and affect the hormonal balance of a natural cycle. For that reason, most herbs that have an effect on hormones are contraindicated during ART cycles. There are a few exceptions, but this should be managed carefully and with support from the team managing the ART cycle.

Herbs That Can Be Taken During an ART Cycle

In general, any herb that can have an effect on reproductive hormones or interact with medications should be stopped. That includes many of the herbs we have talked about so far, with the following exceptions:

Black cohosh (Actaea racemosa) root: for ART cycles that include the medication clomiphene citrate (Clomid), black cohosh may play a role in improving pregnancy rates (Shahin et al., 2008). Clomid is used to help increase ovulation, but a possible negative side effect is that Clomid can thin the uterine lining (because it blocks estrogen which is normally responsible for building a thick uterine lining). Black cohosh helps maintain a thick uterine lining (Shahin et al., 2009), which is necessary for an embryo to implant. Again, work with the team prescribing Clomed to make sure everyone is in agreement. 

Green tea (Camellia sinensis) leaf and rooibos (Aspalathus linearis) leaf are both safe herbs to enjoy for their health-promoting benefits and rich antioxidant content during ART cycles. 

Gentle nervine and nutritive tonic herbs can be used during ART cycles safely and can be very beneficial for supporting a person while undergoing what is often a stressful and uncertain time. Consider the following herbs based on the constitution of the person: nettle (Urtica dioica) leaf, dandelion (Taraxacum officinale) root, chamomile (Matricaria chamomilla) flower, peppermint (Mentha x piperita) leaf, passionflower (Passiflora incarnata) aerial parts, skullcap (Scutellaria lateriflora) aerial parts, and ginger (Zingiber officinale) rhizome.

Culinary doses of food-like herbs are safe and encouraged during ART cycles! They are loaded with antioxidants and other nutrients to support overall health and reproduction.

Liver support: It can be helpful in between ART cycles to provide some gentle liver support to recover from a medicated cycle if a person did not become pregnant. I like to suggest a decoction of dandelion (Taraxacum officinale) root, chicory (Cichorium intybus) root, and burdock (Arctium lappa) root daily at the start of menstruation and continuing for the rest of the month, or until the next medicated cycle begins. If constipation is an issue, add yellow dock (Rumex crispus) root to the decoction as a gentle laxative. Another supportive herb for this time is nettle (Urtica dioica) leaf, prepared as an overnight infusion to help replenish minerals and other nutrients. 

Herbal Safety When Trying to Conceive and During Pregnancy

Below are some general safety guidelines, but this is not a comprehensive list of all herbs to avoid when trying to conceive or when pregnant. Always do your own research on herbs you are suggesting or using. The timing of herbs within a cycle should also be taken into consideration. For example, it is best to use herbs in the luteal phase that are considered safe if the person were to become pregnant in that cycle. 

Any herb with emmenagogue action should be avoided once ovulation occurs and throughout pregnancy. Common herbs with possible emmenagogue action include angelica (Angelica archangelica), blue cohosh (Caulophyllum thalictroides), goldenseal (Hydrastis canadensis), motherwort (Leonurus cardiaca), mugwort (Artemisia vulgaris), pennyroyal (Mentha pulegium), rue (Ruta graveolens), wormwood (Artemisia absinthium), and yarrow (Achillea millefolium) (Gardner & McGuffin, 2013).

Teratogens (substances that cause structural abnormalities in the fetus) should be completely avoided when trying to conceive and pregnant. Most herbs classified as teratogens are not commonly used but can include: cherry bark (Prunus spp.), lupine (Lupinus spp.), and hellebore (Veratrum spp.) (Gardner & McGuffin, 2013). While not classified as a teratogen, Asian ginseng (Panax ginseng) is not recommended during pregnancy due to in vitro evidence of possible embryotoxic and teratogenic effects at high doses (Chan et al., 2004). 

The internal use of pyrrolizidine alkaloids (PAs) should be avoided in pregnancy. PAs have been shown to cause veno-occlusive disease (VOD) in developing babies when ingested during pregnancy (Avila et al., 2020). VOD is a rare but potentially life-threatening liver problem. Topical use of herbs that contain PAs should be used short term and in small amounts only if necessary. Examples of herbs that contain PAs are borage (Borago officinalis), butterbur (Petasites hybridus), coltsfoot (Tussilago farfara), and comfrey (Symphytum officinale).

Herbs in pregnancy: Once pregnant, it’s best to use herbs with caution and only when indicated, especially in the first trimester. Stick to herbs that have been shown to be safe in studies and have a long history of traditional use in pregnancy. Start with lower doses and build up if necessary. Below is a list of herbs generally considered safe during pregnancy based on the American Herbal Products Association’s Botanical Safety Handbook (Gardner & McGuffin, 2013):

  • Alfalfa (Medicago sativa) leaf
  • Astragalus (Astragalus mongholicus) root
  • Black haw (Viburnum prunifolium) bark
  • California poppy (Eschscholzia californica) aboveground parts
  • Chamomile (Matricaria chamomilla) flower
  • Cramp bark (Viburnum opulus) bark
  • Dandelion (Taraxacum officinale) root and leaf 
  • Echinacea (Echinacea angustifolia) root
  • Eleuthero (Eleutherococcus senticosus) root
  • Hawthorn (Crataegus spp.) berry and aerial parts
  • Lavender (Lavandula spp.) flower bud
  • Lemon balm (Melissa officinalis) aerial parts
  • Marshmallow (Althaea officinalis) root or leaf 
  • Nettle (Urtica dioica) leaf
  • Oat (Avena sativa) seed and straw
  • Passionflower (Passiflora incarnata) aerial parts
  • Peppermint (Mentha × piperita) leaf
  • Plantain (Plantago spp.) leaf
  • Raspberry (Rubus idaeus) leaf – in the 2nd and 3rd trimester
  • Reishi (Ganoderma lucidum) mushroom
  • Skullcap (Scutellaria lateriflora) aerial parts
  • Spearmint (Mentha spicata) leaf
  • St. John’s wort (Hypericum perforatum) aerial parts
  • Valerian (Valeriana officinalis) root
  • Wild yam (Dioscorea villosa) rhizome
  • Yellow dock (Rumex crispus) root

As always and especially in pregnancy, it is important to consult an experienced herbal practitioner and work in concert with your medical team before integrating herbs into your regimen. 

Conclusion

In this Session, we covered the menstrual cycle, how the HPO axis can affect fertility and egg quality, and common conditions affecting fertility. We also discussed herb safety during ART cycles, when trying to conceive, and in pregnancy.
